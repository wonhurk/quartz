**Aspartate aminotransferase (AST)** is a critical serum enzyme biomarker that reflects hepatocellular injury and liver disease severity, with serum levels directly correlating to the extent of hepatic damage and pathological progression. The enzyme serves as an accessible, validated marker for disease stratification and treatment monitoring across multiple liver pathologies, including [[hepatitis-b|chronic hepatitis B]] and [[mash|metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH)]], where elevation indicates disrupted hepatic metabolism and dysbiotic perturbations of the gut microbiota.¹²

---

## Clinical significance as a hepatocellular injury marker

Serum AST concentration provides a direct indicator of hepatocellular damage and metabolic disruption. When hepatocytes sustain injury or undergo pathological remodeling, AST is released into the circulation, making it a sensitive and accessible marker of liver dysfunction.¹ AST elevation occurs across multiple etiologies — from viral infection to metabolic derangement — and its magnitude correlates directly with disease progression and response to therapy. Combined with [[alt|alanine aminotransferase (ALT)]], AST enables clinicians to characterize patterns of liver injury, track longitudinal changes in hepatic integrity, and stratify patients by disease severity.¹²

---

## Association with hepatitis B viral mutations

In [[hepatitis-b|chronic hepatitis B infection]], specific viral mutations drive pronounced elevations in serum AST that directly reflect disease severity and host metabolic dysregulation. Patients carrying the A1762T/G1764A mutations or the dual mutant genotype (A1762T/G1764A + G1896A) exhibit significantly elevated AST levels compared to those infected with wild-type virus.¹ These mutations dysregulate host metabolism, particularly [[cholesterol-metabolism|cholesterol metabolism]], triggering widespread proteomics and metabolomics perturbations throughout the hepatocellular compartment. Integrated multi-omics profiling identified AST as a key component of biomarker panels capable of distinguishing between HBV viral genotypes and predicting host molecular response patterns linked to clinical outcome.¹ The correlation between AST elevation and specific viral mutations suggests that serum AST levels can serve as a non-invasive surrogate for identifying high-risk HBV variants and monitoring disease trajectory.

---

## Metabolic liver disease and gut dysbiosis

Beyond viral hepatitis, AST serves as a critical indicator of liver injury in [[mash|metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH)]], where its serum elevation correlates with dysbiotic shifts in gut microbial composition and the pathogenic potential of specific bacterial species. In MASH pathogenesis, the enrichment of candidate pathobionts — particularly increased abundance of Turicibacter sanguinis — directly correlates with markers of hepatocellular injury, including elevated AST.² The microbial dysbiosis associated with heightened AST levels exacerbates hepatic steatosis, inflammation, and fibrosis through disruption of bile acid metabolism and suppression of [[fxr|farnesoid X receptor (FXR)]] signaling.² This reciprocal relationship between serum AST, dysbiotic microbial composition, and hepatic pathology underscores the enzyme's value as a biomarker reflecting both direct hepatocellular damage and systemic dysmetabolism linked to disrupted gut-liver axis signaling.
