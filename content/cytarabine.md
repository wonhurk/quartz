**Cytarabine** is a nucleoside antimetabolite chemotherapy agent and the foundational component of induction therapy for [[acute-myeloid-leukemia|acute myeloid leukemia (AML)]]. It is classically administered as part of the 7+3 regimen—seven days of cytarabine combined with three days of [[anthracycline|anthracycline]]—which has served as the standard of care and clinical comparator for decades.¹ The clinical efficacy of cytarabine-based therapy varies substantially by leukemic molecular subtype, with newer liposomal formulations such as CPX-351 (liposomal cytarabine-daunorubicin) demonstrating selective survival benefit in molecularly defined AML subgroups while conferring no advantage—or even harm—in others.¹ Chemoresistance to cytarabine emerges in specific AML contexts, particularly [[minimally-differentiated-aml|minimally differentiated AML (AML-M0)]], where [[runx1|RUNX1]] disruption and associated alterations drive stem cell-like transcriptional features that suppress drug sensitivity.²

---

## Mechanism

Cytarabine exerts its cytotoxic effect as a nucleoside analog that interferes with DNA synthesis. Upon cellular uptake and phosphorylation, the drug is incorporated into DNA during the S phase of the cell cycle, blocking chain elongation and triggering apoptotic death pathways. Its activity is particularly pronounced in rapidly dividing cells characteristic of acute leukemias. The drug's efficacy in AML depends on the inherent proliferative capacity and differentiation state of the leukemic clone, with differentiated and proliferative subtypes showing greater sensitivity than quiescent stem cell-like populations.

---

## Standard 7+3 regimen and CPX-351

The 7+3 induction regimen—cytarabine at standard doses combined with an [[anthracycline]]—has remained the cornerstone of AML treatment for four decades, achieving complete remission rates of 60–80% in younger patients. The development of CPX-351, a liposomal formulation that coencapsulates cytarabine and daunorubicin in a fixed 5:1 molar ratio, was designed to improve drug delivery, reduce toxicity, and enhance efficacy. However, the clinical benefit of CPX-351 over standard 7+3 is not universal; rather, it is sharply stratified by the underlying molecular biology of the AML.¹

---

## Molecular stratification and selective benefit

DNA sequencing of the pivotal CPX-351 phase 3 randomized trial revealed dramatic differences in overall survival benefit across four molecularly defined AML subgroups.¹ Patients with TP53-mutant AML experienced two-year overall survival of only 7% regardless of treatment arm, and CPX-351 conferred no survival advantage over standard 7+3. In contrast, patients with myelodysplasia-related AML (AML-MR)—characterized by WHO-defined mutations in genes including ASXL1, BCOR, EZH2, SF3B1, STAG2, and U2AF1—achieved median overall survival of 9.7 months with CPX-351 versus 6.8 months with 7+3, representing a significant and clinically meaningful improvement.¹ The "other-AML" category (lacking TP53 mutations or myelodysplasia-related features) showed two-year overall survival of 37%, with no significant CPX-351 benefit. Notably, patients with DDX41-mutant AML exhibited exceptional outcomes, with two-year overall survival of 70% regardless of treatment.¹ These findings underscore the importance of molecular diagnosis in guiding cytarabine-based induction strategy selection. Among patients undergoing subsequent hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, CPX-351 further improved two-year survival in the AML-MR subgroup (76% versus 27%), suggesting that optimized induction therapy enhances transplant candidacy and post-transplant outcomes.¹

## Chemoresistance in minimally differentiated AML

[[Minimally-differentiated-aml|Minimally differentiated AML (AML-M0)]] presents a unique clinical challenge characterized by profound chemoresistance to cytarabine and other standard agents. Comprehensive multi-omics analysis of pediatric AML-M0 cases revealed that this subtype exhibits global DNA hypermethylation and widespread transcriptional suppression, particularly affecting genes involved in oxidative phosphorylation and ribosome biogenesis compared to non-M0 AML.² Genomic profiling identified loss-of-function alterations in [[runx1|RUNX1]] in 26% of AML-M0 cases, a frequency substantially higher than in other AML subtypes.² RUNX1 disruption acts as a critical driver of the chemoresistance phenotype; functional studies using CRISPR-mediated [[runx1|RUNX1]] knockout demonstrated that loss of RUNX1 function induces stem cell-like transcriptional features and reduces expression of genes required for oxidative phosphorylation and ribosomal assembly.² Notably, [[runx1|RUNX1]] alterations are associated with significantly worse prognosis, and cells bearing [[runx1|RUNX1]] mutations exhibit markedly reduced in vitro sensitivity to multiple agents including cytarabine and anthracyclines.² This molecular mechanism suggests that conventional chemotherapy intensification alone may be insufficient for AML-M0 patients harboring [[runx1|RUNX1]] alterations, and alternative or adjunctive approaches—such as hypomethylating agents or targeted signaling inhibitors—warrant investigation.

---

## Pediatric applications

Cytarabine remains a fundamental component of AML induction therapy across all age groups, including pediatric populations. However, pediatric minimally differentiated AML, while rare, represents a treatment-refractory subtype where [[runx1|RUNX1]] and other genetic alterations (ETV6 loss, RAS/FLT3/JAK pathway activation) drive reduced drug sensitivity.² The identification of [[runx1|RUNX1]] alterations as an independent predictor of poor prognosis in pediatric AML-M0 highlights the potential utility of molecular stratification for precision therapy design. Hypomethylating agents and targeted inhibitors of dysregulated signaling pathways may offer complementary or alternative strategies in this molecularly defined high-risk population.
