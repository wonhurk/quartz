**Oral cavity cancer** is a malignant disease of the oral mucosa characterized not only by tumor growth but also by profound psychological, functional, and social complications that remain significantly under-recognized in clinical practice. Patients experience visible disfigurement, impairments in speech (dysarthria) and swallowing (dysphagia), and associated social stigma, which collectively precipitate heightened [[anxiety]], [[depression]], and social withdrawal that adversely affect quality of life and treatment adherence.¹ The disease is frequently preceded by chronic inflammatory precancerous conditions, particularly [[oral-lichen-planus|oral lichen planus (OLP)]] of the tongue, which exhibits layer-specific molecular heterogeneity and is associated with elevated malignant transformation risk.² Effective clinical management demands a multidisciplinary approach addressing both oncological and psycho-oncological dimensions of patient care.

---

## Clinical burden and manifestations

Oral cavity cancer imposes a distinctive disease burden distinguished by its anatomical visibility and functional consequences. The oral cavity is an exposed anatomical site; tumors and their treatment effects are immediately apparent to the patient and to observers, creating visible disfigurement that carries profound psychosocial weight distinct from many internal malignancies. Concurrent functional impairments — difficulty speaking due to dysarthria and difficulty swallowing due to dysphagia — compound the psychological impact by compromising fundamental communicative and nutritive functions essential to social engagement and daily living.¹ The convergence of visible disfigurement, functional loss, and associated social stigma creates a uniquely stressful oncological context. Patients with oral cavity cancer are particularly susceptible to heightened [[psychological-distress|psychological distress]], with prominent manifestations of anxiety, depression, and social withdrawal, as they confront altered self-image, potential social rejection, and disability affecting basic activities of daily life.

## Psychological distress and psychiatric management

[[Psychological-distress|Psychological distress]] emerges as a common yet clinically under-recognized complication in oral cavity cancer patients, with documented adverse effects on quality of life, treatment adherence, and clinical outcomes.¹ The psychosocial burden is multifactorial: beyond the existential threat of cancer diagnosis itself, patients face psychological sequelae of visible facial and oral disfigurement, functional impairments in communication and swallowing, and internalized social stigma rooted in disease-related appearance changes and disability. Two psychotropic agents — olanzapine and mirtazapine — have been evaluated for efficacy and safety in managing cancer-related psychological distress in patients with advanced oral cavity cancer.¹ Both agents possess anxiolytic and antidepressant properties addressing prominent anxiety, depressive symptoms, and associated distress characterizing this patient population. Integrated psycho-oncological care, incorporating pharmacological intervention and structured psychotherapy, remains essential for comprehensive symptom management and optimization of patient outcomes and treatment compliance.

## Precancerous conditions and malignant progression

[[Oral-lichen-planus|Oral lichen planus (OLP)]] is a chronic immune-mediated inflammatory disease affecting the oral mucosa with particular clinical significance when localized to the tongue. Tongue OLP carries elevated risk of malignant transformation to oral cavity cancer and is frequently accompanied by taste dysfunction.² The molecular landscape of tongue OLP reveals substantial layer-specific heterogeneity across the basal epithelial layer, spinous epithelial layer, and lamina propria, each compartment exhibiting distinct proteomic signatures and inflammatory states.² Keratin 17 functions as a central molecular orchestrator of inflammatory crosstalk between epithelial and stromal layers, amplifying chronic inflammation and contributing to the precancerous phenotype.² This layer-specific molecular complexity indicates that mechanistic understanding of OLP-to-cancer progression requires compartment-specific investigation rather than treating the lesion as a uniform biological entity. The keratin 17-mediated inflammatory axis may represent a therapeutic target for preventing malignant transformation in high-risk OLP patients.
