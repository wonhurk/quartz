**FOLFOX** is a combination chemotherapy regimen comprising 5-fluorouracil (5-FU), leucovorin (folinic acid), and oxaliplatin, widely established as first-line treatment for [[colorectal-cancer|colorectal cancer]] and administered via hepatic arterial infusion (FOLFOX-HAIC) for unresectable [[hepatocellular-carcinoma|hepatocellular carcinoma]]. The regimen operates through dual mechanisms: 5-FU inhibits nucleotide synthesis by targeting thymidylate synthase, while oxaliplatin induces platinum-DNA crosslinks. A primary mechanism of chemoresistance involves dysregulation of sphingolipid biosynthesis, orchestrated by the RNA modification enzyme [[dkc1|DKC1]], which forms a positive feedback loop with [[wnt|canonical WNT signaling]] to drive therapy evasion and cancer stemness.¹

---

## Mechanism

FOLFOX is a three-drug combination designed to disrupt DNA synthesis and repair. 5-Fluorouracil functions as a thymidylate synthase inhibitor, depleting cellular nucleotide pools and preventing DNA replication. Leucovorin potentiates 5-FU activity by increasing its polyglutamation, enhancing cellular retention and sustained target inhibition. Oxaliplatin, a third-generation platinum compound, forms covalent DNA-platinum adducts that block replication fork progression and transcription, triggering intrinsic apoptotic pathways in rapidly cycling tumor cells. The sequential or concurrent administration of these agents achieves synergistic cytotoxicity by targeting complementary vulnerabilities in cancer cell survival and proliferation.

## Colorectal cancer treatment

FOLFOX has emerged as the standard first-line chemotherapy regimen for metastatic and locally advanced [[colorectal-cancer|colorectal cancer]], offering superior progression-free and overall survival compared to 5-FU monotherapy or fluoropyrimidine combinations lacking oxaliplatin. The regimen forms the backbone of most modern colorectal cancer treatment protocols, frequently combined with targeted agents such as bevacizumab (anti-VEGF) or cetuximab (anti-EGFR) depending on tumor RAS/BRAF mutational status. However, acquired chemoresistance represents a major clinical limitation, preventing sustained therapeutic benefit in a substantial proportion of patients and necessitating novel resistance-circumvention strategies.

## Hepatocellular carcinoma applications

FOLFOX delivered via hepatic arterial infusion (FOLFOX-HAIC) has demonstrated remarkable efficacy in unresectable [[hepatocellular-carcinoma|hepatocellular carcinoma]] when combined with targeted therapy and immunotherapy. The HILL phase 2 trial evaluated the triple-combination of FOLFOX-HAIC, [[lenvatinib]] (a multikinase inhibitor targeting VEGFR and FGF signaling pathways), and [[durvalumab]] (an anti-[[pd-l1|PD-L1]] immune checkpoint inhibitor) as initial therapy in 40 uHCC patients with median follow-up of 23.1 months. The regimen achieved a 75% objective response rate (9 complete or partial responses, 21 partial responses), 95% disease control rate, median progression-free survival of 15.8 months, and 1- and 2-year overall survival rates of 97.5% and 94.0%, respectively. Notably, 17.5% of patients underwent curative R0 conversion surgery, with 42.9% of those achieving pathological complete response. Safety was favorable, with 85.0% of patients experiencing only grade 1–2 adverse events and no grade 4 toxicities recorded. This integrated approach represents a paradigm shift in uHCC treatment, combining hepatic arterial chemotherapy, multikinase inhibition, and immune checkpoint blockade in a single therapeutic platform.²

## Resistance mechanisms

FOLFOX chemoresistance in [[colorectal-cancer|colorectal cancer]] is driven by complex dysregulation of sphingolipid metabolism orchestrated by [[dkc1|DKC1]], a nucleolar protein essential for pseudouridine synthesis and telomerase biogenesis. Elevated DKC1 expression associates with poor patient prognosis and drives three distinct oncogenic programs: accelerated cell cycle progression, suppression of apoptosis, and acquisition of cancer stemness and drug-resistant phenotypes.¹ Mechanistically, canonical [[wnt|WNT signaling]] establishes a positive feedback loop with DKC1, wherein WNT pathway activation induces DKC1 expression, and elevated DKC1 further amplifies WNT target gene transcription, creating a reinforcing axis of therapy resistance.

The DKC1-mediated resistance phenotype operates through dysregulation of sphingolipid biosynthesis. Integrated transcriptomic and lipidomic analyses reveal abnormal accumulation of very-long-chain fatty acid ceramides and disrupted metabolic flux through sphingolipid synthesis and degradation pathways. A critical regulatory hub involves the DKC1-[[sox2|SOX2]] transcriptional axis, which drives aberrant expression of SGPP2, a sphingolipid phosphatase central to lipid remodeling. This dysregulated sphingolipid metabolism reprograms FOLFOX-resistant cancer cells to resist drug-induced apoptosis while maintaining stemness, self-renewal capacity, and tumor-initiating capability.

---

## Overcoming chemoresistance

FOLFOX-resistant patient-derived [[colorectal-cancer|colorectal cancer]] organoids effectively respond to combined inhibition of [[dkc1|DKC1]] and [[wnt|WNT signaling]], providing a rational therapeutic strategy to overcome chemoresistance and restore drug responsiveness. Targeting DKC1 or WNT signaling individually achieves partial resensitization, but dual inhibition of both the DKC1 node and canonical WNT signaling achieves superior efficacy, suggesting that both arms of the DKC1/WNT feedback axis must be simultaneously disabled to fully restore FOLFOX responsiveness and overcome stemness-driven resistance.¹ These findings identify [[dkc1|DKC1]] as a critical therapeutic target in therapy-resistant [[colorectal-cancer|colorectal cancer]] and suggest that sphingolipid metabolites may serve as plasma-based biomarkers for predicting chemoresistance and monitoring therapeutic efficacy in clinical practice.
