**Anxiety** is a prevalent mental health comorbidity in patients with chronic diseases including [[atopic-dermatitis]], [[oral-cavity-cancer]], and [[endometriosis]], characterized by heightened psychological distress that significantly impairs quality of life, disrupts sleep, and compromises treatment adherence.¹ The condition is mechanistically intertwined with disease-specific stressors such as visible disfigurement, functional impairments, and social stigma, which collectively heighten anxiety manifestation in affected populations.² Importantly, anxiety symptoms respond to both targeted treatment of underlying chronic diseases and direct pharmacological intervention, suggesting dual pathways for clinical management.

---

## Dermatitis comorbidity

[[Atopic dermatitis]], a chronic immune-mediated inflammatory skin condition, is commonly accompanied by significant anxiety symptoms that degrade patient-reported quality of life and sleep architecture.¹ The visible nature of eczematous lesions, combined with intense pruritus and skin pain, creates substantial psychological burden beyond the physical disease manifestations. Treatment of the underlying atopic dermatitis with [[upadacitinib]], a selective Janus kinase inhibitor, demonstrates that anxiety symptoms improve substantially over extended therapy. In the Measure Up 1 and 2 clinical trials, long-term upadacitinib treatment over 140 weeks produced deep and sustained improvements in patient-reported anxiety outcomes alongside dermatologic improvement, indicating that biological disease control directly ameliorates psychological comorbidity.¹

---

## Cancer-related anxiety

Patients with [[oral-cavity-cancer]] experience heightened anxiety due to multifaceted disease-specific stressors including visible disfigurement of the face and neck, speech and swallowing impairments, and associated social stigma.² These factors collectively contribute to profound psychological distress that extends beyond anxiety to encompass depression and social withdrawal, significantly affecting quality of life, treatment adherence, and ultimately clinical outcomes.² The anxiety in this population is mechanistically linked to disease visibility and functional loss, distinguishing it from anxiety in non-disfiguring malignancies and underscoring the importance of integrated psychological management in cancer care.

---

## Pharmacological management

Targeted pharmacological interventions effectively address cancer-related anxiety and associated psychological distress. [[Olanzapine]] and [[mirtazapine]] — an atypical antipsychotic and tetracyclic antidepressant, respectively — have demonstrated efficacy in managing anxiety-related psychological distress in patients with advanced oral cavity cancer.² Both agents address multiple dimensions of psychological distress beyond anxiety alone, providing clinical utility when anxiety is embedded within broader cancer-related emotional burden. The safety and tolerability profiles of these agents permit their integration into comprehensive cancer care protocols, particularly in advanced disease where psychological support is essential for maintaining treatment adherence and quality of life.

---

## Genetic interaction with endometriosis

Endometriosis, a chronic systemic disease affecting approximately 10% of women, shows significant polygenic architecture with complex phenotypic manifestations. Multi-ancestry genome-wide association studies have identified 80 genomic regions associated with endometriosis risk, and notably, anxiety manifests as one of several measurable clinical outcomes that interact with endometriosis polygenic risk.³ Beyond anxiety, the endometriosis polygenic risk score interacts with additional symptom-specific manifestations including abdominal pain, migraine, and nausea, suggesting that shared genetic architecture modulates a spectrum of symptom domains rather than disease risk alone.³ This pattern indicates that anxiety in endometriosis reflects both direct genetic predisposition and secondary psychological response to chronic pain and functional impairment.

---

## Integrated disease management

Recognition of anxiety as a clinically significant comorbidity across multiple chronic diseases — dermatitis, cancer, and endometriosis — highlights the necessity for integrated management approaches that simultaneously address both disease pathology and psychological well-being. The observation that anxiety improves with effective disease-modifying therapy, as demonstrated by [[upadacitinib]] in atopic dermatitis, suggests that biological disease control represents one lever for anxiety reduction.¹ Conversely, direct pharmacological management of anxiety with agents such as [[mirtazapine]] and [[olanzapine]] provides complementary psychological support, particularly when disease-specific therapies achieve suboptimal psychological outcomes.² Comprehensive chronic disease care that systematically screens for and addresses anxiety as part of the treatment protocol optimizes patient outcomes across multiple quality-of-life domains and improves adherence to disease-modifying therapies.
