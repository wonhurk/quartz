**BRAF** is a proto-oncogene serine/threonine kinase and critical regulator within the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling pathway, governing cellular proliferation and differentiation.¹ [[braf|BRAF]] V600 mutations—predominantly V600E and V600K—constitute tumor-agnostic oncogenic drivers across multiple solid malignancies, particularly melanoma, and represent the most common pathogenic BRAF alterations.¹ Beyond canonical V600 mutations, rare non-V600E/K variants account for approximately 1.8% of melanoma cases, exhibiting variable functional impacts that range from partial kinase activation to kinase-dead phenotypes.² Acquired resistance to BRAF-targeted therapy frequently emerges through kinase-dead BRAF mutations that restore [[ras]]-RAF signaling or by disrupting molecular glue complexes designed to target downstream RAS effectors.³

---

## Mechanism

BRAF functions as a Ras-effector kinase that phosphorylates MEK1/2, propagating growth signals through the MAPK cascade. V600 mutations, which account for the vast majority of pathogenic BRAF alterations, destabilize the kinase domain and induce constitutive activation independent of upstream growth factor signaling. This mechanism drives uncontrolled cellular proliferation and is particularly prevalent in melanoma, where the combination of chronic UV exposure and BRAF mutational acquisition creates a permissive oncogenic state. Molecular dynamics simulations reveal that rare BRAF mutations outside codon 600—particularly at codons 599–601—display variable degrees of structural destabilization and constitutive protein activation, suggesting that kinase activity modulation occurs across a spectrum of BRAF variants rather than as an all-or-nothing switch.²

---

## Targeted therapy in melanoma

BRAF/MEK inhibitor therapy has transformed the treatment landscape for V600-mutant melanomas, yielding objective response rates around 66% with durable progression-free survival comparable to immunotherapy in select populations.² While rare BRAF-mutant melanomas show slightly lower response rates to targeted kinase inhibition (approximately 48% versus 66% in V600E/K cases), overall survival and progression-free survival outcomes remain statistically comparable.² This finding suggests that despite biochemical heterogeneity, rare BRAF mutations preserve sufficient kinase activity to remain therapeutically actionable, making molecular screening for non-canonical BRAF variants clinically warranted.

---

## Immunotherapy and BRAF mutation status

Immunotherapy response in melanoma is independent of BRAF mutation status, with no significant differences in progression-free survival, overall survival, or objective response rates between V600-mutant and rare BRAF-mutant tumors.² This tumor-agnostic response pattern implies that BRAF mutations primarily shape immune cell infiltration and checkpoint ligand expression through indirect mechanisms rather than direct modulation of immune recognition. Consequently, treatment selection in BRAF-mutant melanoma can be guided by tumor burden, comorbidities, and prior therapy history rather than by BRAF genotype alone.

---

## Resistance mechanisms

Resistance to BRAF-targeted therapy emerges through multiple convergent pathways. Acquired kinase-dead or hypoactive BRAF mutations confer resistance by paradoxically enhancing [[ras]]-RAF interaction, thereby restoring native RAS-driven signaling and bypassing the need for BRAF kinase activity.³ Additionally, downstream resistance mechanisms targeting the molecular glue complexes that sequester active RAS—such as those designed to inhibit [[cyca|cyclophilin A]]-mediated RAS binding—can be defeated by acquired RAS mutations (including Y64 and Y71 variants) that disrupt the synthetic trimolecular complex.³ These convergent resistance mechanisms underscore the necessity for rational combination strategies and sequential therapeutic approaches to maintain durable disease control.

---

## Novel BRAF inhibitors

[[mosperafenib]] represents a paradigm shift in BRAF inhibitor design as a paradox breaker with demonstrated brain penetrance, addressing a critical clinical limitation of conventional BRAF inhibitors that achieve suboptimal CNS exposure.¹ Paradox breakers retain activity against V600-mutant tumors while simultaneously overcoming resistance mechanisms mediated by wild-type RAS activation, a limitation of earlier-generation inhibitors that paradoxically activated MAPK signaling in RAS-wild-type or RAS-mutant contexts.¹ These next-generation agents expand therapeutic options for patients with chemotherapy-resistant disease and offer enhanced potential for addressing brain metastases in BRAF V600-mutant melanomas.
