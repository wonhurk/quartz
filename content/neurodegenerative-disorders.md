**Neurodegenerative disorders** are a class of age-related diseases characterized by progressive neuronal dysfunction and death driven by multiple interconnected molecular pathways. These conditions share common mechanistic features including accumulation of [[dna-damage|DNA damage]] in postmitotic neurons with impaired repair capacity, dysregulated neurotransmitter metabolism via [[mao-b|monoamine oxidase B]], oxidative stress reflected in [[apolipoprotein-e|apolipoprotein E]] redox status, mitochondrial dysfunction, and declining glymphatic clearance of neural metabolic waste.¹⁻⁶ Biological aging — influenced by ancestry, geography, genetic variants, and redox balance — acts as a major risk driver for these disorders.²

---

## Molecular mechanisms

The pathogenesis of neurodegenerative disorders reflects convergent failure of multiple cellular systems. [[dna-damage|DNA damage]] accumulates progressively in neurons with chronological age and accelerates during disease, driven in part by age-dependent relocalization of repair machinery.¹ The Y-family translesion synthesis DNA polymerase [[polk|POLK]] is highly expressed in neurons and localizes to sites of DNA damage under physiological conditions, but its nuclear abundance decreases progressively with aging while accumulating in the cytoplasm alongside stress granules and endo/lysosomal markers.¹ This nuclear-to-cytoplasmic redistribution correlates with increased DNA damage markers in old brain tissue and may represent a tissue-age biomarker. GABAergic interneurons exhibit significantly higher nuclear POLK expression compared to excitatory pyramidal neurons, suggesting cell-type-specific vulnerabilities in DNA repair capacity.¹

Mitochondrial dysfunction represents another central mechanism linking metabolic syndrome, aging, and neurodegeneration. Impaired mitochondrial function disrupts cellular energy homeostasis and redox balance, contributing to the cascade of neuronal damage.⁶ Oxidative stress compromises the antioxidant buffering capacity of the central nervous system, as evidenced by the progressive shift of cerebrospinal fluid [[apolipoprotein-e|apolipoprotein E]] toward irreversibly oxidized forms with aging and in CNS disorders.⁴ This redox shift impairs cholesterol transport efficiency, with the reduced monomeric form of apoE independently associated with higher transport effectiveness.⁴ Carriers of the apoE3/E4 genotype exhibit higher levels of irreversibly oxidized apoE, reflecting diminished buffering capacity.⁴

## Glymphatic clearance and brain aging

The glymphatic system — responsible for clearing metabolic waste from the central nervous system — shows progressive functional decline with brain aging that is significantly compromised in neurodegenerative and metabolic disorders.⁵ The diffusion tensor imaging along the perivascular space (DTI-ALPS) index provides a quantitative measure of glymphatic clearance capacity and correlates with multiple hallmarks of aging including telomere length, brain structure, and cognitive performance.⁵ Brain age models integrating the DTI-ALPS index alongside choroid plexus volume achieve robust predictive accuracy and replicate well across independent cohorts, outperforming models based on chronological age alone.⁵ The brain age gap — the difference between predicted and chronological age — is elevated in patients with neurodegenerative and metabolic disorders and imparts increased mortality risk.⁵ Sex-specific modifiable factors influence brain aging trajectories, with musculoskeletal health predominating in females and pulmonary metrics in males.⁵ Systolic blood pressure below 120 mmHg is strongly associated with reduced brain age gaps.⁵

## Neurotransmitter dysregulation

[[mao-b|Monoamine oxidase B]] catalyzes the oxidative deamination of neurotransmitters and plays a mechanistic role in the pathogenesis of neurodegenerative disorders.³ Dysregulation of MAO-B activity disrupts neurotransmitter homeostasis and generates reactive oxygen species as byproducts of its catalytic cycle, contributing to oxidative stress burden. MAO-B represents both a mechanistic biomarker target and a potential therapeutic node for intervention in neurodegeneration.³

## Population genetics and biological aging

Comprehensive multiomics profiling across ethnicity, geography, and age reveals that ancestry and environmental context shape distinct molecular risk pathways affecting neurodegenerative disease susceptibility.² Ethnicity-associated molecular features link to host metabolism, autoimmune disease risk, drug metabolism, and neurodegenerative pathways.² Geography influences biological aging trajectories: individuals of East Asian ancestry show lower biological age in their ancestral regions, while those of European ancestry exhibit lower biological age in North America than in Europe.² Diet-microbiome-metabolism interactions display ethnicity-specific patterns with health implications.² Specific genetic variants and gene expression differences associate with lipid metabolism and immune regulation, which in turn modulate neurodegeneration risk.²

## Mitochondrial gene therapy

[[mitofusin-decorated-extracellular-vesicles|Mitofusin-decorated extracellular vesicles (MFNEVs)]] represent an emerging natural-sourced nanoplatform for targeted delivery of nucleic acids to mitochondria, addressing a critical barrier in developing therapies for mitochondrial dysfunction-associated neurodegeneration.⁶ Surface-displayed mitofusin proteins MFN1 and MFN2 direct MFNEVs to mitochondria through cooperation with the mitochondrial fusion machinery.⁶ In both in vitro and in vivo models, siRNA-loaded MFNEVs effectively reduce expression of mitochondrial DNA-encoded genes, while sgRNA-loaded MFNEVs achieve CRISPR-based mitochondrial gene editing and decrease mitochondrial DNA content.⁶ This delivery platform enables targeting of cytoplasm-sensitive macromolecular cargos to mitochondria for treatment of metabolic syndrome, neurodegeneration, and cancers associated with mitochondrial genetic mutations or dysfunction.⁶
