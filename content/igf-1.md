**Insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1)** is a polypeptide hormone that orchestrates metabolism, somatic growth, and neuronal development through activation of the [[igf-1-receptor|IGF-1 receptor (IGF-1R)]] and [[insulin-receptor|insulin receptor (IR)]] on target tissues. Beyond its canonical growth-promoting functions, IGF-1 suppresses [[ferroptosis]] — an iron-dependent form of regulated cell death — and preserves mitochondrial integrity in the metabolically stressed heart through arachidylcarnitine-dependent signaling mechanisms.¹ Circulating IGF-1 levels decline in [[obesity]] and are suppressed by [[environmental-enteric-dysfunction|environmental enteric dysfunction (EED)]], a condition of chronic intestinal inflammation and barrier failure that undermines child linear growth.² Engineered insulin analogs bearing strategic amino acid substitutions can simultaneously activate both [[igf-1-receptor|IGF-1R]] and [[insulin-receptor|IR]] with enhanced affinity, revealing coordinated dual-receptor signaling as a therapeutic principle for neuroprotection and metabolic disease management.³

---

## Mechanism

IGF-1 initiates its biological effects by binding to the [[igf-1-receptor|IGF-1 receptor]], a receptor tyrosine kinase that shares structural and mechanistic homology with the [[insulin-receptor|insulin receptor]]. Both receptors activate overlapping downstream phosphorylation cascades through phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K) and mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathways, promoting glucose uptake, protein synthesis, and cellular survival programs. At the mitochondrial level, IGF-1 confers metabolic protection through arachidylcarnitine-dependent mechanisms that preserve fatty acid β-oxidation and ATP synthesis during periods of high metabolic demand.¹ This mitochondrial cytoprotection is particularly critical in tissues dependent on oxidative phosphorylation, including the myocardium and central nervous system. IGF-1 secretion and circulating levels are regulated by growth hormone, nutritional status, and systemic inflammation. In [[obesity]], plasma IGF-1 is significantly reduced, compromising these cytoprotective mechanisms and predisposing cells to [[ferroptosis]]-mediated cell death.¹

## Cardiac cytoprotection in obesity-induced cardiomyopathy

[[Obesity]] is characterized by dyslipidemia, elevated circulating triglycerides, and profoundly suppressed plasma IGF-1 — a metabolic derangement that precipitates progressive cardiac pathology. High-fat diet feeding in mice induces cardiac hypertrophy, interstitial fibrosis, impaired contractile function, and marked metabolic reprogramming in which oxidative phosphorylation is displaced by glycolytic dependence.¹ Transcriptomic profiling of hearts from both obese mice and human patients identified [[ferroptosis]] as a central node in myocardial injury during obesity cardiomyopathy.¹ Transgenic mice with cardiac-specific IGF-1 overexpression were robustly protected against all cardinal pathological features: contractile function was preserved, cardiac hypertrophy and fibrosis were suppressed, mitochondrial integrity markers (PGC1α and uncoupling protein-2) remained intact, and ferroptotic cell death was substantially blocked.¹ The protective mechanism depends critically on arachidylcarnitine accumulation, which directly antagonizes the lipid peroxidation cascade that initiates ferroptosis. Beyond cardiac effects, IGF-1 overexpression restored glucose tolerance and reduced systemic inflammation, indicating that cardiac IGF-1 signaling coordinates metabolic homeostasis at both organ and systemic levels. These findings establish IGF-1 restoration as a rational therapeutic target for prevention and treatment of [[obesity-cardiomyopathy|obesity-induced heart failure]].

## Growth signaling and the intestinal-inflammation axis

IGF-1 is the predominant physiological determinant of linear growth in infancy and early childhood, with plasma levels tightly correlated with height-for-age outcomes in the first year of life.² [[Environmental-enteric-dysfunction|Environmental enteric dysfunction (EED)]] — characterized by mucosal inflammation, elevated intestinal permeability, and chronic immune activation — suppresses circulating IGF-1 and severely impairs linear growth in children living in low-resource environments. In a prospective Indonesian birth cohort, elevated fecal markers of intestinal barrier damage (myeloperoxidase and alpha-1 antitrypsin) and systemic intestinal translocation (plasma intestinal fatty acid binding protein) were strongly associated with reduced plasma IGF-1 levels.² The mechanistic pathway operates through systemic inflammation: EED-driven elevation of [[cd14|CD14]] and C-reactive protein correlated with decreased IGF-1 secretion and bioavailability.² Importantly, maternal height and birth weight significantly predict child IGF-1 levels and subsequent growth trajectory, indicating that IGF-1-dependent growth is shaped by both prenatal nutritional and immune conditions and postnatal intestinal health. The identification of EED-mediated IGF-1 suppression as a critical mechanism underlying stunting suggests that interventions restoring intestinal barrier function and suppressing chronic inflammation could restore growth signaling and substantially improve linear growth outcomes in malnourished and environmentally vulnerable child populations.

## Neuroprotection via engineered dual-receptor agonism

In the developing and mature nervous system, coordinated [[insulin]] and IGF-1 signaling is essential for neuronal survival, synaptic plasticity, and cognitive development. An engineered insulin analog bearing four strategic amino acid substitutions — [GluB10, D-HisB24, GlyB31, TyrB32]-insulin (1Ins) — achieves approximately 1000-fold increased binding affinity for the [[igf-1-receptor|IGF-1 receptor]] relative to native [[insulin]], approaching native IGF-1 binding strength while retaining high affinity for both [[insulin-receptor|IR-A and IR-B]].³ In differentiated neuronal cells, 1Ins robustly activates both [[insulin-receptor]] and [[igf-1-receptor|IGF-1R]] signaling pathways simultaneously and promotes neuronal survival to a degree substantially exceeding native [[insulin]] or native IGF-1 alone in standardized neuroprotection assays.³ Cryo-electron microscopy structures reveal how the engineered substitutions enable the insulin molecular scaffold to adopt a conformation compatible with the larger binding pocket of the [[igf-1-receptor|IGF-1 receptor]], a conformational flexibility mechanically impossible with native insulin. Critically, in vivo glucose regulation by 1Ins in rodent models remained comparable to native [[insulin]], demonstrating that dual-receptor agonism does not compromise metabolic homeostasis.³ This engineering principle extends the therapeutic scope of IGF-1 biology to neuroprotection, neural regeneration, and metabolic-neurological disorders in which coordinated signaling across multiple growth factor receptors is mechanistically advantageous.
