**Amyloid-beta (Aβ)** is a proteolytic fragment of the amyloid precursor protein whose accumulation and aggregation form a cardinal pathological hallmark of [[Alzheimer's disease]], co-existing with [[tau]] protein tangles.¹ Despite decades of drug development effort targeting amyloid-β clearance and aggregation, current therapeutic approaches have largely failed to rescue synaptic dysfunction and cognitive decline in affected patients, revealing a critical gap between pathological hallmark and functional outcome. Emerging evidence establishes that synaptic dysfunction and synaptic loss — rather than amyloid-β burden alone — are the central drivers of cognitive decline in Alzheimer's disease, necessitating a therapeutic paradigm shift toward mechanisms that directly restore neural function.¹

---

## Pathological role in Alzheimer's disease

Amyloid-β accumulates progressively in the brains of Alzheimer's disease patients, forming extracellular plaques and intracellular deposits that have long been considered the primary pathogenic lesion. However, amyloid-β pathology alone does not fully explain the progressive functional impairment characteristic of the disease.¹ Mounting evidence indicates that amyloid-β-induced synaptic dysfunction and subsequent neuronal loss are the proximate mechanisms driving cognitive decline. The peptide disrupts synaptic plasticity, impairs long-term potentiation, and triggers compensatory synaptic remodeling that ultimately fails to maintain cognitive networks. This disconnect between amyloid burden and clinical severity has emerged as a central challenge in Alzheimer's disease therapeutics, with multiple amyloid-targeting agents producing minimal cognitive benefit despite effective amyloid reduction on brain imaging.

---

## Therapeutic approaches and the amyloid hypothesis crisis

For over two decades, amyloid-β has served as the primary target for Alzheimer's disease drug development, with therapeutic strategies spanning monoclonal antibodies targeting amyloid-β, beta-secretase inhibitors to block amyloid-β production, and small molecules promoting amyloid-β clearance. Yet these conventional approaches have failed to rescue synaptic function or meaningfully slow cognitive decline in clinical trials.¹ This therapeutic failure has reshaped understanding of Alzheimer's disease pathogenesis: amyloid-β pathology is necessary but not sufficient for explaining disease progression. The cross-talk between amyloid-β and tau, the contribution of neuroinflammation, and the role of mitochondrial dysfunction remain incompletely addressed by amyloid-focused interventions alone.

---

## Emerging multi-target strategies: neuromodulation and enhanced delivery

Contemporary therapeutic innovation in Alzheimer's disease has shifted toward complementary mechanisms that bypass the limitations of amyloid-β-centric approaches. Piezoelectric nanoparticle-driven neuromodulation represents one such paradigm: non-genetic, focused ultrasound-based stimulation of hippocampal circuits with piezoelectric nanoparticles restores impaired gamma oscillations, enhances synaptic plasticity, and remodels memory-related network connectivity independent of amyloid-β clearance.¹ This approach directly targets the synaptic dysfunction that drives cognitive decline, suggesting that restoring neural oscillations and synaptic information processing may offer superior therapeutic benefit compared to amyloid reduction alone.

Concurrently, enhanced brain drug delivery via the [[blood-brain-barrier]] represents a second major strategy. Intranasal delivery systems using chitosan-coated bilosomes, for example, substantially improve brain bioavailability of neuroprotective agents such as [[olmesartan]] — an angiotensin II receptor blocker with antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties — achieving marked cognitive improvement in preclinical models of Alzheimer's disease.² These delivery innovations enable brain-penetrant agents that address downstream amyloid-β toxicity and synaptic loss through complementary molecular pathways, including neuroprotection and neuroinflammation suppression.
