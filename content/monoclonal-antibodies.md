## Overview

**Monoclonal antibodies** (mAbs) are highly specific [[protein]] therapeutics and diagnostic reagents derived from single B cell clones, engineered to recognize defined [[epitope|epitopes]] on target antigens with exquisite precision. Since their development, mAbs have become critical agents in infectious disease control, [[cancer]] immunotherapy, and autoimmune diagnostics, with applications expanding rapidly across both therapeutic and diagnostic domains. Recent scientific advances demonstrate their versatility through the discovery of cross-reactive mAbs that neutralize genetically diverse viral pathogens, non-neutralizing protection mechanisms that bypass conventional antibody function, high-throughput discovery platforms leveraging single-cell isolation and optofluidic technologies, and optimized delivery formats such as antibody-drug conjugates that enhance therapeutic efficacy while minimizing off-target toxicity.

## Diagnostic Applications and Enhanced Sensitivity

Monoclonal antibody-based lateral flow immunoassays have achieved exceptional diagnostic performance for emerging and endemic viral pathogens, substantially surpassing conventional antigen detection methodologies. A broad-lineage [[Lassa virus]] rapid diagnostic test employing newly generated mAb pairs targeting the [[lassa-virus-nucleoprotein]] demonstrated approximately 64-fold greater analytical sensitivity compared to the commercially available ReLASV Pan-Lassa Antigen Rapid Test, while maintaining 100% specificity across 60 human serum and plasma specimens and successfully detecting multiple genetically diverse [[Lassa virus]] strains despite lineage-dependent analytical sensitivity variations.¹ This diagnostic advancement aligns with World Health Organization REASSURED framework criteria, providing rapid visual readouts that remain stable at ambient temperatures—critical attributes for decentralized testing workflows in resource-limited endemic settings. The sandwich immunoassay format, employing pairs of mAbs that recognize distinct epitopes on a single antigen, achieves optimal analytical performance when combined with [[au-pt-nanozymes|signal-amplifying nanoparticle labeling]]. Oriented immobilization of mAbs on [[au-pt-nanozymes]] via [[aptamer|aptamer-based capture of the antibody fragment crystallizable region]] preserves antigen-binding domain accessibility while maintaining the peroxidase-mimicking catalytic activity of core@shell Au@Pt nanoparticles, thereby producing enhanced coloration in lateral flow test zones without sacrificing either antibody reactivity or nanozyme catalytic efficiency.² This approach was validated for sensitive detection of [[anti-Müllerian hormone]] in clinical specimens.

## High-Throughput Discovery and Optofluidic Platforms

The rapid isolation of patient-derived monoclonal antibodies targeting conformational epitopes on membrane-bound antigens has been substantially accelerated through [[optofluidic-antibody-discovery|optofluidic platforms]] and single B-cell sorting methodologies. Cell-based optofluidic antibody discovery pipelines enable efficient generation of large mAb libraries while capturing the heterogeneity of individual patient immune responses, including B cell clonality, somatic mutation landscapes, immunoglobulin isotype diversity, and pathogenic potential. These platforms were exemplified in the generation of patient-derived mAbs against [[myelin-oligodendrocyte-glycoprotein]] (MOG), a transmembrane autoantigen targeted in MOG antibody-associated disease (MOGAD), where optofluidic workflows circumvented the labor-intensive and inefficient techniques previously required to isolate mAbs recognizing three-dimensional membrane protein structures.³ By enabling rapid library construction from immune donors and convalescent individuals, optofluidic discovery platforms have dramatically expanded capacity to identify rare B cell clones producing antibodies with potent neutralizing or non-neutralizing protective activity, thereby accelerating the translational pathway from basic immunology to therapeutic candidate identification.

## Cross-Reactive and Non-Neutralizing Protection Against Viral Pathogens

Monoclonal antibodies targeting conserved regions of viral glycoproteins can achieve cross-subtype neutralization of genetically distant influenza viruses, a property fundamentally shaped by immunological imprinting during early childhood viral exposures. Analysis of individuals vaccinated with the 2021–22 seasonal influenza vaccine, particularly those born in the 1990s, revealed that a substantial proportion of isolated mAbs bound to epitopes within the hemagglutinin head domain of both contemporary H1N1 vaccine strains and historical [[influenza-a-h3n2|H3N2]] strains from the mid-1990s, with the unmutated common ancestors of many of these mAbs demonstrating cross-reactivity across these genetically distant subtypes.⁴ This cross-subtype antibody response was recapitulated in ferrets sequentially exposed to historical H3N2 viruses and contemporary influenza vaccines, demonstrating that prior viral exposures fundamentally reshape antibody specificity elicited by entirely different [[influenza-a-h1n1|influenza H1N1]] subtypes—a finding with profound implications for universal vaccine design.⁵ Recent H1N1 viruses have acquired amino acid substitutions that abrogate binding by these cross-reactive mAbs, revealing the evolutionary pressure exerted by population-level antibody responses at the antigenic level.

Protection against viral infection frequently occurs through mechanisms entirely independent of viral neutralization activity. A panel of human mAbs isolated from a [[Crimean-Congo hemorrhagic fever virus]] (CCHFV) immune donor following natural nosocomial infection included antibodies specific for [[glycoprotein-38]] (GP38) that conferred substantial protection against heterologous virus challenge in an experimental mouse infection model despite lacking detectable neutralizing activity.⁶ Instead of preventing viral entry or fusion, GP38-specific mAbs mediated protection primarily through complement-dependent cellular lysis, demonstrating that broadly protective antibody responses may target epitopes that do not directly obstruct viral function. Similarly, neutralizing and non-neutralizing mAbs against [[Powassan virus]] were isolated from a convalescent human donor using single B-cell sorting with viral [[ediii|EDIII]] and full-length E ectodomain as antigen bait.⁷ Multiple mAbs targeting distinct epitopes across the E glycoprotein—including the EDIII lateral ridge/C-C' loop and non-EDIII regions—were evaluated in lethal mouse challenge models, with two mAbs demonstrating substantial protective efficacy and defining key targets of the human antibody response to this emerging tick-borne pathogen.

## Cancer Immunotherapy Through Checkpoint Modulation

Monoclonal antibodies targeting immune checkpoint molecules have emerged as a critical strategy for reversing T cell dysfunction within immunosuppressive tumor microenvironments. [[CD96]]-targeted monoclonal antibody therapy can restore anti-tumor CD8⁺ T cell function in colorectal cancer by disrupting the interaction between [[CD96]] on tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes and its ligand [[CD155]] (necl-5, CD111), which is enriched on quiescent persister tumor cells that accumulate following neoadjuvant chemotherapy.⁸ By blocking the CD155-CD96 interaction, anti-CD96 mAbs restore the cytotoxic capacity of chemotherapy-resistant tumor-infiltrating T cells, highlighting a mechanism by which mAbs can redirect immune recognition of residual disease burden. This approach exemplifies the expanding role of mAbs in modulating immune tolerance within tumor niches and overcoming adaptive resistance mechanisms.

## Physicochemical Characterization and Optimization

The optimization of monoclonal antibody solution properties—including viscosity, thermal stability, and higher-order structure—has become rate-limiting in therapeutic antibody development, particularly for agents administered at elevated concentrations via subcutaneous injection to maximize patient convenience. [[Imaging-based-viscometry|Imaging-based viscometry platforms]] integrating wide-field fluorescence microscopy with single particle tracking (SPT) software enable rapid, low-volume viscosity measurements (down to 2 μL) of mAb solutions, circumventing the severe throughput and sample volume constraints of traditional cone-and-plate rheometry.⁹ Validation using Immunoglobulin G1 and G2 (IgG1/IgG2) solutions at varying protein concentrations and viscosities (2–31 centipoise) achieved excellent correlation with reference methods (R² = 0.97), establishing a high-throughput platform for rapid syringeability assessment and candidate selection during early-stage therapeutic development.

Antibody-drug conjugate (ADC) development requires systematic optimization of linker-payload architecture, conjugation site, and drug-to-antibody ratio (DAR) to preserve mAb thermal stability and structural integrity. Ion mobility-mass spectrometry (IM-MS) combined with collision-induced unfolding (CIU) analysis revealed that distinct linker-payload structures differentially affect the gas-phase stability of the Fc domain, with strong correlation observed between gas-phase stability metrics (CIU₅₀) and thermal stability (Tm).¹⁰ Site-specific conjugation to engineered cysteine residues produced substantially less disruption to antibody higher-order structure compared to conjugation at interchain cysteines, while varying drug-to-antibody ratios and positional isomers with identical DARs exhibited distinct conformational signatures. These findings establish IM-MS and CIU as a high-throughput, information-rich analytical platform for probing ADC stability and guiding rational optimization of linker-payload combinations for cancer immunotherapies with improved therapeutic indices.
