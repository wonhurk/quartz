**Sonodynamic therapy (SDT)** is a non-invasive ultrasound-activated cancer treatment modality that harnesses acoustic cavitation—the nucleation, growth, and violent collapse of gas-filled bubbles under ultrasound irradiation—to generate reactive oxygen species (ROS) and trigger immunogenic cell death in deep-seated tumors. Unlike [[photodynamic-therapy|photodynamic therapy]], which is limited to superficial and light-penetrant tissues, SDT penetrates bone and dense parenchyma, enabling treatment of [[osteosarcoma]] and other malignancies inaccessible to conventional light-activated approaches.¹ The therapeutic cascade integrates engineered nanoparticle platforms—particularly bone-targeting liposomes such as [[lpim|LPIM]]—carrying sonosensitizers including [[icg|indocyanine green (ICG)]], hypoxia-alleviating oxygen sources like [[mno2|MnO₂]], and cavitation nucleators that work in concert to maximize ROS production and activate endogenous anti-tumor immunity via [[cgas-sting|cGAS-STING signaling]].

---

## Mechanism

Sonodynamic therapy operates through a well-defined mechanistic cascade initiated by external ultrasound irradiation. When ultrasound waves penetrate tissue containing engineered nanoparticles, they induce the nucleation and growth of cavitation bubbles at the sonosensitizer-coated nanoparticle interface. The sonosensitizer—typically [[icg|indocyanine green (ICG)]]—converts the mechanical energy from bubble oscillation into reactive oxygen species through acoustic cavitation-driven photochemical processes.¹ The collapse phase, driven by the pressure differential between bubble interior and surrounding liquid, generates localized shock waves and transient cavitation jets that either directly rupture tumor cell membranes or produce ROS that initiate oxidative cell death pathways. This cavitation-based mechanism confers SDT's most distinctive advantage: unlike light-based therapies limited to superficial tissues with short penetration depths, ultrasound waves traverse bone and deep parenchymal tissues with minimal attenuation, rendering SDT particularly valuable for skeletal malignancies and internal cancers resistant to conventional treatment.¹

## Osteosarcoma applications

[[Osteosarcoma]], a highly aggressive primary bone malignancy affecting adolescents and young adults, represents the paradigmatic clinical indication for SDT. Conventional therapies—surgery combined with neoadjuvant and adjuvant chemotherapy—are constrained by narrow safe resection margins and cumulative systemic toxicity. A recent SDT-based nano-immunoamplifier strategy directly addresses these limitations through targeted bone-homing liposomes (LPIM) co-loaded with [[icg|indocyanine green]] sonosensitizer and [[mno2|manganese dioxide (MnO₂)]] oxygen-releasing nanoparticles.¹ The liposomes achieve osteolytic region targeting within 24 hours in vivo through phosphate-mediated bone mineralization recognition. Under low-intensity focused ultrasound activation, this system achieved a 93.5% tumor inhibition rate and markedly reduced osteolytic bone destruction in preclinical models, while triggering immunogenic cell death.¹

The integration of [[mno2|MnO₂]] serves dual critical functions. First, it alleviates the hypoxic tumor microenvironment that would otherwise suppress ROS generation through competitive oxygen consumption. Second, it catalytically consumes intracellular glutathione (GSH), the primary antioxidant barrier protecting tumors from oxidative stress. By simultaneously enhancing ROS production and suppressing GSH-mediated detoxification, [[mno2|MnO₂]] dramatically amplifies SDT-induced immunogenic cell death. Furthermore, the release of Mn²⁺ ions from [[mno2|MnO₂]] nanoparticle degradation activates the [[cgas-sting|cGAS-STING signaling pathway]], establishing an endogenous immunoamplification loop that sustains anti-tumor immune surveillance and CD8⁺ T cell infiltration beyond the initial SDT activation window.¹

## Cavitation optimization and nanoparticle engineering

The efficacy and safety profile of SDT critically depend on precise engineering control of acoustic cavitation dynamics. Hollow mesoporous silica nanoparticles (HMSNs) have emerged as the superior cavitation nucleators, achieving cavitation thresholds of 0.56 W/cm² under 840 kHz ultrasound irradiation—substantially below the FDA-established safety ceiling of 3 W/cm².² This represents a decisive margin for clinical translation, permitting effective therapy at acoustic intensities far below those associated with tissue damage or off-target cavitation-induced toxicity.

Cavitation nucleation is fundamentally size-dependent, with maximum bubble nucleation enhancement observed at approximately 100 nm particle diameter under therapeutic ultrasound frequencies.² The mechanistic basis involves the concave-convex curvature principle: hydrophobic surface modifications and hollow nanoparticle architectures create effective concave interfaces that stabilize dissolved gas nuclei, drastically lowering the energetic nucleation barrier compared to conventional convex hydrophilic spheres.² Particles smaller than 100 nm present insufficient surface area for stable gas nuclei entrapment, while particles larger than 100 nm present higher nucleation energy barriers due to reduced curvature. This size-dependent sweet spot has profound implications for rational nanoparticle design: optimizing particle dimensions enables therapeutic cavitation at clinically safe acoustic intensities while maximizing bubble oscillation amplitude, expansion rate, and collapse violence—the fundamental drivers of ROS generation and membrane disruption. These quantitative design principles establish a clear pathway for engineering next-generation SDT nanocarriers with improved therapeutic window and translational potential.
