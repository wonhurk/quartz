**Multiple myeloma (MM)** is a malignancy of plasma cells characterized by clonal proliferation of immunoglobulin-producing cells within the bone marrow. Relapsed and refractory forms of the disease present formidable therapeutic challenges, with progression occurring in the majority of patients after initial treatment.¹ Recent advances have demonstrated that [[bcma|B-cell maturation antigen (BCMA)]] serves as a central therapeutic vulnerability, and that [[psmd1|PSMD1]], a critical subunit of the 19S proteasome regulatory particle, represents an emerging target to overcome proteasome inhibitor resistance and activate immune-mediated killing pathways.² The convergence of targeted protein degradation approaches with immunotherapy—including both antibody-drug conjugates and [[car-t|CAR-T cell therapy]]—has fundamentally reshaped the therapeutic landscape for this previously incurable disease.

---

## Mechanism

Multiple myeloma arises through sequential genetic events leading to malignant transformation of bone marrow plasma cells, which proliferate autonomously and produce pathogenic immunoglobulins. The disease exhibits profound heterogeneity, with subsets of tumors showing differential dependence on proteasomal degradation pathways. [[psmd1|PSMD1]], a critical component of the 19S regulatory particle of the proteasome, is significantly overexpressed in MM patient samples and correlates with adverse prognostic features.² PSMD1 overexpression enables tumor cells to sustain high rates of protein synthesis and degradation, providing a protective buffer against proteasome inhibitor-based therapies. Genetic and pharmacological depletion of PSMD1 disrupts this equilibrium, triggering accumulation of polyubiquitinated proteins and forcing cells toward apoptosis.² Additionally, PSMD1 depletion remodels the proteomic landscape to activate immune-related pathways, positioning this target as a mechanism to simultaneously suppress tumor growth and augment anti-tumor immunity.

## BCMA-directed antibody-drug conjugates

[[bcma|B-cell maturation antigen (BCMA)]] is expressed on the surface of malignant plasma cells and serves as the primary target for antibody-drug conjugate (ADC) therapy in relapsed/refractory MM. [[belantamab-mafodotin]], a BCMA-targeting ADC, demonstrated single-agent activity in early trials but was associated with dose-limiting ocular toxicity. The combination of [[belantamab-mafodotin]] with [[carfilzomib]], [[lenalidomide]], and [[dexamethasone]] (KRd-b) was designed to leverage the cytotoxic potential of each agent while managing toxicity through optimized dosing schedules. In a phase 1/2 study of relapsed/refractory MM patients, the KRd-b combination achieved an overall response rate of 89.5%, with 78.9% of patients achieving very good partial response or better.¹ Among all complete responders, minimal residual disease (MRD) negativity was achieved in 100%, indicating profound tumor suppression. At a median follow-up of 19.3 months, the 24-month progression-free survival rate reached 74.3% and overall survival 85.1%, substantially exceeding historical benchmarks for this population.¹

The recommended phase 2 dose of belantamab mafodotin was established at 1.9 mg/kg administered intravenously every 8 weeks, achieving a favorable safety profile with no dose-limiting toxicities at this level. Keratopathy, the mechanistically predicted on-target toxicity of BCMA-directed ADCs, occurred in 94.7% of patients but was predominantly grade 1 to 2 in severity and reversible upon dose interruption or supportive ophthalmologic management.¹ Grade ≥3 keratopathy was observed in 31.6% of patients—a rate comparable to historical cohorts despite the less frequent dosing schedule—and none required permanent treatment discontinuation. Hematologic and gastrointestinal adverse events were consistent with the known profiles of proteasome inhibitor and immunomodulatory agent monotherapy, indicating that the combination did not substantially increase toxicity beyond additive effects.

## CAR-T and immune-escape mechanisms

[[car-t|Chimeric antigen receptor T-cell (CAR-T) therapy]] directed against [[bcma|BCMA]] has demonstrated substantial clinical benefit in relapsed/refractory MM patients, with durable remissions in a proportion of treated individuals.³ However, immune escape and cellular therapy resistance remain significant clinical challenges. Investigation of the molecular features of residual disease following BCMA CAR-T therapy has revealed upregulation of [[cd74|CD74]], a receptor for migration inhibitory factor (MIF), on tumor cells.³ [[cd74|CD74]] activation promotes immunosuppressive signaling and facilitates tumor evasion of CAR-T recognition. Preclinical studies combining [[cd74|CD74]] blockade with BCMA-directed CAR-T cells have demonstrated enhanced CAR-T proliferation, cytokine production, and anti-tumor activity in patient-derived MM models.³ These findings suggest that dual-targeting strategies—simultaneously disrupting BCMA-driven survival signals while neutralizing CD74-mediated immune escape—may overcome resistance to single-antigen CAR-T therapy and extend the durability of remissions.

## PSMD1-targeted protein degradation

Beyond BCMA-directed approaches, [[psmd1|PSMD1]]-directed therapies have emerged as a novel strategy to overcome proteasomal resistance and activate immune-mediated tumor control. Small interfering RNA (siRNA) targeting PSMD1, delivered via lipid nanoparticles (LNPs), effectively reduced tumor cell viability in MM cell lines and primary patient samples while sparing normal hematopoietic cells.² In xenograft models of MM, PSMD1 siRNA LNPs significantly suppressed tumor growth and prolonged survival compared to vehicle controls.² The mechanism underlying this anti-tumor effect involves both direct induction of apoptosis through polyubiquitinated protein accumulation and indirect immune activation. Proteomic profiling revealed that PSMD1 depletion triggers robust upregulation of immune-related pathways, including antigen presentation and interferon signaling, suggesting that this approach may repurpose protein degradation targeting as a mechanism of immunotherapy.² Critically, PSMD1-directed targeting overcame the protective effects of the bone marrow microenvironment and reversed resistance to proteasome inhibitors, positioning this strategy to address one of the most significant barriers to long-term disease control in MM.
