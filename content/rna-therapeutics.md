**RNA therapeutics** represent a transformative treatment modality that harnesses lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) as carriers to deliver nucleic acids—including siRNA, mRNA, and gene editing components—directly to diseased tissues, enabling molecular repair beyond the reach of conventional small-molecule drugs.¹ Unlike chemical therapeutics, RNA therapeutics target underlying genetic and molecular pathologies by silencing pathogenic genes, delivering therapeutic proteins, or performing precise genomic edits in patients with inherited and chronic diseases.² The clinical efficacy of RNA therapeutics has historically been constrained by two complementary delivery challenges: the chemical and physical instability of LNP formulations during liquid storage and cold-chain distribution, and the inability of nanoparticles to penetrate pathological tissue barriers and achieve sufficient cellular transfection within target organs.¹²

---

## Mechanism and formulation challenges

LNP-based delivery depends on the interplay of ionizable lipids, PEG-conjugated lipids, structural lipids, and cholesterol to form stable nanostructures that protect encapsulated nucleic acids while facilitating cellular uptake.¹ However, LNPs in conventional aqueous suspension face progressive degradation during storage, particularly at room temperature and during extended cold-chain transport—a critical limitation for point-of-care and global distribution.¹ Furthermore, in diseased tissues characterized by pathological mucus hypersecretion or dense extracellular matrices, LNPs encounter substantial diffusion barriers that prevent penetration to target cells. In the lung, for example, aberrant mucin production and mucociliary dysfunction create a viscous microenvironment that traps nanoparticles, severely limiting the efficacy of inhaled or intrathoracic RNA therapeutics.¹

Recent advances have tackled these barriers through rational formulation engineering. A lyoprotectant platform incorporating N-acetylcysteine (NAC)—a clinically validated mucolytic agent—into sucrose-based matrices enables both formulation stabilization and biological function within a single design.¹ During lyophilization, NAC is integrated as a functional additive that preserves the physicochemical integrity of LNPs while remaining bioactive. Upon reconstitution in the lung, the released NAC performs extracellular mucolysis, enzymatically degrading mucin polymers and expanding the diffusion space for nanoparticles, while the simultaneously released siRNA-loaded LNPs penetrate the cleared mucus and enter epithelial cells for intracellular RNAi-mediated gene silencing.¹

---

## Respiratory applications

In [[mucus-hypersecretory-lung-disease|mucus-hypersecretory lung diseases]]—characterized by pathological mucin overproduction, chronic airway inflammation, and compromised lung function—the NAC-lyophilized LNP platform enables efficient siRNA delivery and therapeutic gene suppression.¹ In murine models of mucus-driven respiratory disease, single-dose administration of lyophilized siRNA-LNPs achieved enhanced therapeutic outcomes through this sequential dual-action mechanism: the extracellular NAC component remodeled the mucus barrier, permitting nanoparticle diffusion and cellular entry, while the intracellular RNAi component suppressed inflammatory pathways and restored airway function.¹ This approach overcomes both the storage instability that has historically limited RNA therapeutics for chronic respiratory diseases and the tissue-penetration barrier imposed by pathological mucus hypersecretion.

## Cardiac applications and optimized delivery

The cardiac therapeutic space presents distinct delivery challenges, requiring high transfection efficiency within cardiomyocytes across a mechanically complex tissue. To identify optimal LNP formulations for cardiac gene delivery, researchers developed a high-throughput screening platform using human induced pluripotent stem cell-derived cardiomyocytes (hiPSC-CMs), which recapitulate human cardiac physiology more faithfully than animal models.² Through chemical library screening, the lead LNP formulation—designated 18:1 TAP10—was identified as a potent cardio-tropic LNP with exceptional transfection capability in human cardiomyocytes.²

The choice of administration route critically determines the spatial distribution and cellular tropism of LNP delivery within the heart.² Intravenous administration achieves broad but shallow tissue penetration with moderate cardiomyocyte transfection but high efficiency in endothelial cells (up to 40%). Intrathoracic and intracoronary routes enable improved myocardial distribution with intermediate cardiomyocyte engagement. By contrast, intramyocardial (direct myocardial injection) achieves the highest cardiomyocyte-specific transfection, with 36% transfection efficiency at the injection site and 13% distal penetration—substantially exceeding non-myocyte populations.² This route-dependent differentiation enables strategic matching of delivery approaches to therapeutic goals: systemic diseases benefit from intravenous delivery, while focal cardiac pathologies exploit the superior cellular specificity of direct intramyocardial injection.

---

## Gene editing for cardiac disease

The therapeutic potential of optimized cardiac LNPs extends beyond gene silencing to in vivo gene editing. Adenine base editor (ABE) components—comprising ABE mRNA and necessary guide RNAs—have been successfully delivered using 18:1 TAP10 LNPs to patient-derived hiPSC-CMs derived from individuals with [[dmd|Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD)]].² In this experimental system, ABE-mediated adenine-to-guanine conversion achieved 80% on-target gene correction and restoration of full-length dystrophin protein expression, functionally reversing the molecular defect.² In vivo validation using a humanized DMD mouse model demonstrated that intramyocardial LNP-ABE administration generated dystrophin-positive cardiomyocytes in the injected left ventricle, establishing proof-of-concept for cardiac-directed gene editing in genetic heart disease.² This approach exemplifies how optimized LNP chemistry combined with rational route selection enables molecular repair of heritable myocardial pathologies previously inaccessible to therapy.

---

## Platform development and future directions

The human cardiomyocyte screening platform used to identify 18:1 TAP10 represents a paradigm shift in rational LNP discovery, moving away from empirical animal studies toward human cell-based prediction of transfection efficiency.² This approach accelerates identification of formulations optimized for human biology while reducing reliance on species-specific extrapolation. Similarly, the functional lyoprotectant strategy for respiratory delivery demonstrates how formulation design can simultaneously address stability, bioavailability, and therapeutic mechanism—collapsing multiple challenges into a single rational engineering solution.¹ Future advances will likely extend these principles across additional tissue compartments, targeting the central nervous system, liver, kidney, and muscle through formulation optimization coupled with targeted ligand engineering and refined understanding of LNP biodistribution.
