**Chlorin e6 (Ce6)** is a photosensitizer and pharmaceutical scaffold employed in multimodal cancer therapeutic platforms that generates reactive oxygen species through light-activated photodynamic therapy and serves as a chemical linker for conjugation with chemotherapeutic agents including [[doxorubicin]].¹² Its dual-mode mechanism—direct ROS-dependent cytotoxicity upon light exposure and indirect drug-dependent cytotoxicity via controlled release of conjugated chemotherapy—positions chlorin e6 as a versatile component in integrated cancer treatment systems.

---

## Mechanism

Chlorin e6 exerts phototherapeutic activity by absorbing photons and generating singlet oxygen and hydroxyl radicals that induce oxidative damage to cancer cell membranes and subcellular organelles.¹ When incorporated into nanoplatform systems, chlorin e6 functions synergistically with complementary mechanisms. In copper-coordinated nanoformulations designed for colorectal cancer, Ce6-generated ROS synergizes with [[cuproptosis|copper-driven cuproptosis]] to activate overlapping cell-death pathways, thereby amplifying overall cytotoxicity and specifically targeting cancer stem cells.¹ Additionally, chlorin e6 derivatives serve as chemical scaffolds for covalent conjugation with chemotherapeutic payloads through responsive linker molecules. These linkers—comprising amide bonds, disulfide bridges, and thioketal fragments—undergo selective cleavage in response to the intracellular reductive and oxidative microenvironment, enabling controlled [[doxorubicin]] release within target cells and minimizing systemic toxicity.²

---

## Colorectal cancer and immunotherapy integration

In colorectal cancer, chlorin e6-based nanoplatforms integrate photodynamic therapy with metabolic intervention and copper-driven [[cuproptosis]] to overcome tumor stemness and immunosuppressive microenvironment formation. The platform incorporates surface engineering with d-α-tocopheryl polyethylene glycol succinate (TPGS), which potently activates tumor cell macropinocytosis—a fluid-phase endocytosis pathway—thereby significantly enhancing intracellular nanocarrier accumulation.¹ Concurrently, the platform includes glutaminase inhibitors that reprogram glutamine metabolism in tumor cells while simultaneously enhancing dendritic cell maturation, a critical step in priming adaptive T-cell responses. The synergistic combination of Ce6-generated ROS and copper-catalyzed [[cuproptosis]] selectively ablates cancer stemness, effecting a robust phenotypic conversion of immunologically cold tumors into T cell-inflamed hot tumors.¹ This integrated metabolic-immunological co-regulation strategy established durable immunological memory in preclinical models, achieving 100% long-term survival in rechallenged mice at 90 days post-treatment and demonstrating the platform's potential for durable anti-tumor immunity.

---

## Breast cancer doxorubicin conjugates

Beyond standalone photodynamic therapy, chlorin e6 derivatives function as chemical scaffolds for covalent conjugation with [[doxorubicin]], enabling combined light-activated and chemotherapy-dependent mechanisms of cytotoxicity. Conjugates incorporating responsive linker fragments—particularly disulfide and thioketal linkages—undergo controlled cleavage under intracellular reductive and oxidative conditions, progressively liberating [[doxorubicin]] within target cells.² In vitro evaluation on human breast adenocarcinoma MCF-7 cells demonstrated that chlorin e6-doxorubicin conjugates exhibit dual cytotoxic modes: light-induced cell death via ROS generation and light-independent cell death via [[doxorubicin]] release from labile linkers.² Fluorescence microscopy tracking confirmed successful internalization of conjugates into tumor cells and subsequent release of [[doxorubicin]], with liberated drug accumulating in cell nuclei—the primary site of chemotherapeutic action.² This conjugate strategy demonstrates promise for developing anticancer agents with multimodal mechanisms of action, potentially reducing systemic chemotherapy toxicity through selective light-activated and intracellular chemotherapy release.
