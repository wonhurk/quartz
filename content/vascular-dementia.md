**Vascular dementia** is a major neurodegenerative disease mechanistically defined by progressive dysfunction and age-related fragility of the [[blood-brain-barrier|blood-brain barrier (BBB)]], leading to disrupted vascular integrity and cognitive decline. Unlike the amyloid-driven pathology of [[alzheimers-disease|Alzheimer's disease]] or the protein aggregation patterns of other neurodegeneration subtypes, vascular dementia emerges from the vulnerability of the BBB itself—a dynamic system whose permeability and structural integrity fundamentally change across the human lifespan.¹ Large-scale proteomic profiling of 2,279 human brain samples across six major neurodegenerative diseases reveals that vascular dementia displays distinct molecular subtypes and disease-specific protein network alterations, while simultaneously sharing dysregulated pathways in microglial activation and synaptic function with other neurodegenerative conditions.

---

## Molecular subtypes and proteomic signatures

Comprehensive multilayer proteomic analysis through the pan-neurodegeneration atlas (PanNDA) has uncovered multiple distinct molecular subtypes within vascular dementia, identified through integration of whole proteome, detergent-insoluble proteome fractions, and posttranslational modifications including phosphorylation and ubiquitination.¹ This molecular heterogeneity reflects the complex interplay between vascular dysfunction, neuroinflammation, and synaptic compromise that characterizes the disease across different affected individuals and brain regions. Within protein network analyses, vascular dementia exhibits specific hub regulators and disease-characteristic alterations that distinguish it from other neurodegenerative diseases, providing mechanistic insights into the molecular basis of cognitive dysfunction in this vascular subtype.

---

## Blood-brain barrier dysfunction across the lifespan

The BBB is not a static structure but a dynamic system whose properties fundamentally evolve across the human lifespan, a perspective critical for understanding vascular dementia pathogenesis.² During early development, the BBB operates in an acquisitive state, actively transporting nutrients and growth factors necessary for brain maturation. In healthy adulthood, the BBB becomes heterogeneous—maintaining selective permeability while adapting to regional metabolic demands. However, with advancing age, the BBB undergoes progressive fragility, characterized by structural breakdown, altered tight junction dynamics, and increased paracellular permeability.¹ This age-related deterioration represents a critical pathogenic mechanism in vascular dementia, as declining BBB integrity compromises the physical and chemical barriers essential for maintaining neuronal homeostasis and preventing accumulation of neurotoxic peripherally-derived substances within the brain parenchyma.

The consequences of BBB disruption extend beyond structural compromise. Progressive [[blood-brain-barrier|barrier]] dysfunction impairs [[cns-drug-exposure|CNS drug exposure]], altering the pharmacokinetics and therapeutic efficacy of medications intended to reach neural tissue, while simultaneously permitting increased infiltration of peripheral immune cells, inflammatory mediators, and potentially harmful blood-derived proteins into the brain microenvironment.

## Shared and disease-specific molecular pathways

While vascular dementia exhibits distinct molecular signatures, it shares critical dysregulated pathways with other major neurodegenerative conditions, suggesting convergent mechanisms of neuronal dysfunction across disease subtypes.¹ [[gpnmb|GPNMB]] — a lysosomal and microglial protein — is dysregulated across multiple neurodegenerative diseases including vascular dementia, reflecting abnormal innate immune activation and impaired lysosomal clearance capacity. Similarly, [[nptx2|NPTX2]], a synaptic pentraxin critical for synaptic plasticity and dendritic spine formation, shows dysregulation in vascular dementia alongside other neurodegenerative pathologies, indicating fundamental compromise of synaptic transmission and learning-dependent plasticity. Alongside these shared alterations, vascular dementia displays disease-specific protein network changes that predominantly reflect vascular endothelial dysfunction, pericyte loss, and disrupted angiogenic signaling—molecular hallmarks absent or less prominent in non-vascular neurodegenerative conditions.

---

## Vascular integrity and cognitive health preservation

The emergence of vascular dementia as a major neurodegenerative disease underscores that cognitive health in aging depends critically upon preserving vascular integrity and BBB function.² Therapeutic strategies for vascular dementia must therefore prioritize mechanisms that stabilize the neurovascular unit, enhance pericyte coverage of cerebral capillaries, promote angiogenesis and vascular repair, and restore tight junction proteins that maintain BBB selectivity. The lifespan-based perspective reveals that understanding how the BBB naturally transitions from the acquisitive state of development through the heterogeneous state of adulthood to the increasingly fragile state of aging is essential for designing interventions that effectively counteract age-related vascular decline. Furthermore, recognition that vascular dementia often co-occurs with [[stroke|cerebrovascular events]] highlights the mechanistic overlap between acute ischemic injury and the chronic vascular cognitive impairment that defines this disease.
