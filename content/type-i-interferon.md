**Type I interferon (IFN-I)** is a critical cytokine family produced by innate immune cells in response to pathogen-associated molecular patterns and cellular damage signals, functioning as a central bridge between innate and adaptive immunity.¹ Its production is governed by multiple complementary signaling pathways, most prominently through [[sting|STING-mediated]] transduction in response to [[ferroptosis]] and derepressed endogenous [[retrotransposon]]s, and through [[tlr3|TLR3]]-based recognition of viral nucleic acids such as polyinosine-polycytidylic acid. The baseline capacity of an individual to mount robust IFN-I responses—particularly through the TLR3 axis—has emerged as a quantitative predictor of vaccine efficacy and adaptive immune outcomes.¹ Conversely, aberrant STING-driven IFN-I production becomes pathogenic in autoimmune and inflammatory diseases, necessitating pharmacological IFN-I suppression as a therapeutic strategy.

---

## Mechanism

Type I interferon production is activated through multiple convergent danger-sensing pathways. [[sting|STING]] acts as a central cytosolic nucleotide sensor that, upon recognition of cyclic dinucleotides or abnormal DNA, undergoes phosphorylation and recruitment of downstream kinases that activate transcription factors including IRF3 and NF-κB, culminating in robust IFN-I transcription. [[ferroptosis|Ferroptotic]] cell death triggers STING pathway activation through release of damage-associated molecular patterns, establishing an immunogenic cell death signature that synergizes with direct IFN-I induction to amplify antitumor immune responses.² 

Epigenetic silencing of endogenous retrotransposons, mediated by [[cbx4|CBX4]]-driven H3K9me3 and H3K27me3 deposition, normally suppresses innate immune activation. Loss of CBX4 in tumor cells or macrophages derepresses silenced retroelements such as RLTR4-Mm-int, which are recognized by cytosolic RNA sensors (pattern recognition receptors) and activate the TLR3-independent innate immune cascade, triggering robust type I interferon production.² [[tlr3|TLR3]], a transmembrane toll-like receptor, directly recognizes viral pathogen-associated molecular patterns including double-stranded RNA and synthetic [[polyinosine-polycytidylic-acid|poly(I:C)]]. TLR3 engagement initiates MyD88-independent TRIF-dependent signaling, activating IRF3 and producing type I interferons in response to viral infection.¹

## Cancer immunotherapy

Type I interferon acts as the central cytokine driving the anticancer immune response, particularly when induced to high levels through a mechanism termed "IFN-I tempest." Ferroptosis-based immunotherapy platforms engineered to simultaneously trigger ferroptotic cell death and abundant IFN-I production harness this synergy to suppress tumor metastasis and establish durable immunological memory in the tumor microenvironment.² The IFN-I response enhances infiltration and cytotoxic activity of [[cd8-t-cell|CD8+ T cells]] and natural killer cells, thereby heightening sensitivity to immune checkpoint blockade.² Type I interferon acts both through direct antiviral effects—priming infected or abnormal cells for death—and through indirect immune licensing, wherein IFN-I primes dendritic cells for optimal antigen presentation and polarizes the tumor microenvironment toward pro-inflammatory activation.

A critical feature of the IFN-I tempest strategy is its self-limiting inflammatory capacity. In the context of ferroptosis-driven IFN-I induction, accumulated lipid droplets inhibit dendritic cell maturation, thereby limiting excessive CD8+ T cell activation while preserving memory T cell establishment.² Furthermore, accumulated excessive IFN-I is degraded by acidic hydrolases within lysosomal compartments, preventing late-stage pathological inflammation. This self-regulatory mechanism enables therapeutic IFN-I exploitation without the collateral inflammatory toxicities that arise when STING is activated aberrantly.

## Inflammatory disease pathogenesis

Aberrant or constitutive activation of the STING pathway drives pathological type I interferon production in autoimmune and inflammatory diseases. In acute kidney injury, dysregulated STING signaling triggers excessive type I interferon production and pro-inflammatory cytokine release, amplifying renal and hepatic tissue damage. Pharmacological STING inhibition, exemplified by next-generation tetrahydroquinoline-based small molecules, potently suppresses STING-dependent IFN-I and pro-inflammatory signaling in both murine and human systems, attenuating renal and hepatic injury in cisplatin-induced acute kidney injury models.³ This disease context establishes type I interferon as a pathological mediator requiring active suppression in specific inflammatory contexts, contrasting sharply with its therapeutic exploitation in cancer immunotherapy.

## Vaccine efficacy prediction

Prevaccine type I interferon responses to TLR3 agonists emerge as a quantitative biological predictor of variable vaccine outcomes. Whole blood stimulation with [[polyinosine-polycytidylic-acid|poly(I:C)]] (polyinosine-polycytidylic acid), a TLR3-selective viral agonist, in healthy adolescents and adults prior to vaccination reveals significant associations between prevaccine type I interferon production and subsequent postvaccine antigen-specific T cell cytokine responses including interleukin-2, interferon-gamma, and IL-21.¹ This association holds across vaccine modalities—mRNA vaccines such as BNT162b2 as well as inactivated virus vaccines such as CoronaVac—and has been confirmed in independent cohorts of healthy donors. Genetic polymorphisms in the TLR3 locus itself associate with baseline IFN-I response capacity and downstream vaccine efficacy.¹ These findings suggest that a simple prevaccine immune assay measuring TLR3-induced type I interferon production could serve as a predictive biomarker for identifying individuals at risk of suboptimal vaccine responses, enabling stratified immunization strategies.
