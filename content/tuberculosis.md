## Lead

**Tuberculosis (TB)**, caused by the intracellular pathogen [[Mycobacterium tuberculosis]] (Mtb), remains one of the world's most devastating communicable diseases, ranking among the leading infectious causes of global mortality.¹ The disease's persistence and lethality stem from Mtb's evolved capacity to survive and replicate within host [[macrophages]], where it evades immune clearance through multiple overlapping metabolic strategies. Central to pathogenic success are early-phase [[cell wall biosynthesis]] mechanisms, sustained [[cholesterol catabolism]], and [[iron acquisition]] pathways, complemented by virulence factors including [[Malate Synthase]] (GlcB), which enables both persistent intracellular growth and bacterial adherence to host cells.² Recent molecular advances have identified the cell wall enzyme [[DprE1]] as a critical vulnerability, with novel [[benzoselenazinone]] inhibitors demonstrating exceptional anti-TB potency and superior oral bioavailability compared to predecessor compounds.³ Beyond traditional antimicrobial approaches, [[BCG vaccine|BCG vaccination]] confers both direct TB protection and unexpected non-specific immunomodulatory benefits,⁴ while aptamer-based therapeutics targeting virulence factors represent an emerging modality challenged by cellular delivery obstacles.⁵ Disease epidemiology reveals substantial heterogeneity across populations, with disproportionate burden in vulnerable groups including people who smoke illicit drugs in high-incidence regions.

## Microbiology and Temporal Metabolic Requirements

Mycobacterium tuberculosis's survival as an obligate intracellular pathogen depends critically on sequential adaptation to the evolving immune microenvironment of infected macrophages. Using a [[CRISPR]]-interference screen of approximately 20,000 single-guide RNAs covering over 96% of the Mtb genome, researchers identified temporally distinct gene essentiality patterns across the infection continuum.² Early in infection, genes encoding [[cell wall biosynthesis]] functions proved essential for bacterial viability, reflecting the immediate need to maintain cell envelope integrity under initial immune assault. As infection progressed to 4 and 6 weeks, the metabolic bottleneck shifted toward genes governing [[cholesterol catabolism]] and [[iron acquisition]], which remained critical across all sampled time points.² This temporal rewiring indicates that Mtb phenotypically adapts its gene expression to match the specific nutritional and immunological pressures present at each infection stage. Functional validation confirmed the biological significance of three genes—*embB*, *fadE29*, and *mbtI*—whose targeted disruption compromised Mtb's ability to persist in lung macrophages, establishing conditional essentiality as a fundamental principle governing intracellular bacterial survival.

## Cell Wall Targeting and DprE1 Inhibitors

The bacterial [[cell wall]] biosynthesis enzyme [[DprE1]] has emerged as a validated and highly promising drug target, given its essential role in the synthesis of arabinogalactan, a critical component of mycobacterial cell envelope architecture. Conventional DprE1 inhibitors based on the [[benzothiazinone]] scaffold, exemplified by PBTZ169, exhibited anti-tuberculous activity but suffered from suboptimal oral bioavailability (typically <10%) and metabolic liability in rodent models.³ Through structure-based design incorporating selenium as a bioisosteric replacement for sulfur within the aromatic ring system, researchers synthesized a new class of [[benzoselenazinone]] compounds with dramatically improved pharmacological properties.³ The most promising derivative, compound 11c, maintained exceptional potency against Mtb (minimum inhibitory concentration <0.03 μM) and potent DprE1 inhibition (IC₅₀ <0.1 μM) while achieving oral bioavailability of 31.9% in mice—a greater than threefold improvement over the benzothiazinone comparator.³ These compounds demonstrated minimal cytotoxicity toward mammalian cell lines (Vero cell IC₅₀ >100 μM), establishing a favorable safety margin. The superior bioavailability of benzoselenazinones positions this chemical series as promising preclinical candidates for progression toward clinical evaluation in TB chemotherapy.

## Virulence Factor Targeting via Aptamer Therapeutics

Complementary to enzyme inhibition, recent work has exploited aptamer-based inhibitors targeting the Mtb virulence factor [[Malate Synthase]] (also designated GlcB), a key enzyme catalyzing glyoxylate pathway reactions essential for Mtb persistence.⁵ Beyond its metabolic role, Malate Synthase functions as an adhesion factor that facilitates bacterial invasion of host cells.⁵ A rationally designed single-stranded DNA aptamer (MS10-Trunc, sequence 5'-GGTGGTGGTGG-3') was synthesized to inhibit Malate Synthase and subsequently incorporated into monoolein-based cationic [[lipid nanoparticles|cubosome lipid nanoparticles]] to overcome inherent barriers to aptamer bioavailability.⁵ Encapsulation within cationic cubosomes reduced the minimum inhibitory concentration of the free aptamer by approximately 3.5-fold against actively replicating Mtb H37Ra and shortened the bacterial lifecycle from five to three days.⁵ This approach addresses the fundamental clinical translation challenge facing aptamer therapeutics: rapid degradation in biological fluids and poor penetration across cellular membranes, both of which are partially resolved through rational lipid nanoparticle formulation design.

## Immunomodulatory Effects of BCG Vaccination

The Bacille Calmette-Guérin (BCG) [[vaccine]], which has protected millions from tuberculosis since its introduction nearly a century ago, confers benefits extending well beyond TB-specific immunity. Vaccination induces non-specific or "trained" immunomodulatory effects that provide unexpected protection against certain non-TB infections. In a nested case-control study within a randomized controlled trial, neonatal BCG vaccination demonstrated a protective effect against recurrent [[herpes simplex virus]] (HSV) infections in early childhood.⁴ Specifically, BCG-vaccinated children showed reduced recurrence rates of latent HSV labialis ("oral herpes" or "cold sores"), which affects a substantial proportion of the pediatric population and currently lacks effective preventive interventions.⁴ This heterologous protection likely reflects BCG's capacity to induce durable shifts in innate immune cell programming through epigenetic and metabolic remodeling, broadening the vaccine's public health significance beyond its original TB-prevention mandate.

## Epidemiological Heterogeneity and High-Risk Populations

Tuberculosis transmission and disease burden exhibit marked heterogeneity across populations and geographic regions. A recent respondent-driven sampling study in the Western Cape, South Africa—a region with exceptionally high TB incidence—identified people who smoke illicit drugs (PWSD) as a disproportionately affected population with significantly elevated TB disease rates compared to the general population.⁶ This finding underscores the necessity for population-specific TB intervention strategies tailored to the unique risk profiles and transmission networks of vulnerable groups. The convergence of substance use, poverty, housing instability, and limited access to healthcare in high-burden settings creates epidemiological conditions favorable to rapid TB transmission, necessitating integrated public health approaches that address both infectious disease control and the underlying social determinants of health.
