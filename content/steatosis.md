**Hepatic steatosis** is the pathological accumulation of triglycerides in hepatocytes that serves as a precursor to [[mash|metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH)]], a progressive liver disease strongly linked to obesity and diabetes. The critical pathogenic step in hepatic steatosis is not merely lipid accumulation, but rather the transition from simple steatosis to fibrosis — a process driven by lipotoxic stress within hepatocytes that triggers the release of pathogenic exosomal proteins, particularly the palmitoylated nucleoprotein [[nat10]], which activate hepatic stellate cells and initiate pathological fibrogenesis.¹ Although the morphologic hallmark of steatosis is visible on imaging and histology, the molecular mechanisms driving progression to fibrosis remain incompletely understood, and effective anti-fibrotic therapeutic interventions remain limited.

---

## Mechanism

Hepatic steatosis develops through the accumulation of triglycerides within hepatocytes, typically driven by metabolic dysfunction in the setting of obesity and insulin resistance. However, simple lipid accumulation is not the determinant of disease progression; rather, lipotoxic stress within fat-laden hepatocytes creates a pathogenic microenvironment. Under conditions of lipotoxic stress, hepatocytes exhibit dysregulated lipid metabolism and increased oxidative stress, triggering the selective release of membrane-bound exosomes containing a distinct set of pathogenic protein cargos. The [[nat10|nuclear protein NAT10]], subject to post-translational palmitoylation, becomes enriched in hepatocyte-derived exosomes under lipotoxic conditions and plays a pivotal role in the steatosis-to-fibrosis transition.¹ These exosomes are internalized by quiescent hepatic stellate cells, causing their activation into pro-fibrogenic myofibroblasts. Once activated, stellate cells produce excessive extracellular matrix and drive progressive hepatic fibrosis — transforming a metabolically dysfunctional but structurally reversible state into an irreversible fibrotic disease.

---

## Progression to fibrosis and clinical consequences

The transition from hepatic steatosis to fibrosis represents the critical determinant of clinical outcomes in [[mash]]. Fibrosis progression is the principal histological predictor of mortality in MASH and serves as the staging criterion that differentiates patients at highest risk for cirrhosis, hepatocellular carcinoma, and death.¹ Patients with simple hepatic steatosis in the absence of fibrosis generally have a more benign prognosis, whereas those with steatosis plus fibrosis face substantially elevated risk of end-stage liver disease. The exosomal trafficking of palmitoylated [[nat10]] and related hepatocyte-derived proteins represents an essential bridge between metabolic dysfunction and fibrogenesis, making this pathway a potential therapeutic target. Nevertheless, effective pharmacologic strategies to interrupt this exosomal signaling cascade or reverse stellate cell activation remain unavailable.

---

## Non-invasive diagnosis and imaging biomarkers

Historically, liver biopsy has served as the gold-standard reference method for definitively diagnosing hepatic steatosis and distinguishing simple steatosis from steatohepatitis with or without fibrosis. However, liver biopsy is invasive, carries procedural risk, and is impractical for repeated monitoring of treatment efficacy or disease progression. Recent advances in computed tomography (CT) imaging have enabled the development of composite biomarkers that non-invasively identify steatosis grade and accurately distinguish [[mash]] in individuals with obesity, particularly in the preoperative evaluation prior to bariatric surgery.² These CT-based approaches leverage multiphase imaging and texture analysis to estimate hepatic fat content and detect features suggestive of inflammation and fibrosis, potentially improving diagnostic accuracy and facilitating longitudinal monitoring without the morbidity of repeated biopsy.
