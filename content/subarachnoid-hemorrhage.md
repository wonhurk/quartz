**Subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH)** is a severe cerebrovascular condition characterized by bleeding into the space surrounding the brain, which initiates both immediate tissue damage and a cascade of secondary brain injuries. Beyond the primary hemorrhagic insult, SAH triggers robust [[microglia]]-mediated neuroinflammatory responses and white matter injury that collectively drive poor neurological outcomes.¹² The pathology involves rapid activation of brain [[microglia]] via the [[ccr5|C-C chemokine receptor 5 (CCR5)]] pathway and dysregulation of [[mir-27a-3p]] in peripheral blood [[exosomes]], both of which converge on the suppression of neuroprotective [[ppar-gamma|PPAR-gamma]] signaling and the induction of [[oligodendrocyte]] apoptosis.

---

## Mechanism

SAH initiates a biphasic injury sequence encompassing primary hemorrhagic damage followed by secondary molecular cascades. The initial hemorrhagic event causes blood accumulation in the subarachnoid space, raising intracranial pressure and directly damaging neurons and their supporting cells. Within hours, SAH activates [[microglia]]—the brain's resident immune cells—toward a pro-inflammatory phenotype that perpetuates neuronal loss and tissue dysfunction. This microglial activation is critically regulated by [[ccr5|CCR5]], which becomes rapidly upregulated on microglial surfaces after SAH and drives pro-inflammatory polarization through chemokine-mediated signaling.² The resulting inflammatory microenvironment suppresses [[oligodendrocyte]] function and promotes white matter damage, a process involving both direct cytotoxic effects and the dysregulation of intrinsic survival pathways within [[oligodendrocytes]].

---

## White Matter Injury and miR-27a-3p Dysregulation

White matter injury represents a critical contributor to persistent neurological dysfunction following SAH, arising from damage to [[oligodendrocytes]] and the myelin sheaths they produce. Following hemorrhage, [[mir-27a-3p]] becomes significantly upregulated in peripheral blood [[exosomes]] and accumulates in white matter tissue, where it inhibits [[ppar-gamma]] expression in [[oligodendrocytes]].¹ This suppression of PPAR-gamma leads to downregulation of the antioxidant enzyme [[prdx1|peroxiredoxin-1 (PRDX1)]] and activation of the [[jnk]]-mediated apoptotic pathway, triggering oligodendrocyte death and myelin breakdown. The miR-27a-3p-mediated disruption of the PPAR-gamma/PRDX1/JNK axis thus represents a mechanistic link between the early post-hemorrhagic inflammatory state and the delayed white matter pathology that undermines long-term functional recovery.

---

## Neuroinflammation and CCR5-Mediated Microglial Activation

The CCR5 pathway emerges as a central regulator of post-SAH neuroinflammation. Within the acute post-hemorrhagic phase, [[ccr5|CCR5]] expression rises prominently on [[microglia]] in cortical and perilesional regions, where it receives signals from chemokine ligands and orchestrates the shift toward a pro-inflammatory phenotype characterized by elevated production of interleukin-1β, tumor necrosis factor-α, and other damage-amplifying cytokines.² This microglial polarization perpetuates the recruitment of peripheral immune cells, amplifies local cytokine gradients, and sustains an inflammatory microenvironment hostile to neuronal and oligodendrocyte survival. The sustained CCR5 signaling on [[microglia]] thus acts as a molecular valve controlling the duration and intensity of post-hemorrhagic neuroinflammation.

---

## Therapeutic Approaches

Two complementary strategies targeting the distinct pathological mechanisms of SAH have demonstrated neuroprotective efficacy in preclinical models. Intranasal delivery of [[exosomes]] loaded with [[mir-27a-3p]] antagomir—short RNA sequences that inhibit miR-27a-3p—effectively crosses the blood-brain barrier and targets [[oligodendrocytes]], alleviating white matter injury by restoring PPAR-gamma-dependent survival signaling.¹ This exosome-based approach reduces oligodendrocyte apoptosis, promotes the proliferation and maturation of oligodendrocyte precursor cells, and leads to improved neurological and electrophysiological recovery. Concurrently, pharmacological blockade of [[ccr5|CCR5]] using maraviroc, an FDA-approved [[ccr5]] antagonist, shifts [[microglia]] toward an anti-inflammatory phenotype while suppressing pro-inflammatory cytokine production.² Maraviroc treatment attenuates brain edema, suppresses neuronal apoptosis, and significantly improves both early and long-term neurological outcomes by modulating the [[ccr5]]-dependent inflammatory microenvironment. Together, these approaches—one targeting oligodendrocyte-intrinsic miR-27a-3p dysregulation and the other suppressing microglial-extrinsic pro-inflammatory activation—offer complementary mechanistic avenues for neuroprotection in SAH.
