**Receptor for Advanced Glycation End Products (RAGE)** is a pattern recognition receptor that binds advanced glycation end products (AGEs) and danger-associated molecular patterns, particularly the S100 proteins [[s100a8|S100A8]] and [[s100a9|S100A9]], to initiate inflammatory and pro-metastatic signaling cascades.² RAGE acts as a critical molecular hub linking metabolic dysfunction and aging to pathological outcomes in both acute ischemic stroke and cancer metastasis. The receptor's activation by circulating and tissue-derived ligands triggers distinct signaling programs depending on context—from acute hyperglycemia-induced neutrophil extracellular trap (NET) formation in stroke to chronic age-dependent inflammatory remodeling of the tumor microenvironment.¹²

---

## Mechanism

RAGE mediates its pathological effects through ligand-dependent activation of intracellular signaling cascades. The receptor recognizes both endogenous (AGEs, [[s100a8|S100A8]]/[[s100a9|S100A9]]) and exogenous damage-associated molecular patterns, each capable of triggering inflammatory gene expression and cytokine production. Upon ligand binding, RAGE engages downstream effectors including the diaphanous formin [[diaph1|DIAPH1]], which reorganizes cytoskeletal architecture and modulates cell migration, invasion, and extracellular matrix remodeling.² In aging contexts, RAGE activation orchestrates broad transcriptional changes affecting inflammatory pathways, epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) programs, angiogenesis, and extracellular matrix composition. The age-dependent elevation of circulating RAGE ligands—particularly [[s100a8|S100A8]]/[[s100a9|S100A9]] proteins and glycation products—creates a feed-forward loop that amplifies pro-metastatic signaling in both tumor cells and host stromal cells.²

## RAGE in acute ischemic stroke

Acute hyperglycemia during ischemic stroke dramatically amplifies cerebral injury through RAGE-dependent mechanisms. During the acute phase following middle cerebral artery occlusion and reperfusion (MCAO/r), elevated blood glucose activates RAGE on neutrophils and endothelial cells, triggering the [[diaph1|DIAPH1]]-dependent formation of neutrophil extracellular traps ([[nets|NETs]]).¹ Although blood glucose spontaneously normalizes within hours, the pro-inflammatory effects of RAGE persist, driving extensive NET formation within 24 hours of reperfusion. These NETs mechanically obstruct cerebral microvessels and release proteolytic enzymes and damage-associated molecular patterns that exacerbate ischemic-reperfusion injury and paradoxically impair successful recanalization.¹ Pharmacological antagonism of RAGE with FPS-ZM1 (N-benzyl-4-chloro-N-cyclohexylbenzamide) or enzymatic degradation of NETs with deoxyribonuclease I (DNase I) substantially reduces microvascular obstruction and brain infarction, establishing RAGE and its downstream NET pathway as tractable therapeutic targets in stroke patients with hyperglycemia.¹

## RAGE and aging-dependent breast cancer metastasis

Aging emerges as a critical determinant of breast cancer metastatic potential through RAGE-mediated host-tumor crosstalk. Aging significantly accelerates breast cancer dissemination to distant organs across multiple mouse models, and this effect is critically dependent on host RAGE expression.² The aged microenvironment exhibits substantially elevated levels of RAGE ligands, including [[s100a8|S100A8]]/[[s100a9|S100A9]] proteins and AGE accumulation, which directly promote tumor cell invasion and metastatic colonization.² Mechanistically, circulating cytokines and [[s100a8|S100A8]]/[[s100a9|S100A9]] from aged hosts activate RAGE on tumor cells, remodeling their transcriptional landscape to favor aggressive properties including enhanced invasion capacity, EMT activation, and migratory potential.² Simultaneously, RAGE activation in the tumor stroma orchestrates inflammatory, angiogenic, and extracellular matrix remodeling programs that prepare the metastatic niche for incoming cancer cells.²

In human patients, elevated expression of the AGER gene—which encodes RAGE—in primary breast tumors strongly predicts poor survival outcomes, with the prognostic effect amplified in older patients.² Furthermore, an aging- and RAGE-associated transcriptional signature enriched in human cancers correlates with shorter overall survival and increased metastatic burden.² These clinical observations establish elevated RAGE expression as a molecular marker of aggressive disease and position RAGE-mediated host aging effects as a critical mechanism driving age-associated increases in cancer mortality.²

## Therapeutic antagonism of RAGE

Given RAGE's central role in both acute stroke injury and age-promoted metastasis, RAGE-targeted therapy represents a promising approach across disease contexts. The RAGE antagonist FPS-ZM1 effectively blocks RAGE-ligand interactions, suppressing both [[diaph1|DIAPH1]]-dependent NET formation in ischemic stroke and [[s100a8|S100A8]]/[[s100a9|S100A9]]-driven tumor cell invasion in aging-associated metastasis.¹² Direct degradation of NETs via DNase I provides complementary mechanistic benefit in acute stroke by physically removing the pathological NET scaffolds that obstruct cerebral microvessels.¹ In the metastatic context, neutralization of circulating RAGE ligands—particularly [[s100a8|S100A8]]/[[s100a9|S100A9]]—suppresses tumor cell invasion and metastatic progression, offering an alternative or complementary strategy to direct RAGE antagonism.² These data collectively support RAGE and its ligands as high-priority targets for precision medicine in older cancer patients and for improving acute stroke outcomes in hyperglycemic individuals.
