**Automated Insulin Delivery (AID)** is a hybrid closed-loop technology that automates basal [[insulin]] infusion in response to continuous glucose monitoring while maintaining user control over mealtime [[insulin]] boluses. Unlike fully closed-loop systems, AID preserves patient agency in meal decisions, substantially reducing clinical and cognitive burden while delivering improved [[glucose]] stability and reduced [[hypoglycemia]] risk across diverse populations. Three commercially available AID systems — the Medtronic 780G, Tandem Control-IQ, and Omnipod 5 — have demonstrated consistent efficacy in real-world settings, with each using proprietary algorithms to automatically adjust basal [[insulin]] delivery based on continuous sensor data while allowing patients to direct meal-related dosing through simplified or fixed bolus strategies.¹

---

## Mechanism

Automated insulin delivery operates on a hybrid closed-loop architecture that balances technological automation with preserved patient autonomy. The system continuously reads [[glucose]] values from a sensor and calculates basal [[insulin]] infusion rates using integrated algorithms, automatically modulating delivery upward during hyperglycemia and suspending infusion during predicted [[hypoglycemia]]. Users retain full control over mealtime bolus decisions, substantially simplifying the mental burden of carbohydrate counting and complex insulin-to-carbohydrate calculations. This design philosophy combines the safety advantages of automated basal control — particularly the reduction of nocturnal [[hypoglycemia]] and overnight glucose excursions — with the practical flexibility of user-initiated meal boluses. The result is a system that addresses both physiological stability and the psychological dimensions of diabetes self-management, reducing therapy burden that historically limited treatment adherence.

---

## Type 1 Diabetes

Real-world comparative analyses demonstrate consistent glycemic control improvements across the three commercially available AID systems in adults with [[type-1-diabetes|type 1 diabetes]]. A multicenter evaluation of the Medtronic 780G, Tandem Control-IQ, and Omnipod 5 found that all three systems achieved comparable reductions in hemoglobin A1C and time in range, with no significant differences in [[hypoglycemia]] burden across platforms.¹ This convergence in real-world outcomes suggests that the fundamental automated basal control architecture—rather than proprietary algorithm variations—is the primary driver of glycemic improvement. The finding provides clinicians and patients with confidence in system interchangeability based on practical considerations such as device form factor, infusion set options, and lifestyle integration rather than efficacy differentials.

---

## Type 2 Diabetes and Simplified Bolusing

The application of AID to [[type-2-diabetes-mellitus|type 2 diabetes]] has been enabled through the recognition that insulin-treated adults with type 2 disease respond effectively to [[meal-bolus-strategy|simplified meal bolus strategies]]. A clinical trial of Control-IQ+ AID in adults with insulin-treated type 2 diabetes demonstrated that simplified bolus approaches—including fixed-dose strategies and meal-independent bolusing—achieved safety and glycemic efficacy equivalent to complex, individualized insulin-to-carbohydrate ratio calculations.² This finding substantially reduces the clinical and cognitive burden associated with [[insulin]] therapy in type 2 diabetes, a population for which treatment complexity has historically limited uptake and adherence. By automating basal control and simplifying mealtime decisions, AID systems enable broader adoption of optimized [[insulin]] therapy in type 2 disease, particularly in patients with lower health literacy or limited access to specialized diabetes education.

---

## Performance in Challenging Clinical Scenarios

The robustness of AID extends to medically complex environments where glycemic stability is difficult to achieve using conventional regimens. During inpatient hospitalization—a setting characterized by irregular meal patterns, acute illness, medication interactions, and frequent therapeutic interventions—AID systems maintain effective glycemic control more reliably than conventional [[insulin]] regimens, reducing both hyperglycemic and [[hypoglycemia]]-related complications.³ Similarly, during Ramadan fasting in individuals with [[type-1-diabetes|type 1 diabetes]], AID systems facilitate successful metabolic control while substantially reducing fear of [[hypoglycemia]], a major psychological barrier to religious observance in this population. Users of AID systems reported significantly lower [[hypoglycemia]] anxiety compared to those on conventional [[insulin]] or multiple daily injection regimens, demonstrating that automated basal control addresses both physiological stability and the psychological dimensions of diabetes management across cultural and religious contexts.⁴

---

## Enhanced Automated Insulin-Glucagon Delivery

Building on the therapeutic success of insulin-only AID, hybrid systems that co-deliver [[insulin]] and [[glucagon]] (automated insulin-glucagon delivery, or AIGD) provide superior glycemic stability during acute physiological stress. In a randomized controlled crossover trial comparing AIGD with standard AID during challenging inpatient scenarios—including acute illness protocols and critical illness simulation—AIGD systems demonstrated more effective [[glucose]] stabilization and reduced severe [[hypoglycemia]] episodes.³ The addition of automated [[glucagon]] counters the inherent limitations of [[insulin]]-only systems during catecholamine surges and metabolic stress, particularly when delayed hepatic glycogenolysis or insulin clearance cannot be compensated by basal adjustments alone. While AIGD systems currently remain in research or early-stage commercial development, they represent the next frontier for maximizing automated diabetes management across all populations and clinical contexts, particularly in hospitalized and critically ill patients.
