**Dravet syndrome (DS)** is a severe, infantile-onset neurodevelopmental disorder caused by loss-of-function variants in [[scn1a]], the gene encoding the α subunit of the voltage-gated sodium channel [[nav1.1]].¹ The disease is characterized by fever-triggered and spontaneous [[seizure|seizures]] emerging in the first year of life, rapid evolution to [[drug-resistant-epilepsy|drug-resistant epilepsy]], progressive cognitive decline, and a notably elevated incidence of [[sudep|sudden unexpected death in epilepsy (SUDEP)]].¹ Current approved treatments—including valproic acid, clobazam, and stiripentol—manage acute seizure frequency but fail to correct the underlying genetic defect, leaving the majority of patients dependent on multimodal antiepileptic drug regimens with limited efficacy and cumulative neurotoxic burden. Recent breakthroughs in [[adenine-base-editing|adenine base editing (ABE)]] have achieved direct genetic correction of SCN1A mutations in living animals, providing proof-of-concept that curative intervention may be within reach.

---

## Genetic basis and molecular pathophysiology

Dravet syndrome follows an autosomal-dominant inheritance pattern, with over 90% of cases arising from de novo mutations in SCN1A.¹ Pathogenic variants span frameshift insertions and deletions, nonsense mutations (such as the recurrent R613X variant), and missense changes that disrupt channel gating—all culminating in reduced sodium channel density or function. [[parvalbumin]]-expressing GABAergic interneurons are disproportionately vulnerable; these inhibitory neurons depend critically on high Nav1.1 expression to generate and sustain the rapid, high-frequency action potentials necessary for feedforward and feedback network inhibition.¹ Loss of this inhibitory drive creates a seizure-prone hyperexcitable state.

Temperature sensitivity—a hallmark of DS—reflects the biophysics of depleted or mutant Nav1.1: elevated temperature accelerates voltage-dependent inactivation of remaining sodium channels and further compromises the excitability threshold of already-vulnerable GABAergic neurons.¹ The result is a bistable network dynamic in which fever tips the balance from homeostatic inhibition toward runaway excitation. Beyond seizures, chronic excitotoxicity and aberrant circuit activity during critical developmental windows underlie the progressive cognitive and behavioral impairments characteristic of the disease.

## Clinical features and therapeutic challenges

Dravet syndrome typically manifests between 6 and 12 months of age with brief, generalized tonic-clonic seizures provoked by fever or, increasingly, occurring spontaneously.¹ Seizure burden accelerates rapidly; many children experience 10–40 monthly episodes despite polypharmacy with multiple antiepileptic agents. The extraordinary refractoriness of DS to conventional anticonvulsants—attributed to progressive GABA receptor downregulation, altered inhibitory plasticity, and inherent circuit-level dysfunction—necessitates aggressive combination therapy and carries substantial cumulative risk of hepatotoxicity, metabolic derangement, and neurodevelopmental compromise.

Cognitive decline is nearly universal, with most patients developing moderate-to-severe intellectual disability and delays in language, motor, and social development.¹ Behavioral disturbances, autistic features, and sleep disorders are frequent comorbidities. Most critically, [[sudep|SUDEP]] incidence in Dravet syndrome is 15–20 times higher than in the general epilepsy population, making it the leading cause of epilepsy-related death in children with this diagnosis.¹ This convergence of intractable seizures, progressive neurological injury, and life-threatening mortality underscores the urgent need for disease-modifying therapy.

## Adenine base editing: Proof-of-concept for genetic correction

[[adenine-base-editing|Adenine base editors (ABEs)]] catalyze direct, irreversible adenine-to-guanine transitions (A•T to G•C) without generating double-strand breaks or requiring exogenous DNA templates, offering a clean path to correcting point mutations. Researchers optimized ABE variants to correct the recurrent SCN1AR613X nonsense mutation—a common DS-causing variant—in engineered cellular models.¹ In human embryonic kidney 293T cells, optimized ABE systems achieved 72% correction efficiency; in mouse Neuro-2a neurons, efficiency reached 92%, with restored synthesis of full-length, functional Nav1.1 protein.¹

To achieve correction in intact neural tissue, dual-[[aav9|adeno-associated virus serotype 9 (AAV9)]] vectors were employed, partitioning the ABE coding sequence and guide RNA across separate particles to circumvent AAV packaging constraints.¹ Neonatal intravenous administration of dual-AAV9-ABE to Scn1aR613X/+ mice—a model that recapitulates DS seizures, fever sensitivity, and cognitive deficits—yielded 59% DNA editing and 97% mRNA editing efficiency across bulk neocortices.¹ Functionally, base editing restored [[parvalbumin]]-positive inhibitory neurons to wild-type excitability and sodium current kinetics, reinstituting their capacity for high-frequency firing and network inhibition.¹

The therapeutic phenotype was striking: ABE treatment virtually eliminated spontaneous seizures and temperature-provoked seizures, and survival at 45 days post-treatment improved 3.3-fold (90% ABE-treated versus 27% vehicle control).¹ Treatment initiated at 12 days of age—a more clinically relevant timepoint after seizure onset—similarly improved 60-day survival approximately 3-fold.¹ These results establish adenine base editing as a viable in vivo approach to correcting SCN1A deficiency and ameliorating core DS pathologies.

## Path to clinical translation

Translating ABE-mediated SCN1A correction to human Dravet syndrome patients presents both promise and challenges. Engineered ABE variants with improved specificity and reduced off-target adenine deamination are in development. Delivery optimization—potentially via intrathecal or intraventricular AAV9 infusion to maximize brain penetrance, or engineering of blood-brain barrier-crossing capsids—will be essential for clinical efficacy.

The genetic heterogeneity of SCN1A (over 1000 variants catalogued) means that patient cohorts may require tailored ABE designs or variant-specific guide RNAs. Alternatively, development of ABE systems targeting common SCN1A hotspots or complementary prime editing strategies could broaden applicability across frameshift and insertion-deletion mutations. The potential for one-time genetic correction to arrest or reverse disease progression offers genuine hope to patients and families facing this devastating diagnosis. Should clinical trials confirm safety and efficacy, ABE-based correction could become the first curative treatment for Dravet syndrome.
