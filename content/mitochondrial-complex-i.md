**Mitochondrial Complex I** (also known as NADH dehydrogenase) is the first multiprotein enzyme complex of the electron transport chain, catalyzing the oxidation of NADH to NAD⁺ while transferring electrons to ubiquinone and pumping protons across the inner mitochondrial membrane.¹ This proton gradient powers ATP synthase, making Complex I the primary driver of oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) and cellular energy production.¹ The complex is dynamically regulated through protein-protein interactions, including binding to the intercellular adhesion molecule [[icam2|ICAM2]], which recruits dynein motors to facilitate mitochondrial trafficking and modulate Complex I assembly, thereby controlling OXPHOS capacity in response to metabolic demand and stress.¹

---

## Mechanism and assembly regulation

Complex I oxidizes NADH at its flavin mononucleotide prosthetic group, transferring electrons through an iron-sulfur cluster chain to ubiquinone in the lipid bilayer. This redox reaction is coupled to proton pumping via a mechanism involving quinone binding and conformational changes across the hydrophobic arm of the complex. Assembly of the mature 45-subunit enzyme requires coordinated incorporation of seven iron-sulfur clusters and one flavin cofactor, steps that are facilitated by specialized chaperones and quality control mechanisms within the mitochondrial matrix.¹

The assembly process is dynamically regulated by [[icam2|ICAM2]], which binds the dynein light chain [[dynlt3|DYNLT3]] and simultaneously engages the mitochondrial Complex I subunit [[mt-nd2|MT-ND2]].¹ This interaction recruits cytoplasmic dynein motors to mitochondria, enabling ATP-dependent transport of mitochondrial precursors and assembly intermediates along microtubule networks. Through this dynein-mediated trafficking mechanism, ICAM2 modulates the efficiency of Complex I assembly and oligomeric state, thereby controlling the maximum OXPHOS capacity of the cell. ICAM2 expression is transcriptionally repressed by estrogen receptor alpha (ERα), and therefore relief of this repression — or upregulation through alternative signals in resistant tumors — elevates OXPHOS output.

## Role in estrogen receptor–positive breast cancer endocrine resistance

Acquired endocrine resistance in [[estrogen-receptor-positive-breast-cancer|ER+ breast cancer]] is driven in part by metabolic reprogramming toward elevated OXPHOS, reducing dependence on estrogen signaling for energy production. Multi-omics analysis of endocrine-resistant breast cancers revealed that ICAM2-positive cells represent a distinct metabolic state characterized by high oxidative phosphorylation capacity.¹ These ICAM2-positive, high-OXPHOS cells were significantly enriched in treatment-resistant tumors compared to sensitive counterparts and independently predicted poor overall survival. Functionally, ICAM2 expression was essential for maintaining endocrine resistance in vivo; knockdown of ICAM2 in resistant xenografts suppressed OXPHOS activity and restored responsiveness to fulvestrant, the selective estrogen receptor degrader.¹ 

Mechanistically, elevated ICAM2 drives the assembly and activation of mitochondrial Complex I through dynein-dependent trafficking of mitochondrial precursor proteins and catalytic subunits. By elevating Complex I activity, ICAM2-positive cells generate sufficient ATP to sustain proliferation independently of estrogen receptor signaling, while simultaneously supporting the synthesis of biosynthetic precursors through active tricarboxylic acid cycle cycling. These metabolically reprogrammed cells also display reduced mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (ROS) owing to enhanced antioxidant buffering capacity downstream of elevated OXPHOS, further promoting survival under therapeutic stress.

## Therapeutic targeting and ferroptosis sensitization

Complex I represents a critical metabolic vulnerability in endocrine-resistant [[estrogen-receptor-positive-breast-cancer|ER+ breast cancer]]. The selective Complex I inhibitor [[iacs-10759|IACS-10759]] potently suppresses the elevated OXPHOS activity of resistant cells, and when combined with fulvestrant, achieves synergistic suppression of tumor growth and metastatic progression in vivo.¹ This combination strategy exploits a synthetic lethal interaction: IACS-10759 forces endocrine-resistant cells back into a state of estrogen dependence by eliminating their metabolic escape route.

Metformin, a widely used antidiabetic agent, similarly inhibits Complex I through direct binding to its ubiquinone-binding pocket, thereby suppressing OXPHOS-driven energy production.² Importantly, Complex I inhibition by metformin alleviates tumor hypoxia by reducing intracellular oxygen consumption, paradoxically creating a normoxic microenvironment that enhances the activity of enzymes dependent on oxygen availability.² Within this oxygen-replete setting, exogenous hydrogen peroxide or lactate oxidase (which generates H₂O₂ from lactate) can effectively drive iron-dependent lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis, a mode of cell death that is otherwise suppressed in hypoxic tumors.

Simultaneously, metformin suppresses the cystine/glutamate antiporter [[slc7a11|SLC7A11]] (xCT), thereby reducing cystine import and depleting the intracellular pool of glutathione (GSH).² Without adequate GSH, the glutathione peroxidase [[gpx4|GPX4]] cannot detoxify lipid hydroperoxides, and ferroptotic cell death proceeds unimpeded.² In this manner, Complex I inhibition converges on ferroptosis through dual mechanisms: relieving hypoxia-mediated suppression of lipid peroxidation and simultaneously disarming the GSH-GPX4 antioxidant axis that would otherwise survive the ferroptotic trigger.
