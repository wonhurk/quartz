**Interleukin-17 (IL-17)** is a proinflammatory cytokine with critical roles in immune regulation that becomes pathologically dysregulated during [[immune-checkpoint-blockade|immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI)]] therapy for cancer. In patients receiving checkpoint inhibitors, IL-17 is aberrantly produced at high levels by a unique subset of [[treg|regulatory T cells]] (AtypTregs) that co-express [[cd137|CD137]] and [[il-6r|IL-6 receptor]], driving systemic inflammation and [[ici-ia|immune checkpoint inhibitor-induced inflammatory arthritis (ICI-IA)]].¹ These IL-17-producing cells are remarkable for exhibiting paradoxically reduced immunosuppressive capacity despite their [[treg|Treg]] lineage identity while simultaneously promoting proinflammatory responses in synovial tissue. The frequency of IL-17-producing AtypTregs directly correlates with disease severity, yet these same cells paradoxically associate with improved overall survival in cancer patients, highlighting a complex immunological paradox at the intersection of anti-tumor immunity and autoimmunity. [[il-6r|IL-6 receptor]] blockade effectively reduces IL-17-producing AtypTregs and ameliorates ICI-IA without compromising anti-tumor immunity, establishing the IL-17 pathway as a mechanism-based therapeutic target.

---

## Mechanism

IL-17 functions as a central proinflammatory cytokine that drives tissue inflammation through activation of resident and infiltrating immune cells. In the context of checkpoint inhibitor therapy, IL-17 signaling from atypical T regulatory cells amplifies synovial inflammation by directly activating synoviocytes and promoting recruitment of inflammatory effector populations to the joint microenvironment. The IL-17 pathway operates through canonical intracellular signaling cascades that synergize with other proinflammatory mediators to establish and perpetuate local tissue inflammation.

---

## Pathogenic role in immune checkpoint inhibitor-induced arthritis

IL-17 emerged as a critical pathogenic driver of ICI-IA through comprehensive single-cell multi-omics profiling of paired peripheral blood, synovial fluid, and tumor tissue from patients developing arthritis during checkpoint inhibitor treatment.¹ A distinct population of regulatory T cells co-expressing CD137 and IL-6R (AtypTregs) aberrantly produce high levels of IL-17, which directly promotes proinflammatory responses in the synovial microenvironment. These IL-17-producing AtypTregs are distributed across tissue compartments—present in inflamed synovial fluid, peripheral blood, and tumor tissue—while maintaining shared clonotypes and phenotypic signatures, suggesting systemic activation and trafficking during cancer immunotherapy.¹ Importantly, the frequency of IL-17-producing AtypTregs in patients directly correlates with increased arthritis severity, establishing IL-17 production as both a pathogenic mechanism and a quantitative biomarker of disease burden.¹

---

## Immunosuppressive dysfunction in atypical Tregs

Despite their [[treg|Treg]] origin and CD25⁺ phenotype, IL-17-producing AtypTregs exhibit aberrantly reduced immunosuppressive capacity compared to conventional [[treg|regulatory T cell]] populations.¹ This paradoxical loss of immune-suppressive function coupled with enhanced proinflammatory cytokine production suggests that IL-17 expression marks a functional transformation of [[treg|Tregs]] from immune-suppressive to proinflammatory effector states. The co-expression of CD137 and IL-6R identifies this unique pathogenic subset, distinguishing these cells from both classical [[treg|Tregs]] and other CD137-expressing T-cell populations engaged in anti-tumor immunity.

---

## Paradoxical association with improved survival

A striking clinical paradox emerged from the integrated analysis of ICI-IA cohorts: despite driving local joint inflammation and systemic inflammatory arthritis, IL-17-producing AtypTregs paradoxically associate with improved overall survival in cancer patients treated with checkpoint inhibitors.¹ This counterintuitive finding suggests that the mechanisms generating joint autoimmunity may be intimately linked to heightened anti-tumor immune activation. The mechanistic basis for this survival advantage remains to be fully elucidated but likely reflects increased T-cell activation state, enhanced tumor antigen recognition, and systemic inflammatory signaling that confers therapeutic benefit against malignancy while incidentally precipitating arthritis as an immune-related adverse event.

---

## Therapeutic targeting via IL-6 receptor blockade

[[il-6r|IL-6 receptor]] blockade emerged as a targeted therapeutic strategy for ICI-IA by selectively reducing IL-17-producing AtypTreg populations.¹ Anti-IL-6R monoclonal antibody therapy substantially decreased both the frequency and absolute numbers of IL-17-producing AtypTregs and correspondingly reduced circulating IL-17 levels, resulting in improved arthritis outcomes, reduced synovial inflammation, and enhanced quality of life in treated patients.¹ Critically, IL-6R blockade did not compromise the anti-tumor immune response, maintaining checkpoint inhibitor efficacy and overall survival while mitigating immune-related adverse events.¹ This selective therapeutic approach demonstrates that IL-6 signaling is specifically required for maintaining the proinflammatory Treg state, identifying IL-6R as a precision target for ICI-associated toxicity management without sacrificing therapeutic benefit.

---

## Autoantibody-mediated IL-17 pathway regulation

Beyond cell-intrinsic IL-17 production, humoral immune responses targeting the IL-17 pathway have been associated with enhanced response to checkpoint inhibitor therapy. In longitudinal analyses of ICB-treated patients, individuals who developed autoantibodies against IL-17 pathway proteins showed improved therapeutic responses, particularly when such antibodies possessed neutralizing capacity against their respective targets.² This autoantibody-mediated suppression of IL-17 signaling represents a complementary humoral mechanism by which IL-17 pathway inhibition may facilitate anti-tumor immunity during cancer immunotherapy, suggesting that both adaptive cellular and humoral responses contribute to IL-17 pathway regulation during checkpoint blockade.
