**Collagen** is a fibrous structural protein constituting the primary architectural scaffold of the [[extracellular-matrix|extracellular matrix (ECM)]], providing mechanical integrity to tissues while orchestrating cell-matrix signaling through integrin receptors. Its fibrillar networks transmit biomechanical cues and regulate transcriptional programs governing cell survival, proliferation, and invasive behavior.¹ The enzyme [[lox|lysyl oxidase (LOX)]] catalyzes collagen crosslinking, stabilizing fibrillar architecture and generating matrix environments that modulate disease progression in contexts ranging from cancer metastasis to chronic tissue injury.¹ Collagen serves dual roles as both a therapeutic target in collagen-dependent malignancies and a biomaterial foundation for regenerative medicine applications including three-dimensional bioprinting and scaffold-based tissue engineering.²

---

## Structural properties and regulation

Collagen forms supramolecular fibrillar assemblies through hierarchical self-organization, creating networks whose density, alignment, and crosslinking status dictate tissue mechanical properties and cellular responses. [[lox|Lysyl oxidase]] family enzymes catalyze the oxidative deamination of lysine residues, generating reactive aldehydes that spontaneously condense to form covalent crosslinks stabilizing collagen fibrils.¹ These crosslinks stiffen the matrix, increase fiber alignment, and enhance resistance to proteolytic degradation. Collagen fibers interact with cell-surface integrin heterodimers—most notably [[itgav|αVβ5 (ITGAV/ITGB5)]]—transducing mechanical signals into intracellular cascades that regulate [[myc|MYC]], [[nf-kb|NF-κB]], and [[ap-1|AP-1]] transcriptional programs.¹ Fiber density and alignment are quantifiable structural parameters that serve as pharmacodynamic biomarkers of therapeutic interventions targeting ECM-dependent pathways.

## Cancer biology: invasive lobular carcinoma

Collagen-rich stromal matrices are essential for the progression of [[invasive-lobular-carcinoma|invasive lobular carcinoma (ILC)]], a breast cancer subtype characterized by loss of [[cdh1|E-cadherin (CDH1)]] and preferential growth in fibrotic microenvironments. Genome-wide CRISPR screens identified [[itgav|ITGAV]] and [[itgb5|ITGB5]] as synthetic lethal partners of CDH1, revealing that ILC cells depend on collagen-integrin signaling to compensate for lost cell-cell adhesion.¹ This dependency creates a therapeutic vulnerability exploitable by [[lox|LOX]] inhibition. The clinical pan-LOX inhibitor [[pxs-5505|PXS-5505]], initially developed for myelofibrosis, remodels fibrillar collagen architecture in preclinical ILC models, reducing fiber density and alignment while suppressing both primary tumor expansion and metastatic seeding across estrogen receptor-positive and triple-negative subtypes.¹ PXS-5505 treatment downregulates [[itgav|ITGAV]] and [[itgb5|ITGB5]] expression, dismantles [[myc|MYC]], [[nf-kb|NF-κB]], and [[ap-1|AP-1]] transcriptional networks, and induces tumor regression without systemic toxicity, nominating this collagen-targeted strategy for window-of-opportunity clinical trials in ILC, alone or combined with endocrine therapy.¹

## Tissue engineering and bioprinting

Decellularized tissue-derived collagen matrices retain native ECM composition and ultrastructure, enabling fabrication of tissue-specific bioinks for three-dimensional bioprinting of functional constructs. Airway mucosa-derived ECM (MudECM) preserves collagen alongside glycosaminoglycans through optimized decellularization protocols achieving greater than 98% cellular material removal while maintaining bioactivity.² When processed into thermo-responsive bioinks, MudECM exhibits shear-thinning rheology and thermal gelation properties compatible with extrusion-based bioprinting platforms.² Human tracheal epithelial cells cultured on MudECM hydrogels under air-liquid interface conditions display enhanced mucociliary differentiation, tight junction formation, goblet cell maturation, and directional mucus transport compared to type I collagen controls, demonstrating that tissue-specific collagen matrices encode instructive signals for lineage-appropriate differentiation.² Bioprinted bilayered airway constructs incorporating MudECM support epithelial-stromal organization in vitro and promote functional tissue regeneration when implanted into rat tracheal defect models, establishing collagen-based bioinks as viable platforms for reconstructing complex mucosal tissues.²

## Non-cancer therapeutic applications

Beyond oncology, engineered collagen scaffolds address unmet needs in wound healing and tissue regeneration. In diabetic wound healing, where excessive [[reactive-oxygen-species|reactive oxygen species (ROS)]] and persistent inflammation impair tissue repair, collagen-containing composite hydrogels provide structural support while delivering therapeutic cargo.⁴ Injectable systems combining methacrylated silk fibroin with pH-responsive zinc sulfide nanoparticles form porous, shear-thinning gels that restore [[fibroblasts|fibroblast]] and endothelial cell function under oxidative stress, polarize macrophages toward reparative M2 phenotypes, and accelerate wound closure with enhanced re-epithelialization in streptozotocin-induced diabetic mouse models.⁴ Multilayered electrospun membranes incorporating drug-loaded nanofibers coordinate sequential release of antibacterial and anti-inflammatory agents while absorbing excess tissue exudate through capillary transport mechanisms, extending therapeutic action from hours to days.⁵ These platforms leverage collagen's biocompatibility and biodegradability to create temporary scaffolds that guide endogenous tissue regeneration before resorbing.

## Novel scaffold engineering approaches

Advanced fabrication techniques enable precise control over collagen scaffold architecture to optimize regenerative performance. Dual-frequency ultrasound strategies using phase-shift droplets as cavitation nuclei create tunable porosity within collagen hydrogels through acoustic droplet vaporization and controlled bubble implosion.³ Micron-sized [[perfluoropentane|perfluoropentane]] droplets stabilized by phospholipid shells are embedded in collagen matrices and activated sequentially by 3.5 MHz imaging ultrasound (triggering vaporization into microbubbles) followed by 200 kHz therapeutic ultrasound (inducing oscillation and collapse), generating localized pores at reduced pressure thresholds compared to conventional methods.³ This noninvasive approach yields scaffolds with 56.53 ± 3.91% porosity and mean pore diameters of 39.42 ± 10.28 μm, dimensions validated by scanning electron microscopy and predicted by Marmottant modeling of microbubble dynamics.³ Ultrasound-treated collagen scaffolds exhibit enhanced elasticity and interconnected pore networks that facilitate cell infiltration, nutrient diffusion, and vascular ingrowth, demonstrating how physical modification of collagen architecture can be spatially and temporally controlled without chemical crosslinkers or porogens that may compromise biocompatibility.³
