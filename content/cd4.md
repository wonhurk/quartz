**CD4** is a cell surface glycoprotein expressed on helper T cells that serves as the primary marker for assessing immunological status, particularly in HIV infection where CD4+ T-cell counts guide treatment decisions and predict immune reconstitution. As a co-receptor for the [[t-cell-receptor|T-cell receptor (TCR)]], CD4 plays a central role in adaptive immunity by stabilizing interactions between T cells and antigen-presenting cells. The protein functions in dual therapeutic contexts: monoclonal anti-CD4 antibodies suppress immune activation in transplantation, while anti-CD4 autoantibodies emerge as pathogenic factors that impair immune recovery in treated HIV infection despite effective viral suppression.¹²

---

## HIV immune monitoring

In people with HIV, CD4+ T-cell counts constitute the definitive measure of immunological status and antiretroviral therapy efficacy. Prior to the adoption of universal treatment policies, CD4 thresholds determined eligibility for antiretroviral therapy initiation, particularly in vulnerable populations such as pregnant women where the [[option-b-plus|Option B+]] policy ultimately extended lifelong therapy to all regardless of CD4 count.⁵ CD4+ T-cell enumeration remains the gold standard for monitoring immune reconstitution on suppressive antiretroviral therapy, though paradoxically, a subset of virologically suppressed patients experience persistently poor CD4 recovery driven by [[anti-cd4-igg|anti-CD4 autoantibodies]].²

---

## Anti-CD4 autoimmunity and bacterial translocation

Anti-CD4 autoantibodies represent a pathogenic mechanism of impaired immune reconstitution in HIV-treated patients who achieve virological suppression yet fail to recover normal CD4+ T-cell counts. Recent evidence establishes that systemic translocation of Staphylococcus aureus and its peptidoglycan drives anti-CD4 IgG production, creating a bacteria-mediated autoimmune loop that persists despite antiretroviral therapy.² Plasma levels of anti-CD4 IgG correlate inversely with CD4+ T-cell counts and positively with markers of S. aureus translocation in treated patients. In experimental models, S. aureus peptidoglycan induces anti-CD4 autoantibodies, reduces the frequency of CD4+ T cells among gut T-cell populations, and promotes surface IgG binding and apoptosis of CD4+ T cells.² This bacterial-driven autoimmunity mechanism distinguishes itself from the broader spectrum of autoantibodies observed in antiretroviral-naive patients, most of which normalize following therapy initiation.²

## Therapeutic anti-CD4 antibodies in transplantation

Monoclonal anti-CD4 antibodies have emerged as a targeted immunotherapy for preventing [[graft-versus-host-disease|graft-versus-host disease (GVHD)]] in allogeneic hematopoietic cell transplantation. The humanized antibody [[max-16h5|MAX.16H5]] enables potent GVHD prophylaxis through a single ex vivo treatment of donor transplants, circumventing the systemic toxicity, opportunistic infections, and cancer relapse risks associated with conventional immunosuppressants.¹ Mechanistically, MAX.16H5 treatment impairs [[t-cell-receptor|T-cell receptor]] signaling and shifts T-cell populations toward an interleukin-10-dependent regulatory phenotype characterized by increased CD4⁺CD25⁺ regulatory T cells (Tregs) and suppressed activation markers.¹³ This functional reprogramming reduces activation and proliferation of both CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cells, producing sustained GVHD protection comparable to repeated clinical immunosuppressant administration but achieved through a single pretransplant incubation.¹ The regulatory T-cell bias induced by anti-CD4 treatment restores CD25 expression and promotes immune tolerance without requiring ongoing pharmacological intervention.³

---

## Immune monitoring beyond HIV

CD4+ T-cell absolute counts serve as biomarkers of immune function recovery across diverse immunosuppressive conditions. In sepsis, where immunosuppression frequently drives treatment failure through impaired host defense, selenium nanoparticle adjunctive therapy has demonstrated efficacy in restoring lymphocyte populations, with particular improvements in CD3⁺, CD4⁺, and CD8⁺ T-cell subset counts observed by days four through ten of treatment.⁴ These lymphocyte reconstitution dynamics correlate with reduced pro-inflammatory cytokine levels, improved organ function, and decreased intensive care unit mortality, positioning CD4 enumeration as a functional readout of immune restoration in critical illness.⁴ Beyond acute infection, CD4⁺CD25⁺ regulatory T-cell proportions represent a key parameter in autoimmune disease susceptibility, with maternal environmental factors during fetal development influencing Treg frequencies and autoantibody patterns that shape subsequent diabetes incidence.³
