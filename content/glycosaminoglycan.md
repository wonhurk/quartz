**Glycosaminoglycans (GAGs)** are essential linear polysaccharides of the extracellular matrix with critical regulatory roles in skeletal development, tissue homeostasis, and regenerative medicine. [[heparan-sulfate|Heparan sulfate]], the predominant GAG variant, is synthesized through sequential enzymatic addition by the glycosyltransferases [[ext1]] and [[ext2]].¹ These molecules function as structural scaffolds and signaling co-factors, influencing chondrocyte differentiation and tissue organization. Their pathological dysregulation drives hereditary multiple osteochondromas through a gene dosage-dependent mechanism, while their preservation in decellularized tissue matrices enables advanced applications in three-dimensional bioprinting and functional tissue regeneration.

---

## Mechanism and synthesis

Glycosaminoglycans are synthesized through stepwise enzymatic extension catalyzed by [[ext1]] and [[ext2]], which sequentially add monosaccharide units to form heparan sulfate chains. In normal skeletal development, heparan sulfate biosynthesis orchestrates the balance between chondrocyte proliferation and terminal maturation within developing growth plates, serving as a critical regulator of differentiation signaling.¹ The total GAG content within cells comprises multiple chemical species, with heparan sulfate constituting a major fraction under physiological conditions. These molecules act bidirectionally, providing structural support while simultaneously functioning as regulatory ligands for developmental signaling pathways.

## Osteochondroma pathogenesis and gene dosage requirement

The relationship between GAG dysmetabolism and hereditary multiple osteochondromas has been clarified through patient-derived cellular models employing CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing in induced pluripotent stem cells. Isogenic comparisons of wild-type, single-mutant, and double-mutant cell lines revealed a critical gene dosage effect: heterozygous [[ext1]] mutations in single-mutant cells produced no detectable phenotypic alterations in GAG metabolism or chondrocytic gene expression, demonstrating that haploinsufficiency alone is insufficient for disease pathogenesis.¹ 

Biallelic inactivation of [[ext1]] in double-mutant cells produced a striking metabolic paradox — total glycosaminoglycan levels increased substantially while heparan sulfate content decreased markedly.¹ This dysregulation was coupled with profound upregulation of chondrocytic transcription factors SOX9, COL2A1, and ACAN, coupled with abnormal chondrocyte differentiation in three-dimensional organoid cultures. These organoids exhibited enlargement and structural disorganization that partially recapitulated the histopathological hallmarks of osteochondromas. Transcriptomic profiling identified [[wnt-signaling|Wnt signaling]] as the most significantly enriched pathway among differentially expressed genes following biallelic [[ext1]] loss, establishing a mechanistic link between aberrant GAG metabolism and dysregulated developmental signaling that drives neoplastic chondrocytic growth.¹

## Tissue engineering and regenerative applications

Glycosaminoglycans are preserved during decellularization of airway mucosa and remain functionally active components of advanced bioinks for three-dimensional bioprinting. When processed into hydrogel form, airway mucosa-derived extracellular matrix retains glycosaminoglycans and collagen while achieving greater than 98% removal of cellular material.² These preserved GAGs critically contribute to the rheological properties of the resulting bioink, imparting shear-thinning viscosity and thermoresponsive gelation characteristics essential for precise three-dimensional printing of anatomically complex airway structures.²

Tissue-specific GAGs within decellularized airway mucosa matrices actively promote epithelial differentiation and mucociliary function. Human tracheal epithelial cells cultured on GAG-rich airway extracellular matrix hydrogels showed enhanced mucociliary differentiation, tight junction formation, goblet cell development, and directional mucus transport relative to collagen-only controls, demonstrating the biological relevance of matrix-resident glycosaminoglycans.² Bilayered airway constructs fabricated by three-dimensional bioprinting with GAG-containing decellularized extracellular matrix bioinks supported robust fibroblast viability and stromal organization in vitro. When implanted into rat tracheal defect models, these constructs demonstrated functional tissue regeneration and restoration of airway epithelial architecture in vivo, validating the therapeutic potential of GAG-enriched biomaterials for regenerative medicine applications requiring preservation of tissue-specific matrix properties and complex three-dimensional geometries.²
