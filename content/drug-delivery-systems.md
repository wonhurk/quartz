**Drug delivery systems** (DDS) are advanced pharmaceutical technologies engineered to optimize the temporal and spatial release of therapeutic agents, decoupling drug absorption from passive dissolution. Modern DDS platforms encompass multiple complementary strategies—from dual-responsive [[cyclodextrin]] nanocarriers to personalized three-dimensional printed capsules to long-acting intramuscular suspensions—collectively enabling precise pharmacokinetic control, improved patient compliance, and the clinical foundation for precision medicine.

---

## Mechanism

Controlled drug release operates through the principle of stimulus-response or geometric design, wherein the carrier matrix or structure itself regulates when and where a payload becomes bioavailable. In [[cyclodextrin]]-based systems, inclusion complex formation stabilizes the drug while remaining responsive to physiological triggers. In [[polyvinyl-alcohol]] printed matrices, shell geometry and infill density directly modulate dissolution kinetics through engineering of porosity and polymer surface area. In intramuscular suspensions, particle size and stabilizer adsorption govern the rate of dissolution at the injection depot, controlling the window over which drug becomes available for absorption. Collectively, these mechanisms replace the uncontrolled kinetics of conventional tablets or solutions with tailored release profiles that match therapeutic needs.

## Stimuli-responsive nanocarriers

Dual-responsive nanocarriers exemplify the sophistication achievable through rational carrier design. [[Cyclodextrin]]/cholesteryl oleate nanoparticles self-assemble into core-shell architectures in which a crystalline cholesteryl oleate core is surrounded by multiple nanosheet shells of inclusion complex crystals.¹ When heated above 55°C—a physiologically plausible temperature in postprandial fed-state conditions—the core melts into liquid-phase nanodroplets (100–200 nm) while the nanosheet shells dissociate from the nanoparticle interface.¹ Alternatively, exposure to fed-state simulated intestinal fluid triggers dissociation of the nanosheets through bile salt interactions, while the crystalline cholesteryl oleate core surprisingly retains its structural integrity.¹ This dual environmental responsiveness allows rational exploitation of gastrointestinal physiology: the thermal trigger responds to postprandial temperature elevation, whilst the bile trigger responds to lipid-rich meal ingestion, enabling intelligent release synchronized with absorptive capacity.

## 3D-printed personalized formulations

Three-dimensional printing via [[fused-deposition-modeling]] (FDM) has emerged as a transformative manufacturing paradigm for personalized oral drug delivery. [[Polyvinyl-alcohol]] capsule shells engineered with systematically varied geometry and internal infill density achieve tunable immediate-, sustained-, and delayed-release profiles.² Low infill density and thin shells accelerate drug liberation into the gastrointestinal lumen, whereas higher infill density and thicker shells extend release over hours.² The approach extends beyond monotherapy: dual-compartment capsules have been successfully fabricated to codeliver [[ketoprofen]] and [[famotidine]] in a single personalized polypill, enabling combination therapy with independently controlled release profiles.² In silico pharmacokinetic modeling guides capsule specifications before fabrication, predicting distinct plasma concentration curves for immediate-release versus sustained-release designs.² Dissolution testing, micro-computed tomography, and solid-state analysis confirm that the designed porosity variations translate into controlled drug-polymer interactions, with amorphous solid dispersions supporting the computational predictions and validating the rational design approach.

## Long-acting intramuscular suspensions

Extended-duration depot formulations achieve prolonged systemic exposure through optimization of particle size and stabilizer chemistry. [[Vortioxetine]] pamoate suspensions stabilized with [[poloxamer]] 338 exemplify this platform: 15% drug loading with 3% poloxamer yields optimal injection properties, including suitable viscosity (10–13 cP) and moderate injection force (8–24 N).³ Particle size proves critically important; 2.5 μm vortioxetine pamoate particles achieve extended half-lives of 188 hours in rats and 39.6 hours in dogs following intramuscular administration—a dramatic increase over 2.8 hours and 8.3 hours via oral administration, respectively.³ Burst release is minimized (10.2% in rats, 3.8% in dogs), enabling slow and steady drug absorption from the intramuscular depot over weeks. Systematic optimization of drug loading, milling bead parameters, and mechanical processing conditions via Planetary Ball Mill yields the target particle size distribution, demonstrating that pharmaceutical engineering principles directly translate suspension performance into extended clinical dosing intervals and improved therapeutic adherence.
