**Minimal residual disease (MRD)** represents small populations of cancer cells persisting after treatment that are undetectable by conventional clinical assessment yet identifiable through ultrasensitive molecular assays. The emergence of circulating tumor-DNA (ctDNA) detection has revolutionized MRD monitoring, enabling non-invasive assessment of disease burden with superior prognostic power compared to traditional pathologic response evaluation.¹ In [[her2]]-positive and [[tnbc|triple-negative breast cancer]] undergoing neoadjuvant therapy, ctDNA-based MRD assessment identifies patients with cryptic residual disease despite apparent clinical remission, enabling improved risk stratification and personalized treatment planning.¹ The technology fundamentally reshapes how clinicians interpret treatment response across cancer types, shifting from anatomic assessment to direct molecular quantification of tumor-derived nucleic acids in blood.

---

## Detection and Assessment

Minimal residual disease is operationally defined as cancer cell populations remaining after primary therapy that escape conventional clinical detection methods—including physical examination, imaging, and serum tumor markers—yet persist at levels sufficient to compromise long-term survival.¹ The development of circulating tumor-DNA assays has transformed MRD detection by enabling direct molecular quantification of tumor-derived nucleic acids in patient plasma. These tissue-free, epigenomic assays leverage ultrasensitive detection platforms capable of identifying disease burden orders of magnitude smaller than conventional histopathology, fundamentally shifting from anatomic response assessment to quantitative molecular disease monitoring.

The biological basis for ctDNA detection lies in the continuous release of tumor cell-derived DNA fragments into the bloodstream during cell turnover, apoptosis, and necrosis. Because ctDNA carries somatic mutations and epigenetic alterations unique to each patient's malignancy, its detection provides direct molecular evidence of cancer cell persistence. This approach overcomes critical limitations of pathologic response assessment, which relies on tissue sampling and may overlook small disease-burdened populations in patients with otherwise favorable imaging and clinical findings.

## Breast Cancer Applications

In stage II/III [[her2]]-positive and [[tnbc]] patients receiving neoadjuvant therapy, ctDNA-based MRD assessment provides prognostic information exceeding pathologic complete response (pCR) evaluation alone.¹ Although pCR correlates with improved long-term outcomes, many patients achieving less than pCR nevertheless experience long-term disease-free survival, revealing the imprecision of pathologic response as a sole risk stratification marker.¹ Conversely, ctDNA detection identifies a subset of patients with apparently favorable pathologic responses who harbor persistent molecular disease—a population at substantially higher recurrence risk.¹ This complementary information enables clinicians to identify patients with "hidden disease" masked by reduced residual tumor cellularity and intensify adjuvant strategies accordingly.

The superior risk stratification provided by ctDNA-based MRD creates actionable opportunities for treatment personalization. Patients with detectable ctDNA despite clinical remission represent a cohort potentially benefiting from adjuvant intensification, while those with rapid ctDNA clearance may avoid unnecessary toxicity from standard regimens. This molecular-driven approach improves outcomes in currently underserved populations with non-pCR disease and favorable long-term prognosis.

## Colorectal Cancer Applications

In stage III colorectal cancer patients, tissue-free epigenomic ctDNA assays demonstrate significant prognostic value for postoperative risk stratification in adjuvant chemotherapy trials.² A phase III trial evaluating FOLFOX-based adjuvant therapy established that ctDNA detection after surgery identifies patients at exceptionally high recurrence risk despite conventional staging criteria.² This postoperative molecular assessment enables clinicians to distinguish patients requiring treatment intensification or enhanced surveillance from those likely to be cured by standard chemotherapy alone.

The timing of ctDNA-based MRD assessment—immediately after surgical resection and before or during adjuvant therapy—provides a critical window for treatment optimization. Persistent ctDNA despite adjuvant chemotherapy indicates exceptional recurrence risk, whereas rapid ctDNA clearance correlates with favorable prognosis. This molecular information, unavailable through conventional clinicopathologic assessment, fundamentally reshapes perioperative decision-making and enables better-informed counseling regarding treatment intensity, surveillance strategy, and long-term outcome prediction.

## Chronic Lymphocytic Leukemia and Targeted Therapy Resistance

Despite revolutionary advances in targeted therapy, Bruton tyrosine kinase inhibitors—including [[ibrutinib]]—frequently fail to achieve complete remission or undetectable MRD in [[cll|chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL)]] patients.³ Monotherapy with ibrutinib does not reliably induce undetectable MRD even with extended treatment duration, highlighting a fundamental challenge in cancer medicine: the persistence of residual disease despite molecularly targeted interventions.³

Single-cell RNA sequencing analysis reveals distinct phenotypic and functional differences between ibrutinib-sensitive and ibrutinib-resistant CLL cells, accompanied by profound remodeling of the tumor immunosuppressive microenvironment.³ Therapy-resistant cells exhibit altered surface marker expression, differential metabolic dependencies, and enhanced apoptotic resistance compared to therapy-sensitive populations. Simultaneously, the immune microenvironment evolves during BTKi treatment, with expansion of immunosuppressive T-cell subsets that compromise anti-tumor immunity and limit monotherapy efficacy.³ Understanding these molecular and immunologic differences is essential for developing combination strategies capable of achieving complete disease eradication in CLL patients currently managed with single-agent BTKi therapy.

## Immunotherapy Approaches

Emerging immunotherapeutic strategies show promise in targeting residual disease populations that persist despite conventional chemotherapy and targeted agents. Immunogenic cell death (ICD)-primed autophagosome-based vaccines enhance CD8+ T-cell responses by selectively enriching tumor-associated antigens and danger-associated molecular patterns while depleting suppressive by-products generated during cell death.⁴ These engineered immunogenic cargo-containing autophagosomes achieve robust cross-presentation by dendritic cells, licensing efficient recognition through DNGR-1-dependent pathways and promoting tumor-specific CD8+ T-cell priming.⁴ In preclinical peritoneal metastasis models, [[icd]]-primed autophagosome immunotherapy achieves approximately 90% tumor clearance and provides a scalable foundation for personalized vaccines targeting minimal residual disease.⁴

This immunotherapeutic paradigm represents a fundamentally distinct strategy from conventional cytotoxic approaches. By converting residual disease from a failure of direct cell-killing therapy into a target for adaptive immunity, ICD-primed vaccines offer complementary mechanisms for achieving durable disease control in patients with MRD that escapes conventional treatment modalities.
