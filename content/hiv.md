**HIV** (human immunodeficiency virus) is a retrovirus that causes acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS) and remains a leading global public health challenge, particularly affecting pregnant women and their infants. The virus enters host cells by binding the CD4 receptor and the C-C chemokine receptor 5 ([[ccr5|CCR5]]) as an obligate co-receptor.¹ A small population naturally carries the [[ccr5|CCR5]]-Δ32/Δ32 homozygous deletion, conferring near-complete protection from infection and establishing [[ccr5|CCR5]] blockade as a validated therapeutic principle.¹ Contemporary HIV management integrates policy-driven interventions ensuring engagement across critical life stages with emerging gene therapy approaches designed to replicate the protective effect of [[ccr5|CCR5]] deletion.

---

## Mechanism of viral entry

HIV attachment and fusion depend critically on sequential binding to the CD4 receptor and the C-C chemokine receptor 5 ([[ccr5|CCR5]]) co-receptor on target cells, particularly [[cd4-t-cells|CD4+ T lymphocytes]].¹ The virus first engages CD4, triggering conformational changes that expose the hidden [[ccr5|CCR5]] binding epitope and enabling membrane fusion.¹ Individuals homozygous for the [[ccr5|CCR5]]-Δ32 deletion—a non-functional 32-base pair deletion that removes the functional receptor—are resistant to infection by CCR5-tropic viral strains.¹ This natural genetic variant has proven that complete [[ccr5|CCR5]] blockade is compatible with sustained immune function and normal health, establishing it as a rational therapeutic target for both prevention and treatment strategies.

---

## Perinatal transmission and the Option B+ policy

Mother-to-child transmission remains a major HIV clinical challenge in resource-limited settings. In January 2015, South Africa implemented Option B+, a policy extending lifelong antiretroviral therapy (ART) to all pregnant women living with HIV regardless of [[cd4-t-cells|CD4 count]], preventing perinatal transmission and establishing lasting clinical engagement.² The policy recognizes that postpartum disengagement from HIV care substantially elevates risks for adverse maternal health outcomes and ongoing transmission through breastfeeding.² Women frequently disengage from care in the postpartum period due to changing social needs, altered perceived health urgency, and healthcare access barriers—a pattern necessitating integrated clinical and social interventions that maintain support continuity beyond delivery.²

## Gene therapy approach: AAV-mediated CCR5 blockade

Emerging molecular strategies aim to mimic the protective [[ccr5|CCR5]]-Δ32/Δ32 genotype through targeted somatic gene therapy. Adeno-associated virus (AAV) vectors have been engineered to deliver and sustain expression of [[leronlimab]], a monoclonal antibody that blocks [[ccr5|CCR5]] function, in HIV-infected individuals.¹ Preclinical studies in simian-human immunodeficiency virus (SHIV)-infected rhesus macaques demonstrated long-term viral suppression in a subset of treated animals.¹ Approximately one-third of macaques achieved detectable plasma [[leronlimab]] concentrations and full [[ccr5|CCR5]] receptor occupancy on [[cd4-t-cells|blood CD4+ T cells]] for over one year, with stringent or partial control of SHIV viremia in six of nine animals achieving sufficient [[leronlimab]] expression for complete receptor occupancy.¹ In animals with uncontrolled viremia despite [[leronlimab]] expression, short-term administration of exogenous [[leronlimab]] induced complete suppression or marked reduction (100-fold) in viral load, suggesting that achieving a critical antibody concentration threshold is essential for viral control.¹

Antidrug antibody (ADA) responses present a significant immunological barrier to sustained [[leronlimab]] expression. Approximately half of AAV-treated macaques developed anti-[[leronlimab]] antibodies that rapidly cleared plasma [[leronlimab]], initially limiting transgene efficacy and establishing ADA formation as a major obstacle to clinical translation.¹ Remarkably, in these same animals, spontaneous [[leronlimab]] transgene reemergence occurred approximately one year after AAV delivery, restoring [[ccr5|CCR5]] receptor occupancy and enabling sustained viral control despite initial ADA formation.¹ This unexpected reemergence suggests complex immune dynamics within tissue compartments or selective tolerance mechanisms, raising important questions about the long-term immunogenicity profile of AAV gene therapy recipients and opportunities for optimizing transgene persistence and efficacy.
