**RET** is a proto-oncogene encoding a receptor tyrosine kinase whose fusion events and activating mutations drive multiple human cancers, particularly [[medullary-thyroid-cancer|medullary thyroid cancer]] and RET fusion-positive [[nsclc|non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC)]]. Upon oncogenic activation through chromosomal rearrangement or point mutation, RET undergoes constitutive autophosphorylation and recruits downstream signaling mediators including [[shc|SHC]], [[akt|AKT]], and [[erk|ERK]], establishing a signaling axis that promotes malignant proliferation and survival.¹ RET fusions represent a clinically actionable precision oncology target, occurring in approximately 1–2% of NSCLC cases.² [[Pralsetinib]], an oral selective RET inhibitor, achieved FDA approval for metastatic RET-altered malignancies based on robust clinical responses; however, the rapid emergence of resistance mutations spanning the solvent-front (G810R/S/C), gatekeeper (V804M), hinge (Y806H), and catalytic loop (M918T) positions has necessitated development of next-generation agents such as [[cn-3|CN-3]].¹

---

## Mechanism

RET is a transmembrane receptor tyrosine kinase normally regulated by extracellular ligand binding to its ectodomain. Pathologically, RET becomes constitutively active through two cardinal mechanisms: chromosomal rearrangements that fuse RET to constitutively expressed promoters or open reading frames (generating RET fusion proteins), and point mutations that directly alter kinase domain architecture to promote autophosphorylation. Once activated, RET phosphorylates tyrosine residues within its own intracellular domain and on recruited adapter and signaling proteins. The [[shc|SHC/AKT/ERK]] cascade represents a critical downstream pathway, wherein phosphorylated RET recruits the adapter protein SHC, which then activates AKT through phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase signaling and ERK through classical Ras/mitogen-activated protein kinase cascade mechanisms. This sustained signaling loop drives increased expression of proliferation genes and suppresses apoptotic programs, conferring the hallmark properties of transformed cells.¹

## Cancer applications

RET-altered disease encompasses distinct cancer subtypes reflecting different modes of RET activation. [[Medullary-thyroid-cancer|Medullary thyroid cancer]], arising from neural crest-derived calcitonin-producing cells, shows the highest RET-dependence of any human cancer—approximately 50% of sporadic cases and virtually all hereditary familial medullary thyroid carcinomas harbor RET point mutations. In contrast, RET fusion events are less common in medullary disease but have emerged as a defining precision medicine category in [[nsclc|NSCLC]]. RET fusions occur in 1–2% of non-small cell lung cancers, predominantly in patients with adenocarcinoma histology, younger age at diagnosis, and favorable performance status at presentation.²

Clinical validation of RET inhibition came from the ARROW phase I/II trial, which evaluated [[pralsetinib]], an oral selective RET inhibitor. In treatment-naïve RET fusion-positive NSCLC patients with measurable disease, pralsetinib achieved an objective response rate of 78% and a median overall survival of 50.1 months.² In previously treated patients exposed to platinum-based chemotherapy, the response rate declined to 63%, yet median overall survival remained durable at 39.7 months.² Common treatment-related grade ≥3 adverse events included anemia (21%), hypertension (15%), and neutropenia (13%), with three treatment-related deaths—two from pneumonia and one from interstitial lung disease coupled with rhabdomyolysis. These results established pralsetinib as the first FDA-approved selective RET inhibitor for metastatic RET-altered NSCLC and RET fusion-positive thyroid cancers, validating RET inhibition as an effective precision oncology strategy.²

## Resistance mechanisms and next-generation inhibitors

Despite the clinical efficacy of [[pralsetinib]] and first-generation selective RET inhibitors, resistance mutations have emerged as a principal mechanism limiting durable responses. The most frequently encountered resistance variants map to four functionally critical regions of the RET kinase domain: the solvent-front (encompassing G810R, G810S, and G810C mutations), the gatekeeper position (V804M), the hinge region (Y806H), and the catalytic loop (M918T).¹ These mutations reduce inhibitor binding affinity or alter the kinase domain geometry required for inhibitor accommodation, allowing tumor cells to escape selective pressure. Different first-generation inhibitors display variable activity against individual resistance mutants, indicating that single-agent sequential therapy may be limited by distinct resistance profiles.

[[CN-3]] was rationally designed to overcome this heterogeneous resistance landscape by optimization of the RET inhibitor scaffold. Biochemically, CN-3 demonstrated sub-5 nanoMolar IC50 potency against all tested RET variants, including the solvent-front, gatekeeper, hinge, and catalytic loop resistance mutations.¹ In cellular assays, CN-3 suppressed proliferation of RET-driven models with high potency: TT medullary thyroid cancer cells (IC50 = 2.48 ± 0.78 nM) and LC-2/ad lung adenocarcinoma cells (IC50 = 17.05 ± 4.90 nM), while demonstrating negligible activity in RET-independent and normal cells.¹ At the mechanistic level, CN-3 blocked RET autophosphorylation and suppressed downstream [[shc|SHC]]/[[akt|AKT]]/[[erk|ERK]] signaling, inducing cell-cycle arrest in the G0-G1 phase and triggering apoptosis in RET-driven cellular models.¹ In vivo xenograft studies revealed dose-dependent antitumor efficacy with favorable tolerability. Kinase selectivity profiling indicated moderate off-target activity restricted primarily to a limited group of receptor tyrosine kinases, suggesting a manageable toxicity profile suitable for clinical translation.¹
