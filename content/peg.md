**Polyethylene glycol (PEG)** is a synthetic hydrophilic polymer widely conjugated to nanomedicines, biologics, and drug carriers to extend systemic half-life and improve therapeutic efficacy. Its core mechanism is steric shielding—the grafted polymer chains reduce renal clearance, inhibit opsonization by serum proteins, and minimize recognition by the reticuloendothelial system, thereby prolonging circulation time.¹ Applications span [[afcdc|antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs)]], [[lnp|lipid nanoparticles (LNPs)]] for mRNA delivery, and targeted drug conjugates. However, a major clinical limitation has emerged: repeated or chronic exposure to PEG triggers adaptive anti-PEG immune responses, paradoxically accelerating blood clearance and causing hypersensitivity reactions.² Rational optimization of PEG architecture—through shortened chains, high grafting density on rigid surfaces, and enzyme-cleavable linkers—now enables PEGylation while mitigating immunotoxicity.

---

## Mechanism of action

PEG extends the circulating half-life of nanomedicines through multiple complementary mechanisms. The hydrophilic polymer coating prevents adsorption of opsonins (immunoglobulins and complement components) to the nanoparticle surface, reducing recognition and sequestration by the mononuclear phagocyte system. Additionally, the steric barrier created by PEG chains restricts extravasation into non-target tissues and minimizes glomerular filtration, particularly for smaller conjugates. The physicochemical properties of the grafted PEG—including chain length, grafting density, and flexibility—directly determine the efficacy of this protective effect. Longer PEG chains (e.g., PEG2000) theoretically provide greater steric coverage but paradoxically display higher immunogenicity due to increased epitope accessibility for antibody binding.²

## Applications in nanomedicine

PEGylation has become standard in clinical and experimental nanomedicines. In [[afcdc|antibody fragment-drug conjugate (AFCDC)]] platforms, programmable site-specific dual conjugation using genetically encoded noncanonical amino acids enables attachment of both cytotoxic payloads and MMP2-cleavable PEG chains.¹ The PEG component extends systemic retention while tumor-resident [[mmp2|matrix metalloproteinase-2 (MMP2)]] and related proteases progressively remove the polymer layer, yielding smaller drug-conjugate complexes with improved tissue penetration and stromal diffusion. This strategy resolves the antibody-antigen trade-off in solid tumors: full-length antibodies (∼150 kDa) penetrate poorly due to size and binding-site barriers, whereas antibody fragments clear rapidly; PEGylation of fragments restores pharmacokinetics while maintaining the penetration advantage of smaller molecules.

[[lnp|Lipid nanoparticles (LNPs)]] for mRNA therapeutics likewise depend on PEG engineering to achieve clinical efficacy. PEG-conjugated lipid surrogates in the LNP formulation stabilize colloidal suspension, extend shelf life, and suppress innate immune activation. The PEG-mediated circulation prolongation enables sufficient time for LNPs to reach target tissues and cells before hepatic degradation or renal filtration.

---

## Anti-PEG immunity and immunotoxicity

A major and sometimes unpredictable clinical limitation of PEGylated nanomedicines is the induction of anti-PEG adaptive immune responses, particularly in patients receiving repeated doses. Circulating anti-PEG immunoglobulins (both IgG and IgM) bind to the polymer coating and accelerate blood clearance through complement activation and Fc receptor-mediated uptake.² In some patients, anti-PEG immunity escalates to severe hypersensitivity or infusion reactions, limiting treatment feasibility. Chain length dramatically influences this phenomenon: longer PEG chains (PEG2000) present more exposed epitopes and exhibit substantially higher antibody binding compared to shorter variants (PEG500), as quantified by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) and in vivo immunogenicity studies.² The mechanism includes complement activation—anti-PEG IgG engages C3 and terminal complement components, driving systemic inflammation and cytokine release.

---

## Optimization through architectural design

Recent advances overcome anti-PEG immunity by systematically tuning polymer architecture. A short-chain, high-density PEG brush design—specifically PEG500 chains grafted at a density of 4.43 chains per nm² onto rigid mesoporous silica nanoparticles—drastically reduces immune recognition through three synergistic mechanisms.² First, shortened PEG chains inherently limit epitope accessibility for antibody binding. Second, the high grafting density creates a dense steric barrier that shields individual chains from immune recognition. Third, rigid nanoparticle surfaces prevent PEG backfolding and burial, maintaining constant outward presentation and maximal steric protection. In direct comparison, mesoporous silica nanoparticles carrying PEG500 at this density showed near-background anti-PEG IgG and IgM binding, whereas conventional PEG2000-coated liposomes (Lipodox) exhibited robust antibody recognition. Critically, in anti-PEG-immunized mice, doxorubicin-loaded nanoparticles with optimized short-chain PEG (PEG500) preserved full antitumor efficacy with 100% survival, whereas PEG2000-based formulations induced fatal hypersensitivity reactions, demonstrating the clinical importance of polymer architecture.

## Enzymatically cleavable PEG linkers

An alternative strategy for mitigating anti-PEG immunotoxicity while retaining pharmacokinetic benefits is the use of enzyme-cleavable PEG linkers. The [[afcdc|antibody fragment-cleavable PEGylated drug conjugate (AFCDC)]] platform employs matrix metalloproteinase-2 (MMP2)–responsive linkers that are selectively cleaved by tumor-resident proteases.¹ Upon cleavage, the PEG polymer is released from the conjugate, unmasking smaller drug-fragment complexes. This design offers a temporal advantage: PEG protects the conjugate during systemic circulation, but once the conjugate penetrates into the tumor microenvironment where MMP2 is abundantly expressed, proteolytic removal of PEG enables deeper tissue diffusion and improved drug efficacy. This approach combines the circulation-extending benefits of PEGylation with the penetration advantages of smaller, uncoated molecules, and may reduce cumulative anti-PEG immune stimulation by limiting the duration of polymer-surface presentation.

---

## Alternative polymeric coatings

Beyond optimization of PEG architecture, researchers have identified immunologically inert alternative surface coatings to replace PEG entirely. [[heparosan|Heparosan]], a naturally occurring, biodegradable polysaccharide, has emerged as a promising PEG-free coating for lipid nanoparticles.³ Heparosan-engineered mRNA-LNPs demonstrate delivery efficacy comparable to PEG-modified counterparts while exhibiting minimal tissue damage and negligible immune activation. The heparosan coating confers colloidal stability and mRNA encapsulation efficiency equivalent to PEG, yet avoids the anti-polymer immune responses that constrain conventional PEGylated formulations. This platform represents a path toward next-generation nanomedicines that preserve the pharmacokinetic and biophysical advantages of polymer engineering without immunotoxicity.
