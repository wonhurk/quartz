**Immune checkpoint blockade (ICB)** is a cancer immunotherapy that unleashes anti-tumor T cell responses by blocking inhibitory checkpoint proteins, enabling sustained T cell activation and cytotoxic function against malignant cells.¹ While ICB has transformed cancer treatment across multiple malignancies, its efficacy remains heterogeneous—some patients achieve durable remissions while others show minimal benefit. Emerging evidence reveals that treatment outcomes are shaped by interconnected mechanisms including circulating autoantibodies against diverse tumor-associated antigens, the protective mucin-rich [[tumor-glycocalyx|glycocalyx]] that masks checkpoint proteins, and the ability of cancer cells to sustain replication stress under immune pressure.¹ Recent preclinical and clinical findings identify glycocalyx remodeling and DNA damage-amplifying strategies as complementary approaches to overcome ICB resistance and improve therapeutic outcomes across heterogeneous patient populations.

---

## Mechanism

Immune checkpoint inhibitors function by blocking membrane-resident inhibitory receptors on T cells, most notably programmed death receptor 1 (PD-1) and cytotoxic T lymphocyte antigen 4 (CTLA-4), which are engaged by ligands displayed on tumor cells and suppress T cell activation. By preventing this inhibitory signaling, checkpoint blockade restores T cell proliferation, effector function, and persistence within the tumor microenvironment. This reinvigorated anti-tumor T cell response, particularly by [[cd8-t-cells|CD8+ T cells]], drives tumor cell killing and regression. However, the efficacy of ICB is constrained by multiple biological barriers and patient-level factors that collectively account for the substantial variability in clinical treatment responses.

## Humoral responses and autoantibody biomarkers

Beyond the T cell-centric model of checkpoint inhibition, circulating autoantibodies emerge as significant contributors to ICB efficacy. Using broad antigen profiling across 374 ICB-treated patients and 131 healthy controls sampled longitudinally, researchers identified that individuals who developed autoantibodies against specific pathways achieved substantially improved therapeutic outcomes.¹ Notably, autoantibodies targeting the [[type-1-interferon|type I interferon]] pathway, [[il-6|IL-6]], [[il-17|IL-17]], and [[tl1a|TNF-like ligand 1A (TL1A)]] associated with better responses, particularly when these antibodies were functionally neutralizing.¹ The autoantibody landscape in ICB-treated individuals is substantially enriched compared to healthy controls, and these antibody profiles remain relatively stable during treatment, suggesting that early humoral responses may serve as predictive biomarkers of therapeutic benefit. Conversely, patients who developed antibodies blocking proteins in the [[bone-morphogenesis-pathway|bone morphogenesis pathway]] exhibited worse responses, highlighting the unexpected predictive value of humoral profiling.¹ This finding expands the mechanistic understanding of ICB beyond cellular immunity to encompass the contribution of adaptive humoral immunity in determining therapeutic success.

---

## The tumor glycocalyx as a physical barrier

A critical and previously underappreciated obstacle to ICB efficacy is the [[tumor-glycocalyx|tumor glycocalyx]]—a dense, mucin-rich carbohydrate coating that envelops tumor cells.² This glycoprotein shield masks immunologically relevant surface proteins, including checkpoint molecules and tumor-associated antigens, physically preventing immune cell recognition and engagement. The glycocalyx effectively establishes an immune-evasive microenvironment by obscuring the very targets that checkpoint blockade is designed to expose. The composition and thickness of the glycocalyx vary substantially across tumors and patient populations, potentially explaining a significant portion of the heterogeneity in ICB responses observed clinically. The glycocalyx becomes particularly dominant in certain tumor types with high mucin expression, such as mucinous adenocarcinomas and some colorectal cancers, where it may represent a primary resistance mechanism to immunotherapy.

## Glycocalyx-degrading nanovesicle platforms

To overcome the glycocalyx barrier, researchers engineered biomimetic cell membrane nanovesicles that display both mucinase enzymes and immune-enhancing proteins.² Specifically, nanovesicles codisplaying the bacterial protease StcE and [[cd47|CD47]] nanobodies (termed [[stce-ncd47-fnv|StcE-nCD47-FNVs]]) were developed to simultaneously degrade the mucin shield and enhance immune cell targeting to tumors.² The StcE protease catalytically cleaves O-glycosylated mucin domains, stripping away the glycocalyx barrier and exposing previously masked checkpoint proteins and tumor antigens. By coupling glycocalyx-degrading enzymatic activity with [[cd47|CD47]] nanobodies—which mimic the endogenous "don't-eat-me" signal to protect vesicles from premature phagocytosis—the platform achieves superior tumor accumulation and biosafety compared to free enzyme.² In preclinical models of colorectal and breast cancer, [[stce-ncd47-fnv|StcE-nCD47-FNVs]] synergized with endogenous checkpoint blockade to potently suppress tumor growth and metastasis while simultaneously remodeling the immune microenvironment toward pro-inflammatory M1 macrophage polarization and enhanced [[cd8-t-cells|CD8+ T cell]] infiltration.² This platform exemplifies how direct manipulation of the tumor physical microenvironment can potentiate immunotherapy efficacy through complementary mechanisms.

---

## Synergy with DNA-damaging agents and innate immunity

Beyond glycocalyx remodeling, ICB efficacy can be substantially enhanced through complementary DNA damage-driving strategies. Inhibition of [[rnase-h2|RNase H2]], the nucleotide excision repair enzyme that removes ribonucleotides from DNA, synergizes with both [[atr|ATR]] and [[parp-inhibitors|PARP inhibitors]] to elevate replication stress and trigger potent innate immune activation.³ [[rnase-h2|RNase H2]] inhibition increases cytosolic single-stranded DNA accumulation, which activates innate immune sensors and upregulates T cell-recruiting chemokines, thereby amplifying checkpoint blockade efficacy through dual mechanisms of increased immunogenicity and enhanced immune cell trafficking.³ This approach is particularly relevant in [[tnbc|triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC)]], where cancer cells are intrinsically dependent on [[rnase-h2|RNase H2]] overexpression to escape senescence under replication stress—a therapeutic vulnerability that can be exploited through pharmacological inhibition.

---

## TNBC-specific applications and vulnerabilities

[[tnbc|Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC)]] represents a particularly treatment-resistant malignancy that lacks conventional targeted therapeutic options and responds heterogeneously to standard ICB.³ Mechanistically, TNBC cells suppress oncogene-induced senescence through elevated [[rnase-h2|RNase H2]] expression, which permits continued DNA replication despite endogenous genotoxic stress. [[rnase-h2|RNase H2]] inhibition selectively impairs TNBC cell viability while sparing non-tumorigenic cells, thereby establishing a tumor-selective therapeutic window.³ Importantly, [[rnase-h2|RNase H2]] inhibition not only suppresses TNBC growth directly but also substantially enhances immune checkpoint blockade efficacy by amplifying replication stress, DNA damage signaling, and innate immune activation.³ This integrated approach—combining [[rnase-h2|RNase H2]] inhibition with checkpoint blockade—offers a compelling strategy to overcome the intrinsic immunotherapy resistance characteristic of TNBC and improve outcomes in this aggressive disease.

---

## Novel combination strategies and biomarker-driven approaches

The convergence of these mechanistic insights—humoral autoantibody responses, glycocalyx remodeling, and DNA damage amplification—suggests that combination approaches will likely outperform single-agent ICB strategies in overcoming treatment resistance. Future therapies may integrate [[stce-ncd47-fnv|glycocalyx-degrading nanovesicles]] with DNA damage-sensitizing agents such as [[rnase-h2|RNase H2]] inhibitors to address multiple overlapping resistance mechanisms simultaneously. Biomarker-driven selection strategies leveraging autoantibody profiling, tumor glycocalyx characterization, and replication stress signatures could enable patient stratification and personalized combination therapy design. The identification of these complementary and mechanistically non-overlapping pathways for ICB enhancement provides a rational framework for overcoming the substantial heterogeneity currently observed in patient responses and expanding ICB efficacy across a broader range of malignancies and patient populations.
