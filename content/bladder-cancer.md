**Bladder cancer** (BCa) is a heterogeneous malignancy characterized by significant clinical and molecular diversity, requiring tailored therapeutic and diagnostic strategies. Environmental and metabolic factors drive disease progression through distinct molecular pathways; notably, [[acetyl-tributyl-citrate|acetyl tributyl citrate (ATBC)]], a ubiquitous plasticizer, promotes bladder cancer cell proliferation and migration via [[akr1b1|AKR1B1]]-mediated [[epithelial-mesenchymal-transition|epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT)]].¹ Emerging therapeutic modalities including [[photothermal-therapy|photothermal therapy]] and metabolic pathway inhibition, combined with novel [[biomarker]]-based diagnostics using [[extracellular-vesicles|bladder cancer-derived extracellular vesicles (BC-EVs)]], are transforming the management landscape by enabling precise early detection and patient stratification.

---

## Mechanism

The oncogenic progression of bladder cancer operates through multiple interconnected pathways. [[acetyl-tributyl-citrate|ATBC]], an environmental pollutant increasingly recognized as a carcinogen, exerts its transformative effects primarily through the [[akr1b1|AKR1B1]] axis. Computational integration of protein-ligand prediction databases identified 461 potential ATBC-binding targets, of which 142 were directly associated with bladder cancer pathophysiology.¹ Among these, [[akr1b1|AKR1B1]] exhibited the strongest binding affinity and is significantly upregulated in bladder cancer tissue, correlating with advanced disease stage and poor prognosis. Molecular dynamics simulations confirmed the stability of the ATBC-[[akr1b1|AKR1B1]] complex, and functional validation demonstrated that ATBC-driven AKR1B1 activation promotes epithelial-mesenchymal transition, facilitating bladder cancer cell migration and metastatic capacity.¹ This metabolic pathway represents a critical node connecting environmental exposure to EMT reprogramming.

## Photothermal therapy

[[indocyanine-green|Indocyanine green (ICG)]] represents the only FDA-approved photothermal agent suitable for cancer treatment, yet its clinical translation has been limited by poor serum stability, short circulation half-life, and lack of tumor-selective targeting. Biosynthetic [[gas-vesicle|gas vesicles (GVs)]]—subcellular organelles derived from photosynthetic bacteria—overcome these limitations when covalently conjugated with ICG.² The resulting ICG-GV nanoconjugates achieve uniform morphology (~200 nm) with high ICG loading efficiency (58%) and excellent colloidal stability, enabling simultaneous trimodal imaging via ultrasound, near-infrared fluorescence, and photoacoustic modalities.² Pharmacokinetic analysis demonstrates that gas vesicle conjugation significantly prolongs ICG blood circulation half-life and increases bioavailability (area under the curve). Critically, ultrasound-triggered cavitation of gas vesicles enhances intratumoral ICG accumulation, allowing tumor temperatures to exceed 60°C upon near-infrared laser irradiation, leading to complete tumor regression and extended survival in murine [[bladder-cancer|bladder cancer]] xenografts without detectable systemic toxicity.² This clinically translatable strategy represents a paradigm shift toward precision photothermal oncology.

## Adaptive resistance and combination therapy

While [[pikfyve|PIKFYVE inhibition]] has emerged as a promising anti-cancer strategy, adaptive resistance mechanisms limit its monotherapy efficacy. In bladder cancer, PIKFYVE inhibitors trigger compensatory Akt-SREBP1-SCD1 signaling, restoring cellular lipid homeostasis and enabling tumor cell survival. [[polyphyllin-h|Polyphyllin H]], a naturally occurring steroid saponin, overcomes this resistance by targeting [[grb2|GRB2]], a critical adapter protein upstream of Akt phosphorylation.³ Combination therapy targeting both PIKFYVE and GRB2 simultaneously inhibits the lipid-synthetic escape route and restores therapeutic vulnerability, establishing a potent and well-tolerated regimen for bladder cancer treatment.³

## Diagnostic strategies and biomarker profiling

Early and precise diagnosis of bladder cancer is essential for improving patient outcomes. [[extracellular-vesicles|Bladder cancer-derived extracellular vesicles (BC-EVs)]], which are shed directly into the urine by tumor cells and carry disease-specific molecular information, represent noninvasive liquid biopsy targets with high clinical potential.⁴ However, their utility has been hampered by low abundance in urine and massive interference from non-tumor extracellular vesicles. An in vivo metabolic engineering strategy overcomes these barriers using [[ros-responsive-micelle-probe|tumor-targeting reactive oxygen species (ROS)-responsive micelle probes]] that selectively accumulate in bladder tumors and release azide-modified lipids into nascent BC-EV membranes in response to the oxidative tumor microenvironment.⁴ These chemically tagged EVs are subsequently enriched in urine through bioorthogonal click chemistry, effectively eliminating background contamination and enabling precise cancer diagnosis.⁴ Critically, BC-EV biomarker profiling successfully discriminates [[nonmuscle-invasive-bladder-cancer|nonmuscle-invasive]] from [[muscle-invasive-bladder-cancer|muscle-invasive bladder cancer]], allowing treatment intensity and surveillance strategies to be tailored to disease biology rather than anatomical extent alone.⁴ This metabolic programming approach is broadly extensible to other malignancies by modifying targeting and recognition moieties.
