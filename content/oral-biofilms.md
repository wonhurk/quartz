**Oral biofilms** are structured microbial communities that colonize the oral cavity and represent a major pathogenic factor in multiple bacterial diseases, most prominently [[endodontic-infections]], [[dental-caries]], and [[periodontitis]].¹² These biofilms are uniquely challenging to eliminate due to their intricate three-dimensional architecture and marked resistance to conventional disinfectants, which often necessitate repeated therapeutic interventions with high rates of clinical recurrence.¹ The discovery that organisms such as [[streptococcus-mutans]] form mature biofilms refractory to standard antimicrobial agents has spurred development of innovative nanotechnology-based strategies that leverage physical disruption mechanisms and catalytic enhancement to overcome biofilm resilience at substantially reduced drug concentrations.

---

## Mechanism and Structure

Oral biofilms develop through a sequential process wherein planktonic bacteria adhere to oral surfaces and progressively elaborate an extracellular polymeric matrix composed of polysaccharides, proteins, and nucleic acids. This matrix creates a microenvironment of reduced oxygen tension, altered pH gradients, and restricted nutrient diffusion that fundamentally alters bacterial physiology and gene expression.¹ Root canal biofilms exhibit particular complexity due to the anatomy of the endodontium, wherein microorganisms colonize not only the main root canal space but also penetrate lateral canals and dentinal tubules, rendering physical and chemical disinfection extremely challenging.¹ The biofilm-associated phenotype confers phenotypic resistance independent of genetic determinants — cells within the matrix experience reduced exposure to antimicrobial agents and operate under metabolic conditions that suppress growth-dependent antibiotic targets.

---

## Associated Oral Diseases

Oral biofilms are the primary microbial driver of endodontic infection, wherein polymicrobial communities establish themselves in the root canal system following pulpal necrosis or invasion through cavitated carious lesions.¹ These [[endodontic-infections]] represent a major clinical burden, with conventional [[gutta-percha]]-based obturation techniques failing to achieve complete biofilm eradication and leading to high rates of periapical periodontitis and treatment failure.¹ In dental caries pathogenesis, [[streptococcus-mutans]]-dominated biofilms produce lactic acid that demineralizes tooth structure, though conventional fluoride and antimicrobial rinses achieve only transient biofilm suppression rather than lasting elimination.¹ Subgingival biofilms similarly drive the inflammatory cascade in [[periodontitis]], wherein gram-negative anaerobes and spirochetes provoke a host immune response that paradoxically accelerates alveolar bone loss.²

---

## Antimicrobial Resistance and Treatment Failure

Conventional antimicrobial approaches — including chlorhexidine-based rinses, iodine disinfectants, and antibiotic pastes — demonstrate limited efficacy against established oral biofilms despite their potency against planktonic bacteria.² This resistance arises through multiple mechanisms: the extracellular matrix physically impedes penetration of large antimicrobial molecules; the biofilm microenvironment creates pH and redox conditions that inactivate certain classes of agents; and slow-growing or metabolically dormant cells exhibit tolerance to growth-dependent antimicrobials.¹² The consequence is frequent clinical recurrence, necessitating repeated endodontic retreatment, scaling and root planing, or in severe cases, tooth extraction — a particularly problematic outcome for younger patients facing lifetime morbidity.

---

## Nanomotor-Based Biofilm Disruption

Recent advances in nanotechnology have yielded self-propelled [[platinum-mesoporous-silica-nanomotors]] that simultaneously address the structural and chemical barriers to biofilm eradication.¹ These Janus nanoparticles leverage hydrogen peroxide as a fuel source to generate autonomous motion within the biofilm matrix, enabling direct mechanical disruption of the extracellular polysaccharide architecture. Critically, the nanomotors are loaded with [[chlorhexidine]] at concentrations ten-fold lower than conventional commercial formulations, yet achieve potent antimicrobial effects when propelled by H₂O₂ activation.¹ The nanomotor surface is functionalized with a pH-responsive molecular gate coupled to [[ficin]], a cysteine protease that enzymatically degrades biofilm matrix proteins, thereby further compromising structural integrity and enhancing antimicrobial penetration. In clinically relevant models, H₂O₂-activated nanomotors effectively disrupted mature [[streptococcus-mutans]] biofilms on dentin sections and successfully penetrated dentinal microtubules to eradicate intratubular bacterial populations, demonstrating efficacy in the complex geometry of endodontic environments.¹

## Peroxidase Nanozyme with Photothermal Enhancement

An alternative nanotherapeutic strategy employs hybrid peroxidase-like nanozymes designated PB@MoS₂@Au, consisting of a Prussian blue nanocube core decorated with a molybdenum disulfide shell and further functionalized with gold nanoparticles.² This multifunctional nanozyme integrates two complementary activities: enhanced peroxidase-mimetic catalytic activity that catalyzes hydrogen peroxide decomposition to generate reactive oxygen species, and a photothermal conversion efficiency of 37.4% enabling mild hyperthermia (approximately 50°C) in response to near-infrared irradiation.² The combined treatment — nanozyme plus low-concentration hydrogen peroxide (200 μM) plus near-infrared light — exhibits potent bactericidal activity against representative oral bacterial strains including [[enterococcus-faecalis]], achieving bacterial killing rates exceeding 95% in planktonic cultures and exceeding 90% within biofilms. In an ex vivo tooth infection model replicating the clinical scenario of endodontic biofilm burden, this multimodal approach eliminated over 99.5% of [[enterococcus-faecalis]] biofilms from root canals, substantially outperforming conventional irrigation and intracanal medication protocols.²

---

## Clinical Implications

The emergence of nanomotor and nanozyme-based therapies addresses a critical therapeutic gap in oral infectious disease management. By reducing antimicrobial drug concentrations while simultaneously enhancing biofilm eradication rates, these approaches reduce cytotoxicity to periapical tissues, oral mucosa, and systemic absorption risks associated with conventional high-dose disinfectant protocols.¹² Furthermore, the multimodal mechanisms — combining mechanical disruption, enzymatic degradation, catalytic activity, and photothermal effects — reduce the probability of resistance emergence, as biofilms cannot simultaneously evade multiple antimicrobial modalities.¹² Clinical translation of these technologies requires optimization of nanoparticle biocompatibility, development of clinical delivery devices for endodontic and periodontal spaces, and regulatory validation in human efficacy trials.
