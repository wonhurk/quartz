**RAS** is a small GTP-binding protein and master oncogenic regulator that drives proliferation and survival signaling across multiple cancer types, including RAS-mutant solid tumors and acute myeloid leukemia (AML). The protein cycles between an inactive GDP-bound state and an active GTP-bound state, the latter enabling recruitment of downstream effector proteins such as [[raf|RAF]] to trigger mitogenic cascades.¹ Therapeutic breakthrough has been achieved through [[tri-complex-inhibitors|tri-complex inhibitors (TCIs)]], exemplified by [[daraxonrasib]], which function as molecular glues that selectively bind GTP-bound RAS and recruit [[cypa|cyclophilin A (CYPA)]] to form a synthetic ternary complex that physically blocks RAS-mediated signaling.¹ However, clinical resistance emerges through two mechanistically distinct pathways: acquired mutations that disrupt inhibitor binding to RAS (such as Y64 mutations) and mutations that enhance native RAS-RAF signaling independent of TCI binding (Y71 mutations or kinase-dead [[braf|BRAF]] mutations).¹

---

## Mechanism

RAS functions as a canonical molecular switch, with GTP binding inducing a conformational transition that exposes protein-protein interaction surfaces and enables engagement with downstream effectors. In cancer, gain-of-function RAS mutations impair intrinsic GTPase activity or GTPase-activating protein (GAP) recognition, locking the protein in the constitutively active GTP-bound state and driving unrelenting [[raf|RAF/MEK/ERK]] pathway signaling.¹ The discovery that the GTP-bound conformation is uniquely vulnerable to inhibition by [[daraxonrasib]] and related TCIs has provided a selective therapeutic window, as these inhibitors achieve specificity by binding only the nucleotide-bound pocket in its active configuration. The mechanism is fundamentally different from conventional kinase inhibitors: [[daraxonrasib]] does not occupy the nucleotide-binding site but instead recruits [[cypa|CYPA]] as a synthetic protein partner, generating a protein-protein-drug tripartite complex that sterically occludes the [[raf|RAF]] interaction interface.¹

## Cancer applications

[[daraxonrasib]] and related [[tri-complex-inhibitors|tri-complex inhibitors]] have demonstrated clinical activity in RAS-driven solid tumors. Paired analysis of baseline and end-of-treatment samples from 40 patients treated with [[daraxonrasib]] revealed objective responses; however, acquired resistance emerged in approximately 45% of evaluable cases, underscoring the need for mechanistic insight and second-generation approaches.¹ The tri-complex inhibitor strategy offers advantages over traditional kinase-targeted therapies by achieving selectivity for the active RAS-GTP state and avoiding off-target engagement with related signaling proteins.

In pediatric acute myeloid leukemia, RAS pathway mutations occur with particularly high frequency. A comprehensive multi-omics analysis of 23 pediatric minimally differentiated AML (AML-M0) cases—a rare and therapeutically intractable subgroup—revealed activating RAS mutations in 83% of patients, often co-occurring with [[runx1|RUNX1]] loss-of-function alterations and suppression of genes involved in oxidative phosphorylation and ribosomal biogenesis.² [[runx1|RUNX1]] disruption was significantly associated with poor prognosis and, in functional studies using CRISPR/Cas9 knockdown, conferred stem cell-like transcriptional features and reduced sensitivity to conventional chemotherapy agents including cytarabine and anthracyclines.² These findings suggest that RAS pathway activation in AML-M0 cooperates with transcriptional dysregulation to drive chemoresistance and stemness phenotypes.

## Resistance mechanisms

Two convergent resistance mechanisms have emerged in patients treated with [[daraxonrasib]] and are supported by structural and functional analyses. First, acquired RAS Y64 mutations disrupt the molecular glue interface required for [[daraxonrasib]] binding while preserving GTP hydrolysis capacity, enabling the mutant protein to retain transforming capability while evading inhibition.¹ These mutations specifically compromise the ternary complex formation between [[daraxonrasib]], [[cypa|CYPA]], and mutant RAS, allowing the cancer cell to maintain oncogenic signaling despite TCI exposure.

Second, mutations that enhance native RAS-RAF signaling provide a bypass route to resistance. RAS Y71 mutations directly augment the RAS-[[raf|RAF]] heterodimer interaction, while kinase-dead or hypoactive [[braf|BRAF]] mutations shift signaling toward scaffolding-dependent RAS-[[raf|RAF]] engagement, circumventing the need for kinase catalytic activity.¹ Kinase-dead [[braf|BRAF]] mutations are mechanistically notable because they paradoxically enhance RAS-[[raf|RAF]] physical interaction despite abolishing BRAF's catalytic function, making the cancer cell less reliant on kinase-dependent [[raf|RAF]] output and more dependent on its structural role as a [[ras|RAS]]-binding partner. These findings identify [[raf|RAF]] as a critical node for combination therapeutic targeting.

---

## Next-generation strategies

Structural characterization of TCI resistance has enabled rational development of next-generation inhibitors with expanded activity profiles. A second-generation [[tri-complex-inhibitors|tri-complex inhibitor]] has been identified that targets RAS Y64 mutants, offering a therapeutic avenue for tumors that develop Y64 alterations during [[daraxonrasib]] exposure.¹ For cancers driven by kinase-dead [[braf|BRAF]] mutations and enhanced RAS-[[raf|RAF]] signaling, combination strategies—such as pairing [[tri-complex-inhibitors|tri-complex inhibitors]] with [[raf|RAF]] inhibitors or other pathway modulators—have been mechanistically defined to suppress the enhanced RAS-[[raf|RAF]] axis. These advances exemplify how detailed structural and functional dissection of resistance mechanisms can systematically guide inhibitor redesign and rational combination therapy selection in RAS-driven malignancies.
