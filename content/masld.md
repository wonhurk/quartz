**Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD)** is the most prevalent chronic liver disorder worldwide, affecting approximately one-third of the global population, characterized by hepatic lipid accumulation in the context of metabolic dysregulation.¹ The disease can progress from simple steatosis to metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH) with inflammation and [[fibrosis-4-index|fibrosis]], substantially increasing the risk of [[cirrhosis]], hepatocellular carcinoma, and liver-related mortality.² MASLD pathogenesis is governed by complex molecular mechanisms including dysregulated de novo lipogenesis controlled by the [[ifrd1|IFRD1]]-GLUD1-α-ketoglutarate regulatory axis, gut microbiota-mediated effects of dietary lipids, progressive hepatic macrophage remodeling, and metabolic cross-talk with cardiovascular tissues.¹⁻⁶

---

## Mechanism

The molecular pathogenesis of MASLD centers on aberrant de novo lipogenesis (DNL) in hepatocytes, a process tightly regulated by the [[ifrd1|IFRD1]]-GLUD1-α-ketoglutarate axis.¹ [[ifrd1|Interferon-related developmental regulator 1 (IFRD1)]] promotes mitochondrial localization of glutamate dehydrogenase 1 (GLUD1) through direct protein interaction, stabilizing the enzyme's activity and enhancing production of α-ketoglutarate (α-KG). This metabolite functions as an epigenetic modulator, reducing H3K36me3 histone methylation at lipogenic gene promoters and thereby suppressing DNL transcriptionally.¹ Human liver tissues demonstrate an inverse correlation between [[ifrd1|IFRD1]] expression and MASLD/MASH severity, while experimental loss of Ifrd1 in mice exacerbates hepatic steatosis and inflammation. Conversely, hepatocyte-specific [[ifrd1|IFRD1]] expression or α-KG supplementation ameliorates MASH progression, establishing this axis as a critical metabolic-epigenetic regulatory hub.¹

## Dietary synergy and gut microbiota

Western dietary patterns combining saturated fat and cholesterol synergistically drive fibrosing MASH through gut microbiota-dependent mechanisms.² In specific pathogen-free mice, both dietary cholesterol and saturated fat are required to induce fibrosing MASH, whereas germ-free mice lacking intestinal microbiota remain protected despite identical dietary exposure. Each dietary component independently alters gut microbial community structure, potentially via altered bile acid metabolism, but their combination promotes a distinct dysbiotic composition enriched in Parasutterella species that correlates directly with hepatic fibrosis severity.² Cecal contents from mice fed high-fat, high-cholesterol diets contain elevated deoxycholic acid and activate human hepatic stellate cells in vitro, mechanistically linking dietary lipid-induced microbiota shifts to liver fibrogenesis. These findings reveal how specific Western dietary components shape the gut microbiota and contribute to hepatic fibrosis via stellate cell activation.²

---

## Macrophage remodeling

Disease progression from simple steatosis to MASH is characterized by profound remodeling of the hepatic macrophage compartment.⁴ Integrated single-nucleus transcriptomics and spatial multi-omics analyses of human liver samples reveal progressive depletion of tissue-resident Kupffer cells accompanied by emergence of diverse, phenotypically distinct macrophage subsets during MASH development. Spatial profiling demonstrates that advanced disease is marked by accumulation of antigen-presenting, phagocytic GPNMB-positive macrophages displaying adaptive metabolic and pro-inflammatory phenotypes.⁴ These infiltrating macrophages are supported by IL-32-producing hepatocytes and exhibit tight spatial regulation that varies by disease stage. The identified macrophage markers enable stratification of patients by disease activity and stage across independent clinical cohorts, highlighting the central role of innate immune remodeling in MASH pathogenesis.⁴

---

## Cardiovascular complications

MASH-associated metabolic disruptions extend beyond the liver to drive concurrent cardiovascular dysfunction through altered sphingolipid metabolism.⁶ In the PWK/PhJ mouse strain, a robust model for severe MASH that closely mimics the clinical and molecular profile of human disease, chronic Western diet exposure induces hepatic fibrosis alongside cardiac dysfunction. The metabolic perturbations include disrupted amino acid and mitochondrial metabolism, which increase hepatic synthesis of ceramides and deoxy-ceramides. These sphingolipids accumulate in plasma and heart tissue, contributing to elevated cardiovascular disease risk.⁶ Early metabolic changes are characterized by elevated cholesterol levels and altered transcript profiles associated with translation and lipid metabolism, while chronic exposure exacerbates hepatic lipid accumulation, inflammation, and fibrosis. This cardio-metabolic cross-talk establishes MASLD not as an isolated hepatic disorder but as a systemic metabolic disease with multi-organ consequences.⁶

## Clinical management

Advanced [[fibrosis-4-index|fibrosis]] represents the major determinant of liver-related morbidity and mortality in MASLD, making early risk stratification essential for preventing disease-related events and improving long-term outcomes.³ The [[fibrosis-4-index|Fibrosis-4 (FIB-4) index]] is a widely accessible and reliable first-line noninvasive tool for fibrosis risk stratification in clinical practice, enabling identification of high-risk individuals without invasive [[liver-biopsy|biopsy]]. Patients with elevated [[fibrosis-4-index|FIB-4]] values or clinical features suggestive of progressive liver injury should undergo secondary assessment with [[elastography]] and be referred to gastroenterology or hepatology specialists when appropriate.³ While [[liver-biopsy|liver biopsy]] is not routinely required for MASLD diagnosis, it remains essential for establishing a definitive diagnosis of at-risk MASH, assessing inflammatory activity, and guiding therapeutic decisions in selected cases. Evidence-based guidelines emphasize systematic screening of at-risk populations, comprehensive metabolic assessment, and integrated management of cardiovascular and hepatic complications.³

---

## Therapeutic approaches

[[statins|Statin therapy]] has emerged as a protective intervention against hepatocellular carcinoma development in MASLD patients.⁵ Long-term statin use, defined as at least 180 cumulative days over four years, significantly reduces HCC risk in MASLD patients to levels comparable to individuals without steatotic liver disease. While the MASLD group without statin exposure exhibits elevated HCC risk compared to non-steatotic controls, those receiving long-term [[statins|statins]] demonstrate substantially lower HCC incidence, suggesting that prolonged statin therapy may mitigate the oncogenic impact of hepatic steatosis.⁵ These findings from large-scale national cohort studies support a protective effect of [[statins|statins]] against HCC regardless of MASLD status, though further clinical trials are needed to validate optimal dosing strategies and patient selection criteria for HCC prevention in MASLD populations.⁵
