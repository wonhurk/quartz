**Stroke** is a major cerebrovascular disease resulting from disruption of the [[blood-brain-barrier|blood-brain barrier (BBB)]] and [[cerebral-small-vessel-disease|cerebral small vessel disease (CSVD)]], both critical factors in cerebrovascular pathology. The BBB functions as a dynamic, evolving system whose properties vary across the lifespan—acquisitive during early development, heterogeneous in adulthood, and increasingly fragile with aging—directly influencing stroke susceptibility.¹ [[cerebral-small-vessel-disease|CSVD]], identified as a primary cause of both stroke and [[vascular-dementia|dementia]], is characterized by multiple pathological lesions including white matter hyperintensity, lacunes, cerebral microbleeds, enlarged perivascular spaces, and brain atrophy.² Accelerated biological aging, assessed through vascular and metabolic biomarkers, is increasingly recognized as a key risk factor associated with both the presence and longitudinal progression of CSVD and its neuroimaging manifestations.²

---

## Pathophysiology

The cerebrovascular dysfunction underlying stroke operates through two intimately related mechanisms: [[blood-brain-barrier|BBB]] integrity and [[cerebral-small-vessel-disease|CSVD]] burden. The [[blood-brain-barrier|BBB]] maintains selective permeability to protect neural tissue from circulating toxins and inflammatory mediators, but this protective function is not static across the lifespan. During early development, the barrier is acquisitive—progressively tightening its junctions and refining transporter expression to establish adult barrier function.¹ In adulthood, the [[blood-brain-barrier|BBB]] exhibits regional heterogeneity, with barrier properties varying by vascular territory and local neuronal demand.¹ With advancing age, however, the barrier becomes progressively fragile, compromising its ability to exclude harmful blood-borne substances and contributing directly to age-related neurological dysfunction.¹ This age-dependent [[blood-brain-barrier|BBB]] fragility substantially increases vulnerability to stroke and [[vascular-dementia|vascular dementia]].

[[cerebral-small-vessel-disease|CSVD]] represents the pathological manifestation of chronic disruption within the cerebrovasculature. Characteristic lesions include white matter hyperintensity, reflecting demyelination and axonal loss; lacunes, small subcortical infarcts from chronic hypoperfusion; cerebral microbleeds, indicating blood-brain barrier rupture and microvessel integrity failure; enlarged perivascular spaces, reflecting glial dysfunction and impaired fluid clearance; and progressive brain atrophy.² These lesions accumulate through repetitive episodes of microinfarction, microhemorrhage, and inflammatory insult, progressively eroding cognitive reserve and motor function.²

## Biological aging and cerebrovascular risk

Chronological age alone inadequately captures stroke and [[cerebral-small-vessel-disease|CSVD]] risk. Biomarker-based biological age, derived from routinely measured clinical indicators and predominantly reflecting cumulative vascular and metabolic physiologic burden, provides more nuanced risk stratification beyond calendar years.² Individuals with accelerated biological aging—characterized by elevated vascular stress markers, systemic dysmetabolism, and chronic inflammation—exhibit significantly higher prevalence of [[cerebral-small-vessel-disease|CSVD]] lesions and more rapid neuroimaging progression.² This biology-based framework shifts stroke prevention from chronology-centered to physiology-centered approaches, enabling identification of high-risk individuals regardless of age and informing personalized intervention strategies.

---

## Lifespan-based prevention and management

Effective vascular strategies for stroke prevention must account for the dynamic nature of [[blood-brain-barrier|BBB]] function across development and aging. In pediatric populations, understanding the developmental [[blood-brain-barrier|BBB]]'s acquisitive properties is essential for safe central nervous system drug dosing, as the immature barrier exhibits different permeability characteristics than adult or aged barriers.¹ In adulthood, therapeutic approaches should leverage regional [[blood-brain-barrier|BBB]] heterogeneity to target vulnerable vascular territories. In aging populations, strategies must directly address progressive [[blood-brain-barrier|BBB]] fragility through vascular stabilization, anti-inflammatory interventions, and management of underlying metabolic dysfunction.¹ These lifespan-informed vascular strategies are essential for preserving cognitive health and preventing stroke-related dementia across all age groups.
