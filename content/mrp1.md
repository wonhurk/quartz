**MRP1** (Multidrug Resistance-Associated Protein 1, encoded by [[abcc1]]) is an ATP-binding cassette transporter that mediates the ATP-driven efflux of reduced glutathione and conjugated xenobiotics from cells, functioning as a central regulator of intracellular redox homeostasis and inflammatory signaling.¹ The transporter integrates purinergic danger signaling through the [[p2x7r]] receptor with cellular antioxidant metabolism, translating extracellular ATP engagement into rapid glutathione depletion and downstream immune activation. Activation of MRP1 downstream of [[p2x7r]] via [[pi3k]] and [[akt1]] kinase cascades drives intracellular GSH efflux, which depletes the cellular antioxidant pool and triggers neutral sphingomyelinase ([[nsmase]]) activation, thereby linking redox regulation to exosome-mediated immune responses.

---

## Mechanism

MRP1-mediated glutathione efflux is initiated upon stimulation of [[p2x7r]], the P2X7 purinergic receptor, by extracellular ATP and ATP analogues such as BzATP. P2X7R signaling activates the [[pi3k]]/[[akt1]] phosphoinositide kinase cascade, which translocates to the cell membrane and phosphorylates downstream effectors.¹ This phosphorylation event directly stimulates MRP1, driving rapid, ATP-dependent efflux of intracellular reduced glutathione (iGSH) into the extracellular space. In neutrophil-like cells, MRP1-mediated GSH efflux rapidly depletes intracellular pools of the critical antioxidant, uncoupling the cell's defense against reactive oxygen species generation.¹

The intracellular GSH depletion induced by MRP1 activity triggers a second signaling cascade: activation of neutral sphingomyelinase ([[nsmase]]), an enzyme catalyzing the hydrolysis of sphingomyelin to ceramide and phosphocholine. The accumulated ceramide localizes to the membranes of intraluminal vesicles within multivesicular endosomes, promoting vesicle budding and intraluminal accumulation. This ceramide-dependent mechanism drives the formation and release of exosomes — endosome-derived extracellular vesicles — linking redox homeostasis to exosome biogenesis and extracellular signaling.¹

---

## Exosome biogenesis and immune signaling

In differentiated neutrophil-like cells, the MRP1-nSMase-ceramide pathway represents a previously uncharacterized mechanism of exosome biogenesis coupled to purinergic immune sensing. When stimulated with the [[p2x7r]] agonist BzATP, cells undergo rapid, dose-dependent exosome release that correlates with iGSH depletion and nSMase activation.¹ Pharmacological inhibition of nSMase completely abrogates BzATP-induced exosome secretion, demonstrating the sufficiency of ceramide-dependent intraluminal vesicle formation for exosome release. Conversely, blockade of [[pi3k]], [[akt1]], or MRP1 itself prevents both GSH efflux and exosome secretion, establishing MRP1 as a rate-limiting node in this pathway.¹ Exosomes released through this mechanism carry CD63 and other neutrophil-associated markers, fulfill MISEV 2023 criteria for extracellular vesicles, and are proposed to regulate immune responses to danger-associated ATP signals.

---

## Drug resistance in EBV-positive lymphomas

Beyond its role in redox regulation and exosome biogenesis, MRP1 functions as a major cellular drug resistance transporter in [[ebv]]-positive [[nhl|non-Hodgkin lymphoma]]. In EBV-positive NHL, MRP1 expression is markedly elevated and represents the most active xenobiotic efflux system among cellular drug resistance transporters.² The EBV latency proteins [[lmp1]] and [[ebna1]] indirectly upregulate MRP1 expression through the cellular kinase [[nek2]], which drives both lymphoma cell survival and chemoresistance.² Inhibition of NEK2 suppresses MRP1 expression and activity, resulting in decreased efflux of chemotherapeutic drugs, increased intracellular drug accumulation, and enhanced chemosensitivity of lymphoma cells.² This pathway suggests that combination approaches targeting [[nek2]] alongside conventional chemotherapy may overcome MRP1-mediated multidrug resistance and improve outcomes in EBV-driven lymphomas, which are otherwise highly aggressive and therapeutically refractory.

---

## Therapeutic targeting

The dual role of MRP1 in redox homeostasis and drug resistance identifies the transporter as a viable therapeutic target across multiple disease contexts. In EBV-positive NHL, MRP1 inhibition potentiates chemotherapy sensitivity and may be coupled with [[nek2]] inhibitors to suppress both survival signals and drug efflux.² More broadly, blockade of the [[p2x7r]]-[[pi3k]]-[[akt1]]-MRP1 axis in immune cells could modulate exosome release and alter inflammatory signaling without completely suppressing antioxidant defense. Small-molecule MRP1 inhibitors, including structurally diverse classes of competitive and non-competitive blockers, offer strategies to sensitize resistant tumors to conventional therapy while potentially modulating immune cell function in inflammatory contexts.
