**Dextran sulfate sodium (DSS)** is a water-soluble polysaccharide chemical widely employed as a reproducible chemical inducer of acute colitis in mice, serving as the standard experimental model for human [[ulcerative-colitis|ulcerative colitis (UC)]].¹ DSS administration produces acute colonic inflammation, epithelial barrier dysfunction, and dysbiosis of the intestinal microbiota that recapitulate key pathological features of human UC.² The DSS model generates quantifiable, reproducible measures of disease severity—including colonic shortening, weight loss, diarrhea, and fecal occult blood—enabling systematic evaluation of novel therapeutic candidates and dose-response characterization before clinical advancement.

---

## Mechanism

DSS-induced acute colitis is characterized by colonic inflammation, epithelial barrier dysfunction, and dysbiosis of the intestinal microbiota.² The inflammatory phenotype includes elevated production of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and interleukin-17 (IL-17), combined with reduced populations of commensal bacteria that produce short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), particularly butyrate.² Successful therapeutic interventions ameliorate these pathological features by restoring epithelial barrier integrity, suppressing pro-inflammatory cytokine expression, and normalizing microbial composition and SCFA production, demonstrating the model's utility for both phenotypic drug screening and mechanistic studies of UC pathogenesis.²

---

## Preclinical model utility

The DSS model has become the standard platform for evaluating therapeutic efficacy and safety in ulcerative colitis drug discovery because of its high reproducibility, dose-dependent disease induction, and reversibility.¹ Unlike more genetically complex animal models, DSS-induced colitis can be induced and resolved within two to three weeks, enabling rapid characterization of dose-response relationships and efficacy comparison across therapeutic candidates. Objective disease readouts—including colon weight-to-length ratio, histological inflammation scores, serum cytokine concentrations, and microbiota composition—provide quantifiable measures of disease severity and treatment response. The model's predictive capacity for clinical translation is supported by concordance between preclinical DSS efficacy and approved UC medications; the novel S1PR1 agonist TYY-31 achieves therapeutic efficacy at 0.3 mg/kg in DSS-induced colitis, substantially lower than the established drug [[ozanimod]] at 1 mg/kg, validating the model's utility as a gating step for clinical advancement.¹

---

## S1PR1 agonist efficacy

Selective agonists of [[s1pr1|sphingosine-1-phosphate receptor 1 (S1PR1)]] represent a clinically validated therapeutic strategy for UC, and the DSS model has proven essential for evaluating their potency, selectivity, and safety profiles. The compound TYY-31, a 1,2,4-oxadiazole-based S1PR1 agonist, demonstrates single-digit picomolar activity against [[s1pr1|S1PR1]] (EC₅₀ = 1.13 pM) with minimal cross-reactivity against [[s1pr2|S1PR2]], [[s1pr3|S1PR3]], and [[s1pr5|S1PR5]] (EC₅₀ > 10 μM) and over 30,000-fold selectivity relative to [[s1pr4|S1PR4]].¹ TYY-31 efficiently induces [[s1pr1|S1PR1]] internalization and blocks receptor recycling, mechanisms that contribute to its pharmacological activity.¹ In DSS-induced colitis, TYY-31 achieves therapeutic efficacy at 0.3 mg/kg—substantially more potent than [[ozanimod]] at 1 mg/kg—while maintaining excellent cardiac safety profiles in zebrafish-based assays.¹ This enhanced selectivity for [[s1pr1|S1PR1]] over [[s1pr3|S1PR3]] is mechanistically important because [[s1pr3|S1PR3]] activation in cardiac tissue contributes to adverse effects observed with non-selective S1PR agonists, underscoring how the DSS model enables rational optimization of receptor selectivity for improved tolerability and clinical translatability.

## Nanoparticle-based delivery systems

Recent advances in colon-targeted nanomedicine leverage the DSS model to evaluate sophisticated delivery platforms that maximize local drug concentration while minimizing systemic exposure and toxicity. Butyrate-conjugated poly(vinyl alcohol) nanoparticles embedded within inulin hydrogel matrices represent a composite delivery system constructed from Generally Recognized As Safe (GRAS) materials that efficiently encapsulate the natural anti-inflammatory compound ursolic acid (UA) and respond to colonic esterase activity, enabling localized release of both the active agent and the anti-inflammatory short-chain fatty acid butyrate.² This nanoparticle-hydrogel system demonstrates prolonged colonic retention and potent anti-inflammatory effects in DSS-induced colitis, effectively alleviating colonic inflammation, restoring epithelial barrier integrity, and suppressing pro-inflammatory cytokine expression while modulating gut short-chain fatty acid levels.² The biocompatibility and enhanced colon specificity of GRAS material-based nanoparticles position such platforms as promising alternatives to systemic therapies for chronic UC management, providing an evidence-based rationale for advancing such systems to clinical evaluation.
