**Caspase-3 (CASP3)** is an apoptotic cysteine protease and one of five key molecular targets identified through network pharmacology and molecular docking in the [[nadph]]-mediated neuroprotective pathway against ischemic stroke. As an executioner caspase, CASP3 is activated downstream of pro-apoptotic signals to orchestrate irreversible apoptotic cascades through substrate cleavage. In the context of cerebral ischemia, CASP3 represents a critical control point where [[nadph]] intervention preserves neuronal viability by suppressing caspase activation while simultaneously reinforcing blood-brain barrier integrity and promoting angiogenesis.¹

---

## Mechanism

Ischemic stroke initiates a multi-pathway insult wherein cerebral hypoxia triggers both apoptotic and inflammatory cascades. CASP3 occupies a central position in this network of death signaling, and its activation is suppressed by the concerted action of four co-target proteins: [[hif-1a]] stabilization promotes hypoxia adaptation and anti-apoptotic signaling, [[src]] kinase modulates survival pathways, [[nlrp-3]] inflammasome inhibition curtails caspase-1-driven inflammatory cytokine release, and [[akt-1]] activation drives cell survival signaling. The five-target engagement by [[nadph]] creates a multivalent neuroprotective effect that extends beyond simple CASP3 inhibition to encompass coordinated preservation of tissue integrity and restoration of oxygen delivery.¹

---

## Ischemic stroke neuroprotection

[[nadph]] treatment in transient middle cerebral artery occlusion (tMCAO) models demonstrates substantial neuroprotective efficacy at an optimal dose of 7.5 mg/kg, achieving approximately 50% reduction in infarct volume and 58% improvement in neurological function scores.¹ The protection is coupled with attenuation of cerebral edema, declining from 81% to 76% brain water content. This efficacy is mechanistically linked to CASP3 suppression alongside downregulation of inflammatory mediators including interleukin-1β, and enhancement of pro-survival [[akt-1]] signaling. The identification of CASP3 as a key target through network pharmacology validates its centrality to the ischemic cascade and positions [[nadph]] as a multi-target therapeutic agent rather than a single-mechanism intervention.

---

## Blood-brain barrier preservation

The blood-brain barrier (BBB) is structurally and functionally compromised during cerebral ischemia through caspase-dependent proteolysis of tight junction proteins and increased vascular permeability. [[nadph]] therapy, acting through CASP3 suppression and complementary mechanisms, upregulates the tight junction protein [[zo-1]] by approximately 29%, with electron microscopy directly confirming strengthened tight junction ultrastructure and restored paracellular integrity.¹ Concurrently, CASP3 and related proteolytic pathways are inhibited, reducing matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP9) expression by 23% — a key enzyme responsible for extracellular matrix degradation and BBB compromise. The reduction of [[caveolin-1]] by approximately 50% further limits transcytosis-mediated vascular leakage. This multi-level BBB reinforcement is essential for preventing secondary brain injury, edema expansion, and hemorrhagic transformation in the post-ischemic period.

---

## Angiogenesis promotion

In parallel with CASP3 suppression and BBB preservation, [[nadph]] stimulates endothelial cell proliferation and angiogenesis within the ischemic penumbra.¹ This pro-angiogenic effect works synergistically with BBB maintenance to restore perfusion while preventing pathological vascular leakage. The combination of anti-apoptotic signaling (via CASP3 inhibition and [[akt-1]] activation) and tissue-level vascular expansion reflects a coordinated tissue recovery response, addressing both the immediate threat of neuronal loss and the longer-term restoration of metabolic support.

---

## Non-stroke contexts

Beyond ischemic stroke, CASP3 activity is regulated in diverse biological systems. In male mammalian reproduction, astaxanthin-loaded chitosan nanoparticles (ASX-CNPs) have been shown to enhance semen cryopreservation by downregulating the pro-apoptotic gene CASP3 alongside Bax, thereby reducing apoptotic and necrotic spermatozoa and improving post-thaw sperm viability and acrosomal integrity.² This parallel reduction of CASP3 across distinct tissues underscores the centrality of apoptotic regulation to cellular stress responses more broadly.
