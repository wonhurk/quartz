**Quadrivalent Influenza Vaccine (QIV)** is an inactivated vaccine formulation providing immunological coverage against four circulating influenza strains: both major subtypes of influenza A (H1N1 and H3N2) and both lineages of influenza B (Victoria and Yamagata). The vaccine elicits robust strain-specific humoral immunity and, notably, can generate broadly neutralizing monoclonal antibodies—including [[cav-cf22|CAV-CF22]] and [[cav-ch76|CAV-CH76]]—that confer cross-lineage protection against antigenically drifted influenza B viruses despite substantial hemagglutinin divergence.¹ However, recent comparative immunogenicity studies reveal that newer RNA vaccine platforms, particularly self-amplifying RNA (saRNA) formulations at substantially reduced doses, exceed QIV's protective efficacy while offering significant dose-sparing advantages over the standard 2 μg QIV formulation.²

---

## Vaccine composition and immunological basis

QIV contains inactivated viral antigens from four seasonal influenza strains, administered at a standard 2 μg dose per strain component. The inclusion of both influenza B lineages addresses a critical epidemiological challenge: Victoria and Yamagata lineages circulate seasonally with limited cross-reactive immunity, necessitating representation of both lineages in vaccine formulations. Vaccination drives B cell responses to hemagglutinin and neuraminidase antigens, generating both strain-specific antibodies for immediate protection and, in some individuals, cross-reactive antibodies with broader neutralizing potential. The four-strain design optimizes seasonal coverage while creating an immunological context conducive to development of broadly protective responses.

---

## Broadly neutralizing antibodies and antigenic drift resistance

A defining feature of QIV-elicited immunity is its capacity to generate broadly neutralizing monoclonal antibodies that remain protective despite substantial influenza B antigenic drift. The antibodies [[cav-cf22|CAV-CF22]] and [[cav-ch76|CAV-CH76]], isolated following QIV vaccination, exhibit broad neutralizing activity against both Victoria- and Yamagata-lineage viruses in vitro and conferred protection in vivo against contemporary strains of both lineages.¹ These antibodies achieve their broad cross-lineage neutralizing activity through an elegant molecular mechanism: the heavy-chain complementarity-determining region 3 (HCDR3) inserts directly into the viral receptor-binding site to sterically mimic sialic acid, the natural viral attachment determinant.¹ This HCDR3-RBS interaction strategy enables recognition of epitope residues conserved across Victoria and Yamagata lineages, conferring protection even against post-2019 hemagglutinin variants bearing the K136E substitution that disrupts traditional head-directed antibody epitopes.¹ The robust in vivo protection demonstrated by CAV-CF22 and CAV-CH76 in murine challenge models suggests that QIV-elicited broadly neutralizing antibodies contribute meaningfully to durable, multi-strain immunity in vaccinees.

## Comparative immunogenicity and emerging RNA platforms

While QIV remains the standard-of-care vaccine for seasonal influenza protection, comparative immunogenicity studies demonstrate that newer RNA vaccine technologies achieve superior efficacy at substantially lower doses. Trivalent influenza A mRNA vaccines administered at 0.1 μg—a twenty-fold reduction compared to standard QIV dosing—induced robust humoral immunity and conferred complete protection against homologous viral challenge, outperforming the standard 2 μg QIV formulation in preclinical models.² However, conventional mRNA vaccines exhibited suboptimal immunogenicity against influenza B virus, with the 0.1 μg dose failing to elicit detectable antibodies or protection.² This lineage-specific limitation prompted development of self-amplifying RNA (saRNA) vaccine platforms, which employ viral replicase sequences to amplify antigen expression. A single 0.1 μg dose of trivalent saRNA vaccine targeting influenza A and B strains elicited robust humoral immunity and provided complete protection against influenza B virus challenge—a protective outcome unachieved by conventional mRNA vaccines at equivalent doses.² Long-term antibody monitoring over 20 weeks revealed that saRNA vaccines maintained high antibody titers at the low 0.1 μg dose with markedly more durable responses, suggesting potential advantages for sustained seasonal protection.²

## Pandemic preparedness and vaccine optimization

QIV's demonstrated capacity to generate broadly neutralizing antibodies against antigenically drifted influenza B viruses has important implications for seasonal influenza vaccine design and pandemic preparedness strategies. The molecular features underlying CAV-CF22 and CAV-CH76 neutralization—particularly the HCDR3-RBS interaction strategy—inform antibody engineering and immunogen design efforts aimed at eliciting more broadly protective responses in future vaccine formulations. Concurrently, the superior immunogenicity and dose-sparing potential of saRNA platforms suggest a pathway toward next-generation quadrivalent or extended-valence influenza vaccines that combine enhanced protective breadth with reduced manufacturing burden and improved accessibility. Integration of saRNA technology with QIV-inspired strain selection and molecular insights gained from broadly neutralizing antibody studies may yield influenza vaccines with improved efficacy, durability, and coverage against circulating seasonal and pandemic variants.
