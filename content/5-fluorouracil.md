**5-Fluorouracil (5-FU)** is a first-line nucleoside analog chemotherapy agent widely used to treat multiple cancer types including [[colorectal-cancer|colorectal cancer]], [[gastric-cancer|gastric cancer]], [[pancreatic-ductal-adenocarcinoma|pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma]], and [[rectal-cancer|rectal cancer]]. The drug exerts its anticancer effects primarily through induction of G1 phase cell cycle arrest and apoptosis in malignant cells.¹ Despite its efficacy, acquired resistance to 5-FU remains a significant clinical challenge that limits therapeutic outcomes and directly correlates with poor overall survival in multiple cancer types.¹²

---

## Mechanism

5-Fluorouracil disrupts cancer cell proliferation by interfering with DNA and RNA synthesis, ultimately triggering G1 phase cell cycle arrest and programmed cell death. The agent is most frequently deployed in combination regimens such as FOLFIRI—which combines 5-FU with folinic acid and [[irinotecan]]—and in concert with targeted monoclonal antibodies including [[cetuximab]] for metastatic disease.⁵ In [[pancreatic-ductal-adenocarcinoma|pancreatic cancer]], 5-FU is often combined with [[gemcitabine]] to enhance therapeutic efficacy by suppressing clonogenic potential, epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), and cancer stem cell phenotypes.³

---

## Clinical applications

5-Fluorouracil serves as a backbone chemotherapy across multiple gastrointestinal malignancies. In [[colorectal-cancer|colorectal cancer]], it is used both as monotherapy and in combination protocols, with FOLFIRI plus [[cetuximab]] demonstrating efficacy in metastatic settings when guided by circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) monitoring.⁵ For [[gastric-cancer|gastric cancer]] patients, 5-FU represents a standard-of-care option, though therapeutic response is often limited by intrinsic or acquired chemoresistance.² In [[pancreatic-ductal-adenocarcinoma|pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma]]—a lethal malignancy with a five-year survival rate of approximately 13%—5-FU is combined with gemcitabine and targeted agents to suppress tumor growth, proliferation, and stemness in KRAS-driven disease.³ In [[rectal-cancer|rectal cancer]], 5-FU-based neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy is a standard preoperative strategy, with treatment response assessable via methylated DNA biomarkers.⁴

---

## Resistance mechanisms

Acquired resistance to 5-FU is multifactorial and driven by distinct molecular pathways across cancer types. In [[colorectal-cancer|colorectal cancer]], euchromatic histone lysine methyltransferase 2 ([[ehmt2|EHMT2]]) is significantly upregulated in patients with poor responses to 5-FU, directly correlating with lower overall survival rates.¹ EHMT2 overexpression promotes resistance by suppressing protein phosphatase 1B ([[ppm1b|PPM1B]]), a key downstream effector that when lost impairs cell cycle regulation and apoptosis.¹ In [[gastric-cancer|gastric cancer]], the cell surface protein [[kirrel1|KIRREL1]] is significantly upregulated in tumor tissues and actively promotes proliferation, migration, invasion, and 5-FU chemoresistance through activation of the epithelial-mesenchymal transition pathway, specifically by upregulating Vimentin, MMP2, and MMP9.² In [[pancreatic-ductal-adenocarcinoma|pancreatic cancer]], the RNA helicase [[ddx3|DDX3]] interacts with [[paf1|PAF1]]—a component of the RNA polymerase II-associated factor 1 complex—to promote cancer stemness and resistance to both 5-FU and gemcitabine, with both proteins exhibiting progressively increased expression across disease stages.³

---

## Overcoming resistance

Targeting the molecular drivers of 5-FU resistance through rational combination strategies has demonstrated synergistic tumor suppression across multiple cancer types. In [[colorectal-cancer|colorectal cancer]], pharmacological inhibition of [[ehmt2|EHMT2]] using BIX-01294 in combination with 5-FU effectively restored drug sensitivity in resistant cell lines by upregulating [[ppm1b|PPM1B]], inducing G1 phase arrest, and triggering apoptosis.¹ This therapeutic strategy synergistically suppressed tumor growth in 5-FU-resistant xenograft mouse models and was faithfully recapitulated in patient-derived colorectal cancer organoid (PDO) models.¹ In [[pancreatic-ductal-adenocarcinoma|pancreatic cancer]], the small-molecule inhibitor RK-33—which targets [[ddx3|DDX3]]—significantly mitigated clonogenic potential, EMT, and stemness phenotypes when combined with 5-FU and gemcitabine, reducing tumor growth and proliferation while increasing apoptosis in xenograft and organoid models.³ Mechanistically, RK-33 treatment identified MXRA5, EDIL3, COL13A1, and SLC16A2 as top downstream response genes, offering potential new targets to disrupt extracellular matrix remodeling, angiogenesis, cell migration, and cell cycle progression.³ In [[gastric-cancer|gastric cancer]], knockdown of [[kirrel1|KIRREL1]] sensitized cells to 5-FU treatment by suppressing EMT and invasive phenotypes.²

---

## Clinical monitoring

Circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) biomarkers have emerged as powerful tools for predicting and assessing response to 5-FU-based therapy in real time. In [[rectal-cancer|rectal cancer]] patients receiving neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy, a ctDNA assay measuring methylated BCAT1 and IKZF1 demonstrated 87% sensitivity for detecting untreated disease, with methylation levels significantly reduced in tumor tissues from good responders following treatment.⁴ Radiation treatment and 5-FU exposure did not affect BCAT1/IKZF1 methylation in vitro, confirming that reductions in ctDNA levels reflect true tumor response rather than treatment-induced epigenetic changes.⁴ In metastatic [[colorectal-cancer|colorectal cancer]] patients treated with FOLFIRI plus [[cetuximab]], longitudinal ctDNA monitoring enabled assessment of clinical outcomes during both induction and maintenance phases, providing a noninvasive alternative to imaging-based response evaluation.⁵
