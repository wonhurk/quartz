**Dysbiosis** is a disruption of the composition and functional homeostasis of the gut microbiota that impairs colonization resistance and compromises multiple aspects of host health.¹ The condition results from profound alterations to microbial community structure, driven commonly by environmental insults such as antibiotic therapy, and recovery from dysbiosis is substantially modulated by host genetic factors, particularly the blood-group-related glycosyltransferase [[b4galnt2]].¹ Beyond antibiotic-induced dysbiosis, the condition plays a central pathogenic role in metabolic and inflammatory diseases, particularly metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH), where dysbiosis-driven overgrowth of ammonia-producing pathogenic bacteria, especially [[clostridium-perfringens]], triggers hepatic inflammation and fibrosis through a distinct gut-liver axis mechanism.²

---

## Mechanism

Dysbiosis fundamentally represents a loss of microbial diversity and an imbalance in the relative abundances of the constituent bacterial taxa that comprise a healthy microbiota. This altered state compromises colonization resistance — the capacity of resident microbiota to exclude pathogenic bacteria through competitive exclusion and metabolic antagonism. Dysbiosis can be initiated by diverse environmental factors, of which antibiotic therapy is perhaps the most common and well-characterized trigger. Broad-spectrum antibiotics such as streptomycin, kanamycin, and vancomycin indiscriminately disrupt microbial homeostasis by reducing populations of susceptible commensal bacteria, thereby creating an ecological vacuum that can be recolonized by dysbiosis-promoting and sometimes pathogenic taxa.¹ The molecular signatures of dysbiosis include altered expression of bacterial antibiotic resistance genes and suppression of genes encoding bacterial motility and resource acquisition, reflecting fundamental changes in the selective pressures and metabolic landscape of the intestinal ecosystem.

## Antibiotic-induced dysbiosis and recovery

Antibiotic-induced dysbiosis can be experimentally induced through administration of aminoglycoside antibiotics such as streptomycin and kanamycin, or glycopeptide antibiotics such as vancomycin, which produce rapid and severe microbiota disruption.¹ The recovery of the microbiota following antibiotic withdrawal follows a reproducible trajectory, characterized by gradual restoration of microbial diversity and replenishment of taxa depleted during antibiotic exposure. However, the kinetics and completeness of recovery are not invariant across hosts — rather, post-antibiotic microbiota restoration is substantially shaped by host genetic factors. The blood-group-related glycosyltransferase [[b4galnt2]] exerts striking genetic control over post-antibiotic recovery.¹ In mice with homozygous knockout of B4galnt2, microbiota recovery following streptomycin treatment is significantly accelerated compared to heterozygous controls, with B4galnt2-/- mice exhibiting faster restoration of microbial diversity and altered composition.¹ The recovered microbiota in B4galnt2-/- mice is enriched in Lachnospiraceae family members, particularly the genera Blautia and Dorea, which are characteristic of a healthy, diverse microbiota.¹ This accelerated recovery is accompanied by differential regulation of antibiotic resistance genes, including the aminoglycoside nucleotidyltransferase genes aadA and aadE, which contribute to selective advantages for specific bacterial taxa during the post-antibiotic period.¹ Importantly, the genotype-dependent effects of B4galnt2 on recovery are most pronounced following streptomycin treatment and are less consistently observed with kanamycin or vancomycin exposure, indicating that recovery is influenced by an antibiotic-by-genotype interaction shaped by the specific composition of the dysbiotic microbiota each antibiotic produces.

## Dysbiosis in metabolic liver disease

Dysbiosis plays a central and causal role in the pathogenesis of metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH), an increasingly prevalent liver disease characterized by hepatic steatosis, inflammation, and progressive fibrosis. In MASH, dysbiosis is associated with a marked increase in the abundance of the gram-positive pathogenic bacterium [[clostridium-perfringens]], an ammonia-producing organism that disrupts intestinal barrier integrity and triggers hepatic inflammation through a distinct immune mechanism.² The elevated ammonia produced by dysbiotic, C. perfringens-enriched microbiota crosses the compromised intestinal epithelial barrier and reaches systemic circulation, where it exerts profound effects on hepatic immune regulation.² Specifically, elevated intestinal and systemic ammonia triggers FosB-mediated upregulation of the chemokine C-C motif ligand 5 (CCL5) in [[cd8-t-cells|CD8+ T cells]], leading to enhanced CD8+ T cell cytotoxicity within the liver and exacerbating hepatic inflammation and fibrosis.² This mechanism represents a distinct gut-liver axis pathway whereby dysbiosis-driven dysregulation of microbial ammonia metabolism drives CD8+ T cell activation and hepatic pathology. The causality of this pathway has been verified through multiple lines of evidence, including microbiota transplantation experiments, use of C. perfringens NirA-knockout mutants that are incapable of ammonia production, and in vitro and in vivo functional validation studies in both non-human primate and murine MASH models.²

---

## Therapeutic interventions

Recent therapeutic strategies have targeted dysbiosis-related pathogenic mechanisms to attenuate MASH severity. The glycine-based tripeptide DT-109 represents a novel and effective approach to dysbiosis-driven MASH by targeting the pathogenic microbial component and restoring barrier homeostasis.² Treatment with DT-109 effectively reduces the abundance of C. perfringens within the dysbiotic microbiota, thereby lowering intestinal ammonia production.² Concurrent with the reduction in ammonia-producing pathogenic bacteria, DT-109 restores intestinal barrier integrity, presumably through both direct effects on epithelial tight junctions and indirect effects mediated by the restoration of commensal bacteria that support barrier function.² Moreover, DT-109 ameliorates dysregulation of CD8+ T cells in the liver, suppressing the FosB/CCL5 axis and reducing CD8+ T cell-mediated cytotoxicity.² The efficacy of DT-109 has been validated in both non-human primate and mouse models of MASH, suggesting translational potential for human disease.
