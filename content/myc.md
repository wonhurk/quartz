**MYC** is a proto-oncogene and transcription factor that drives malignant transformation and oncogenic progression across multiple cancer types, including [[diffuse-large-b-cell-lymphoma|diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL)]], breast carcinomas, and prostate cancers. As a central transcriptional hub, MYC coordinates the expression of genes essential for cell proliferation, metabolic reprogramming, and immune evasion. The protein exists as a short-lived oncoprotein whose sustained expression depends critically on the transcriptional elongation factor [[cdk9|cyclin-dependent kinase 9 (CDK9)]].¹ MYC expression is regulated by multiple interconnected pathways: the collagen-integrin signaling axis in the extracellular matrix, [[ebv|Epstein-Barr virus]] latency proteins in viral-associated lymphomas, and histone deacetylase-dependent epigenetic mechanisms. These diverse regulatory inputs position MYC as a central oncogenic node with multiple exploitable therapeutic vulnerabilities.

---

## Mechanism

MYC is essential for transcriptional elongation, coordinating the activation of oncogenic gene programs through its interaction with the super-elongation complex. [[cdk9|CDK9]], a subunit of this complex, phosphorylates the carboxy-terminal domain of RNA polymerase II and is absolutely required for maintaining MYC as a short-lived oncoprotein. In castration-resistant prostate cancer, selective [[cdk9|CDK9]] inhibition through structure-based drug design with Compound 91 disrupts Pol II phosphorylation and rapidly downregulates MYC alongside other short-lived oncoproteins such as androgen receptor variant 7 (AR-V7), achieving significant tumor growth inhibition in orthotopic models.¹

MYC expression is also controlled by extracellular matrix signaling through a collagen-integrin axis. In invasive lobular carcinoma (ILC), the loss of CDH1 renders tumors dependent on a collagen-rich matrix and integrin engagement through [[itgb5|ITGB5]] and [[itgav|ITGAV]]. Inhibition of [[lox|lysyl oxidase (LOX)]], the enzyme responsible for cross-linking and stabilizing fibrillar collagen, disrupts this extracellular matrix-integrin axis, leading to coordinated downregulation of MYC together with [[nf-kb|NF-κB]] and [[ap-1|AP-1]] transcriptional programs.² This collagen-integrin-MYC axis represents a tractable dependency in matrix-dependent tumors, with [[lox|LOX]] inhibition by PXS-5505 serving as both a therapeutic agent and a driver of MYC gene signature suppression as a pharmacodynamic readout of drug activity.

Epigenetic mechanisms further modulate MYC expression through histone acetylation. Histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibitors suppress MYC in the context of MYC/[[bcl2|BCL2]] double-expressor diffuse large B-cell lymphomas, providing a mechanistic rationale for combining epigenetic therapy with standard immunochemotherapy regimens.³

## MYC/BCL2 Double-Expressor Lymphoma

[[myc-bcl2-double-expressor-lymphoma|MYC/BCL2 double-expressor diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DEL)]] represents a distinct and clinically aggressive DLBCL subtype defined by concurrent overexpression of both MYC and [[bcl2|BCL2]]. This population has historically been associated with poor prognosis and treatment resistance following standard [[r-chop|R-CHOP]] (rituximab plus cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, vincristine, and prednisone) immunochemotherapy.³ The dual oncogenic drivers confer aggressive growth kinetics and impaired apoptosis, rendering these lymphomas particularly refractory to conventional approaches. Epigenetic dysregulation is a hallmark of DEL pathogenesis, and selective HDAC inhibition with tucidinostat, an oral histone deacetylase inhibitor, has demonstrated promising activity in this population when combined with [[r-chop|R-CHOP]] therapy. Clinical trials have shown that epigenetic targeting of MYC expression via tucidinostat offers a potential avenue to overcome the historically poor prognosis associated with double-expressor phenotypes and to restore chemotherapy sensitivity in this aggressive lymphoma subset.

## Viral Regulation by EBV Oncoproteins

In [[ebv|EBV]]-positive non-Hodgkin lymphomas, MYC expression is directly upregulated by [[ebv|EBV]] latency proteins including [[lmp1|LMP1]], EBNA1, and EBNA2.⁴ These viral oncoproteins hijack cellular signaling pathways to drive MYC-dependent oncogenic transcription, contributing substantially to the aggressive phenotype of EBV-associated lymphomas. The cellular kinase [[nek2|NEK2]] is also upregulated by [[ebv|EBV]] latency proteins and functions as a critical node downstream of viral infection, maintaining both [[lmp1|LMP1]] expression and MYC-driven lymphoma survival. Inhibition of [[nek2|NEK2]] selectively suppresses MYC expression in EBV-positive NHL cells while simultaneously reducing the expression of the viral oncoprotein [[lmp1|LMP1]], leading to selective lymphoma cell death through reactive oxygen species accumulation and gasdermin D-dependent pyroptosis without toxicity to normal lymphocytes.⁴ This pathway integration positions [[nek2|NEK2]] and MYC as functionally linked oncogenic nodes in EBV-driven lymphomagenesis, suggesting that [[nek2|NEK2]] inhibition represents a therapeutic approach to target MYC in viral-associated malignancies.
