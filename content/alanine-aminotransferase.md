**Alanine aminotransferase (ALT)** is a serum liver enzyme serving as a primary clinical biomarker for assessing hepatic inflammation, injury, and disease severity across multiple liver pathologies. Elevated ALT levels in serum reflect hepatocellular damage and are particularly pronounced in chronic [[hepatitis-b|hepatitis B virus (HBV)]] infection, especially in patients carrying specific viral mutations that disrupt host metabolic pathways.¹ ALT measurement, routinely performed alongside [[ast|aspartate aminotransferase (AST)]], provides genotype-specific diagnostic discrimination and prognostic information for HBV variants and metabolic liver disease.

---

## HBV mutations and disease severity

In chronic HBV infection, ALT elevation is directly linked to specific viral genotypes and mutations. Patients infected with HBV carrying the A1762T/G1764A double mutation or additional G1896A variant exhibit significantly higher serum ALT levels compared to those with wild-type virus, and these elevations correlate robustly with increased disease severity.¹ Multi-omics profiling of chronic HBV patients stratified by viral variant revealed that ALT elevation accompanies substantial proteomic and metabolomic dysregulation, with 54 to 79 differentially expressed proteins identified across mutant groups relative to wild-type infection.¹ This relationship between viral genotype, ALT levels, and molecular pathway disruption underscores ALT's utility as a genotype-specific biomarker for predicting clinical outcomes in HBV infection.

---

## Cholesterol metabolism and integrated biomarkers

Concurrent with elevated ALT and [[ast|AST]], chronic HBV infection—particularly in the context of A1762T/G1764A and double mutant strains—disrupts [[cholesterol-metabolism|cholesterol metabolism]] as the dominant dysregulated pathway in integrated serum proteomics and metabolomics analysis.¹ In this metabolic context, ALT emerges as one of six key protein and metabolite biomarkers capable of distinguishing between HBV variant groups, linking viral genotype to altered host molecular responses and clinical severity. The concurrent elevation of both ALT and metabolic dysfunction indicators suggests that HBV-driven hepatocellular injury, as reflected by ALT release, is mechanistically intertwined with systemic cholesterol dysregulation.

---

## Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis

Beyond viral hepatitis, ALT serves as a critical liver injury marker in metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH) assessment and progression monitoring. In preclinical models of MASH induced by high-fat, choline-deficient dietary regimens, ALT levels correlate positively with disease severity and are used alongside histological assessment to evaluate steatosis, inflammation, and fibrosis. In MASH pathogenesis studies, elevated ALT abundance correlates with increased fecal abundance of candidate microbial pathobionts such as *Turicibacter sanguinis*, which exacerbate hepatic inflammation and injury through disruption of bile acid signaling and hepatic FXR pathways.² The measurement of ALT in both animal disease models and clinical MASH patient cohorts—including pre- and post-treatment timepoints—enables non-invasive assessment of treatment response and disease progression in metabolic liver disease.
