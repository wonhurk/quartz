**Cord blood** (umbilical cord blood, UCB) is a rich source of [[hematopoietic-stem-cells|hematopoietic stem cells]] that can be cryopreserved for long-term clinical use in treating over 80 FDA-approved diseases.¹ The transplantation potential of cord blood units is determined primarily by two cellular markers: total nucleated cell (TNC) count and [[cd34|CD34+]]-positive cell quantity, which serve as key predictive indicators of successful engraftment and clinical outcome.¹ When used in allogeneic [[hsct|hematopoietic stem cell transplantation]], particularly for children with [[inborn-errors-of-immunity|inborn errors of immunity]], cord blood offers a readily available alternative to bone marrow or peripheral blood stem cells, especially for patients without an HLA-matched sibling donor.

---

## Mechanism and cell composition

Cord blood contains a heterogeneous population of hematopoietic and progenitor cells suspended in plasma and collected from the umbilical vein immediately after delivery and placental separation. The [[hematopoietic-stem-cells|hematopoietic stem cells]] within cord blood are characterized by high self-renewal capacity and multi-lineage differentiation potential. Total nucleated cell count reflects the overall cellularity of the collected unit and serves as a gross measure of transplantable cell content. The [[cd34|CD34+]] cell population, defined by expression of the CD34 antigen, enriches for the most primitive long-term repopulating stem cells. Following collection, cord blood is cryopreserved in cryoprotective media (typically dimethyl sulfoxide) and stored at ultra-low temperatures, allowing preservation of cell viability and functionality for decades.¹ The viability and recovery of these cellular compartments directly influence post-transplant hematopoietic recovery and long-term engraftment.

## Allogeneic transplantation in inborn errors of immunity

Cord blood allogeneic transplantation has been established as a curative approach for children with [[inborn-errors-of-immunity|inborn errors of immunity]] who lack HLA-identical siblings.² In patients undergoing first [[hsct|HSCT]] for primary immune reconstitution, cord blood provides a readily accessible stem cell source with acceptable graft failure rates and immunological reconstitution. However, its role in salvage transplantation for [[graft-failure|graft failure]] following a failed primary transplant is more complex. Among 159 children who received a second allogeneic HSCT between 2009 and 2020 for non-SCID inborn errors of immunity, the 5-year overall survival and event-free survival rates were 78% and 69%, respectively.² Notably, patients with primary [[graft-failure|graft failure]] (initial engraftment loss) achieved significantly lower 5-year event-free survival (52%) compared with those experiencing secondary graft failure (75%, P<0.001), underscoring the severity of primary graft failure as a clinical presentation.²

## Conditioning intensity and second transplant outcomes

The intensity of the conditioning regimen substantially influences transplant outcomes in the second [[hsct|HSCT]] setting. Patients who received myeloablative conditioning or reduced-toxicity conditioning achieved significantly improved event-free survival rates (74%) compared with those receiving nonmyeloablative conditioning (50%, P=0.003).² Importantly, this differential was not observed for overall survival, suggesting that conditioning intensity influences disease control and graft failure prevention more significantly than mortality per se. Cord blood emerged as the only independent negative predictor of event-free survival on multivariable analysis (hazard ratio 4.9, 95% confidence interval 1.3–18.7, P=0.020), indicating that cord blood as a donor source for second transplantation is associated with approximately fivefold increased hazard of transplant failure compared to other stem cell sources (bone marrow, peripheral blood, or other tissues).² Post-transplant chimerism was robust, with 84.3% of patients achieving whole-blood chimerism exceeding 90% at last follow-up, and the 1-year cumulative incidence of graft failure after the second [[hsct|HSCT]] was 13%.

## Unit selection and quality optimization

Recognizing the clinical importance of cell dose on transplant success, recent efforts have focused on developing predictive selection models to identify cord blood units with high cellular isolation yields.¹ These models aim to prospectively identify [[umbilical-cord-blood|UCB]] units that will yield high counts of both TNC and [[cd34|CD34+]] cells following processing and cryopreservation.¹ For public stem cell banks, where storage capacity is finite and only the highest-quality units are retained, such selection models are of paramount clinical value in optimizing the pool of available cord blood units for future transplantation. Improved ability to predict processing outcomes and isolation yields enables banks to prioritize cryopreservation of units most likely to meet or exceed clinical thresholds for transplantation, thereby enhancing the quality of available resources for patients requiring emergency or elective allogeneic transplantation.
