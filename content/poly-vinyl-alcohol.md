**Poly(vinyl alcohol) (PVA)** is a biocompatible synthetic polymer and Generally Recognized As Safe (GRAS) material with dual functionality in pharmaceutical delivery and flexible bioelectronics. Its versatility derives from multiple design strategies: in pharmaceutical contexts, butyrate-conjugated PVA nanoparticles respond selectively to colonic esterase for environment-specific drug release; in bioelectronic applications, PVA serves as the structural backbone of dual-network composite hydrogels that integrate gelatin, [[chitosan]], and conductive fillers to achieve simultaneous mechanical robustness and electrical functionality. These complementary properties position PVA as a platform polymer for both therapeutic targeting and next-generation wearable sensor technologies.

---

## Mechanism

PVA's responsiveness in pharmaceutical formulations hinges on enzymatic conjugation and hydrogel embedding. Butyrate-conjugated PVA nanoparticles are designed to remain intact during transit through the upper gastrointestinal tract, where neutral pH and limited esterase activity prevent premature degradation. Upon reaching the colon, resident bacterial and epithelial esterases cleave the butyrate ester linkage, simultaneously liberating the encapsulated therapeutic payload and releasing [[butyrate]], a short-chain fatty acid with intrinsic anti-inflammatory properties.¹ When embedded within an [[inulin]]-based hydrogel matrix, these nanoparticles achieve dual targeting: the inulin itself undergoes fermentation by colonic microbiota, enhancing local bioavailability, while the hydrogel extends residence time and stabilizes the nanoparticle cargo during transit.

In bioelectronic applications, PVA functions as the primary structural component of dual-network hydrogels fabricated via photopolymerization. A representative formulation combines PVA, acrylic acid, gelatin, tannic acid, and quaternary ammonium chitosan, reinforced with acetylene black as a conductive filler.² The resulting architecture mimics the "brick-and-mortar" organization of pearl oyster shells, wherein the polymeric matrix acts as the structural "brick" while gelatin and tannic acid provide cross-linking "mortar" and enhanced mechanical interlocking. This composite design simultaneously maximizes tensile strength (5.49 MPa), ductility (736% elongation at break), and electrical conductivity (0.83 S/m), properties that would be mutually exclusive in single-network systems.²

## Colon-targeted drug delivery

The UA@PB/Gel system exemplifies PVA's utility in addressing ulcerative colitis, a chronic inflammatory bowel disease characterized by dysbiotic microbiota and compromised epithelial integrity. In this composite formulation, ursolic acid (UA) is encapsulated within butyrate-conjugated PVA nanoparticles suspended in an inulin hydrogel, enabling colon-specific accumulation and dual-cargo release.¹ In dextran sulfate sodium-induced colitis models, the system substantially alleviates histological inflammation, restores epithelial barrier function through claudin-2 upregulation, and reduces expression of proinflammatory cytokines including TNF-α and IL-6.¹ Critically, butyrate release during esterase-triggered degradation synergizes with UA's intrinsic anti-inflammatory and antioxidant activity, while the sustained-release profile via the inulin matrix minimizes systemic exposure — systemically measurable UA concentrations remain minimal, indicating that therapeutic efficacy is achieved through local colonic action rather than systemic absorption.¹ This biocompatibility profile, coupled with GRAS status of both PVA and inulin, positions the platform for clinical translation with favorable safety margins.

## Wearable bioelectronics

PVA-based dual-network composites address a critical engineering challenge: achieving high mechanical strength, broad strain responsiveness, and conductivity simultaneously without sacrificing flexibility or environmental stability. The PVA/acrylic acid/gelatin/tannic acid/quaternary ammonium chitosan@acetylene black (PAGTH-AB) hydrogel exhibits tensile strength of 5.49 MPa, Young's modulus of 0.69 MPa, and toughness of 22.07 MJ/m³, metrics that rival or exceed conventional elastomers.² As a wearable strain sensor, the composite demonstrates linear piezoresistive response across 0–500% strain range, enabling detection of both gross motions (knee flexion, elbow extension) and fine gestural changes (finger flexion, writing trajectories).² Dynamic response time of 336 milliseconds supports real-time motion capture, while cyclic testing confirms 2000-cycle stability without performance degradation.² The sensing mechanism operates through mechanical disruption of electron-conduction pathways within the acetylene black network; compression increases contact resistance, while stretching decreases it, yielding a measurable signal proportional to strain. Functionally, the sensor maintains performance across frigid conditions, indicating that acetylene black particles remain electrochemically active below room temperature — a property absent in organic conductor-based sensors.²

## Environmental resilience

A defining advantage of PVA composite hydrogels is their exceptional stability across diverse storage and use conditions. The dual-network structure, wherein gelatin and tannic acid provide chemical cross-links complementing the primary PVA backbone, imparts freeze-thaw resistance far exceeding single-polymer hydrogels.² Repeated cycles of freezing and thawing do not induce phase separation or mechanical degradation; instead, ice crystal formation within the interpenetrating network is distributed across multiple polymer phases, minimizing stress concentration and preserving structural integrity.² Long-term water retention remains exceptional, with hydrogels maintaining hydration and mechanical properties over extended storage periods — a property critical for practical deployment of wearable sensors that may experience intermittent use interspersed with dormancy. These characteristics eliminate the need for specialized packaging or climate control, facilitating point-of-care and field-portable bioelectronic applications.²
