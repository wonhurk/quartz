**Biomarker-based biological age (BA)** is a quantitative measure of physiological aging derived from routinely measured clinical indicators, providing complementary prognostic information beyond chronological age alone.¹ Unlike calendar years, which ignore the wide heterogeneity in aging trajectories across individuals, BA integrates vascular and metabolic burden into a unified metric capable of predicting disease progression and stratifying risk. The molecular dimension of BA is captured through transcriptional aging clocks—computational models trained on age-related gene expression patterns—which identify individuals with preserved health signatures despite advanced chronological age. In long-lived individuals, a distinct transcriptional signature centered on sustained expression of [[mrpgs|mitochondrial ribosomal protein genes]] marks attenuated biological aging and preserved mitochondrial function.²

---

## Mechanism

Biomarker-based BA is constructed from standard clinical parameters reflecting the accumulation of physiological burden over the lifespan. These indicators predominantly capture vascular and metabolic stress, integrating hemodynamic, lipid, and glucose homeostasis disruption into a single residual score adjusted for chronological age. This residual—the discrepancy between measured BA and expected age-adjusted levels—reveals individuals with either accelerated or decelerated aging trajectories independent of their calendar age.

Transcriptional aging clocks represent an orthogonal, molecular dimension of BA. These computational models are trained on age-related transcriptomic changes observed in younger, healthy populations, then applied to measure the "molecular age" of individuals across diverse cohorts. When applied to long-lived individuals—those surviving into advanced age with preserved cognitive and physical function—these clocks consistently show that such individuals possess markedly younger transcriptional ages than their chronological ages would predict.² This discrepancy suggests that transcriptional aging may be a modifiable phenotype, capable of being slowed or even reversed in certain cellular contexts.

---

## Clinical prognostic value

Biomarker-based BA provides clinically actionable prognostic information for cerebral small vessel disease (CSVD), a major cause of dementia and stroke characterized by white matter hyperintensities, lacunes, and microhemorrhages. BA residual—the individual's measured biological age adjusted for chronological age—is associated with both the presence of CSVD and its longitudinal progression.¹ This association suggests that BA captures systemic vascular and metabolic stress patterns that extend to cerebrovascular territories, enabling risk stratification independent of age alone.

---

## Molecular signature of healthy aging

In individuals with exceptional longevity, a distinct transcriptional signature emerges: sustained expression of eight mitochondrial ribosomal protein genes (8-mRPGs) that resist the typical age-related expression decline observed in general populations.² Samples with elevated 8-mRPG expression scores exhibit lower aging levels when assessed against established aging- and senescence-related gene sets, indicating that this gene cluster marks a youthful transcriptional state. Crucially, higher 8-mRPG expression correlates with increased expression of mitochondrial-function-related genes, including those involved in aerobic respiration and respiratory electron transport pathways.² This convergence suggests that the transcriptional youthfulness observed in long-lived individuals is mechanistically linked to preserved mitochondrial oxidative capacity—a cellular process essential for energy production and stress resistance throughout the lifespan.
