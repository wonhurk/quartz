**High Mobility Group Box 1 (HMGB1)** is a damage-associated molecular pattern (DAMP) protein released from dying cancer cells that functions as a critical immunostimulatory signal in anti-tumor immunity.¹ As a key hallmark of immunogenic cell death (ICD), HMGB1 operates alongside calreticulin exposure to trigger innate immune recognition and activate dendritic cells, thereby orchestrating the conversion of immunosuppressive tumor microenvironments into immunologically active sites capable of mounting robust anti-tumor responses.¹ The molecule signals through the [[tlr4]]/MyD88 pathway, engaging pattern recognition receptors on innate immune cells to promote [[cd8-t-cells|CD8+ T cell]] infiltration and dramatically enhance therapeutic efficacy when combined with checkpoint inhibitors.

---

## Mechanism and immunogenic cell death signaling

HMGB1 is released from cancer cells undergoing immunogenic cell death as a consequence of cytotoxic stress, serving alongside calreticulin exposure as a distinctive marker of this immunogenic program.¹ Unlike apoptotic or passive cell death, which typically suppress immune responses, the release of HMGB1 converts dying tumor cells into sources of immune activation. Upon its release into the extracellular space, HMGB1 engages [[tlr4]]-mediated signaling through the MyD88 adaptor protein, initiating a cascade of innate immune activation.² This [[tlr4]]/MyD88 axis drives [[dendritic-cells|dendritic cell]] maturation, enhancing their antigen-presenting capacity and promoting the priming of naive T cells for anti-tumor responses.¹ The pattern recognition of HMGB1 by these pathways establishes immunogenic cell death as mechanistically distinct from immunosuppressive death modalities, enabling therapeutic agents to harness the dying cell itself as a platform for immune priming.

## Triple-negative breast cancer immunotherapy

HMGB1 release emerges as a critical marker and driver of therapeutic efficacy in [[triple-negative-breast-cancer]] (TNBC) models employing selenium-conjugated nanoparticles. A biomimetic nanoplatform combining rosmarinic acid and selenium ions (RA-Se@M), coated with homologous cancer cell membranes, induced robust immunogenic cell death in 4T1 TNBC tumors, as evidenced by both calreticulin exposure and HMGB1 release alongside [[dendritic-cells|dendritic cell]] maturation.¹ The resulting HMGB1-mediated immune activation significantly enhanced intratumoral infiltration of [[cd8-t-cells|cytotoxic T cells]] and dramatically amplified therapeutic impact when combined with anti-PD-1 checkpoint inhibitor therapy.¹ This synergistic effect demonstrates that HMGB1-driven innate immune priming bridges the immunosuppressive "cold" tumor microenvironment toward an immunologically "hot" phenotype capable of sustaining durable anti-tumor immunity, establishing HMGB1 as a critical mediator in potentiating immunotherapy for TNBC and highlighting the importance of converting danger-associated signals into therapeutic advantages.

## Inflammatory signaling and non-cancer disease contexts

Beyond cancer, HMGB1 plays a central role in inflammatory responses across multiple disease states, where its effects reflect a dual nature as both immunologically beneficial and pathological depending on context. In sepsis, elevated circulating HMGB1 levels reflect systemic inflammation and immune dysfunction; functional selenium nanoparticles (SeNPs) administered as adjunctive therapy reduced HMGB1 levels alongside significant improvements in lymphocyte counts and [[cd8-t-cells|CD8+ T cell]] subsets, correlating with reduced pro-inflammatory cytokines and improved organ function.³ In hypoxic pulmonary edema, HMGB1 release is coupled to ferroptosis-driven neutrophil extracellular trap (NET) formation through a [[tlr4]]/MyD88-dependent mechanism; [[gpx4|GPX4]] modulates this axis by suppressing ferroptosis and consequently attenuating HMGB1-mediated NET-driven pathology in pulmonary endothelial cells.² Thus HMGB1 functions as a context-dependent danger signal — therapeutically beneficial when deliberately released to trigger anti-tumor immune responses, yet pathological when driving inflammatory cascade amplification in systemic infection or tissue hypoxia — underscoring the importance of understanding its signaling context in therapeutic design.
