**Acute myocardial infarction (AMI)** is a life-threatening cardiac event characterized by acute [[ischemia-reperfusion-injury|myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury]] leading to cardiomyocyte loss, acute cardiac dysfunction, and downstream risk for chronic [[heart-failure|heart failure]] through pathologic [[cardiac-remodeling|cardiac remodeling]].¹ Beyond the classical mechanisms of ischemic cell death, recent discoveries reveal that AMI triggers a previously unrecognized gut-heart axis, wherein dysbiotic enrichment of [[bacteroides-acidifaciens|Bacteroides acidifaciens]] during intestinal hypoxia exacerbates myocardial injury through bacterial production of [[dpp4|dipeptidyl peptidase 4 (BaDPP4)]], which degrades circulating cardioprotective peptides including [[glp-1|glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1)]].¹ Furthermore, [[macrophage]] polarization emerges as a pivotal regulator of post-MI remodeling, with the immunoregulatory molecule [[siglec-e|Siglec-E]] upregulated on monocytes in AMI patients and representing a novel therapeutic target to modulate cardiac inflammation and prevent progression to heart failure.²

---

## Mechanism

Acute myocardial infarction initiates classical ischemia-reperfusion injury when coronary artery occlusion blocks blood flow to the myocardium, followed by reperfusion-induced oxidative stress and inflammatory cell infiltration. This cascade leads to acute cardiomyocyte loss and reduced cardiac contractile function. The tissue damage and metabolic derangement activate complex innate immune responses that shape the trajectory of subsequent cardiac remodeling.

Recent mechanistic work has uncovered a microbiota-dependent amplification mechanism whereby myocardial ischemia induces intestinal hypoxia and elevation of luminal lactate, conditions that selectively enrich [[bacteroides-acidifaciens|Bacteroides acidifaciens]] within the dysbiotic microbiota.¹ This bacterium produces a bacterial isozyme of [[dpp4|dipeptidyl peptidase 4 (BaDPP4)]] that circulates systemically and degrades cardioprotective peptides, particularly [[glp-1|GLP-1]], amplifying myocardial injury beyond the primary ischemic insult.¹ In acute myocardial infarction patients, the abundance of B. acidifaciens and circulating BaDPP4 levels directly correlate with clinical markers of cardiac damage such as troponin elevation, establishing this gut-heart axis as a genuine pathophysiological contributor rather than an epiphenomenon.¹

Simultaneously, post-MI cardiac inflammation is orchestrated by [[macrophage]] polarization toward pro-inflammatory and reparative phenotypes. [[siglec-e|Siglec-E]], an immunoregulatory molecule expressed on monocytes and macrophages, plays a critical role in shaping this polarization landscape.² Siglec-E expression is upregulated in monocytes of AMI patients and in murine MI models.² The engagement of Siglec-E on myeloid cells modulates intracellular signaling pathways that control [[macrophage]] activation state, inflammatory cytokine production, and tissue remodeling functions, creating a molecular checkpoint for controlling excessive inflammation.

---

## Cardiac remodeling and heart failure progression

The acute inflammatory phase following MI, if unchecked, transitions into chronic adverse [[cardiac-remodeling|cardiac remodeling]] characterized by progressive ventricular dilation, myocardial fibrosis, and loss of contractile function that culminates in [[heart-failure|heart failure]].² The [[macrophage]] compartment drives this remodeling through the balance between pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory/reparative phenotypes. Dysregulated [[macrophage]] polarization — wherein excessive pro-inflammatory signaling persists — exacerbates fibrotic responses and impairs beneficial tissue remodeling, leading to maladaptive ventricular remodeling and functional deterioration. [[siglec-e|Siglec-E]] acts as a molecular checkpoint restraining excessive inflammation; its upregulation in acute MI may represent a failed compensatory response. Therapeutic enhancement of [[siglec-e|Siglec-E]] signaling can rebalance [[macrophage]] polarization toward reparative phenotypes, attenuating fibrosis, preserving left ventricular function, and reducing the incidence of post-MI heart failure.²

## Microbiota-based and immunomodulatory therapeutics

Two complementary therapeutic strategies emerge from recent mechanistic insights. The discovery of the gut-heart axis and the pathogenic role of [[bacteroides-acidifaciens|Bacteroides acidifaciens]]-derived [[dpp4|DPP4]] suggests that targeting the hypoxia-lactate-BaDPP4 axis offers a microbiota-based approach to cardioprotection.¹ Pharmacological inhibition of bacterial [[dpp4|DPP4]] with selective inhibitors such as daurisoline mitigates cardiac dysfunction in experimental ischemia-reperfusion models and may offer clinical benefit by preserving circulating levels of cardioprotective peptides such as [[glp-1|GLP-1]].¹ This approach is distinct from established systemic [[dpp4|DPP4]] inhibition (used in diabetes therapeutics) because it selectively targets the microbial isozyme, avoiding off-target effects on host enzymes.

In parallel, modulation of post-MI inflammation through [[siglec-e|Siglec-E]]-directed immunotherapy offers another strategy to prevent adverse cardiac remodeling. Genetic deletion of [[siglec-e|Siglec-E]] in mice subjected to myocardial infarction results in worsened survival, impaired cardiac function, larger infarct size, and exacerbated myocardial fibrosis — findings that underscore the cardioprotective role of endogenous [[siglec-e|Siglec-E]] signaling.² Conversely, therapeutic approaches that enhance [[siglec-e|Siglec-E]] engagement on [[macrophage|macrophages]] could reprogram immune responses toward repair and tissue remodeling, limiting pathologic fibrosis and preserving contractile function. These immunomodulatory strategies complement traditional approaches such as coronary revascularization, beta-blockers, ACE inhibitors, and aldosterone antagonists by addressing the underlying inflammatory and microbiota-dependent pathways that determine post-MI outcomes.
