**3D bioprinting** is a precision tissue engineering technology that employs layered printing of biocompatible inks containing cells and matrix components to fabricate functional tissue constructs with high fidelity and defined architecture.¹ The field has undergone a paradigm shift toward the use of decellularized tissue-derived bioinks that preserve the tissue-specific biochemical and structural properties of native [[extracellular-matrix|extracellular matrix (ECM)]] while maintaining optimal printability. These ECM-derived systems enable not only direct cellular organization and differentiation but also sustained delivery of therapeutic molecules, providing a unified platform for regenerative medicine applications ranging from mucociliary tissue restoration to cell-free therapeutic strategies for chronic diseases.

---

## Mechanism and bioink engineering

The foundational principle behind modern 3D bioprinting relies on bioinks derived from decellularized tissue that preserve native biochemical complexity while achieving minimal residual cellularity. Airway mucosa-derived ECM bioinks, for example, retain collagen and glycosaminoglycans during decellularization while eliminating over 98% of cellular material, ensuring that tissue-specific cues remain embedded within the printed construct.¹ These bioinks exhibit shear-thinning behavior and temperature-responsive gelation properties that are essential for extrusion-based printing—the material flows under the mechanical shear stress of printing but rapidly solidifies upon deposition, enabling precise layer stacking without compromising cell viability.¹

Kidney-derived ECM bioinks demonstrate analogous rheological advantages and support the incorporation of cellular therapeutics such as extracellular vesicles (EVs) derived from renal progenitor cells.² The use of ECM alone, rather than combining ECM with synthetic polymers, preserves renal-specific bioactivity and maintains the tissue microenvironment essential for both cell behavior and sustained therapeutic release. High shape fidelity and efficient layer stacking during bioprinting enable the creation of multi-layered constructs with defined epithelial-stromal organization that recapitulates native tissue architecture.¹

## Airway tissue engineering

3D bioprinting with airway mucosa-derived ECM bioinks addresses a critical clinical gap: current interventions fail to restore functional airway mucosa in chronic airway diseases. When human tracheal epithelial cells are cultured on 3D-printed MudECM hydrogels under air-liquid interface conditions, the tissue-specific biochemical cues drive enhanced mucociliary differentiation, tight junction formation, and goblet cell development compared to collagen-only controls.¹ The printed constructs support directional mucus transport, recapitulating the physiological clearance function of native airway epithelium.

Functional efficacy has been demonstrated in vivo through implantation of 3D-printed bilayered airway scaffolds in a rat tracheal defect model, showing restoration of tracheal integrity and epithelial-stromal organization.¹ This approach proves that precision bioprinting combined with tissue-specific ECM preservation can achieve regeneration of complex multicellular tissues with functional restoration.

---

## Therapeutic delivery and kidney disease

Beyond structural tissue engineering, 3D-printed ECM bioinks serve as controlled-release vehicles for cell-free therapeutics. Kidney-derived ECM bioinks loaded with extracellular vesicles from human renal progenitor cells enable sustained EV delivery over two weeks, with gradual release kinetics that maintain bioactivity.² The EVs are readily internalized by tubular epithelial cells and modulate oxidative stress, proliferation, and injury responses under hypoxic conditions—hallmarks of chronic kidney disease pathology. This system leverages the tissue-specific microenvironment preserved within the kidney ECM to enhance the therapeutic efficacy of EV delivery compared to bolus administration.

The integration of nanotherapeutics into 3D-printed constructs thus opens a new avenue for treating systemic diseases through localized, sustained delivery, circumventing the rapid clearance and off-target effects associated with free EV administration.²
