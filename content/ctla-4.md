**CTLA-4** (CD152) is a cell-surface immune checkpoint protein functioning as a negative regulator of T cell activation and proliferation. Located on T cells and regulatory T cells ([[treg]]), CTLA-4 delivers co-inhibitory signals through its ligation with CD80 and CD86 on antigen-presenting cells, thereby suppressing anti-tumor immunity. Therapeutic blockade of CTLA-4 with monoclonal antibodies reverses this immunosuppression and synergizes powerfully with [[pd-1]] pathway inhibition to unleash durable T cell-mediated anti-cancer responses.¹ The efficacy of anti-CTLA-4 therapy is substantially enhanced when combined with approaches that disrupt Treg-mediated immunosuppression, particularly through targeting the [[igsf3]]-[[tnfr2]] axis.

---

## Mechanism

CTLA-4 exerts its immunosuppressive function by competing with the co-stimulatory molecule CD28 for shared ligands (CD80 and CD86), thereby delivering stronger inhibitory signals to T cells. Upon engagement with CD80/CD86, CTLA-4 recruits the phosphatase SHP2, which dephosphorylates key T cell receptor signaling intermediates, leading to reduced interleukin-2 production, diminished proliferation, and suppressed cytokine secretion. In the tumor microenvironment, CTLA-4 expression is particularly enriched on tumor-infiltrating [[treg]] cells, which constitute a major source of immunosuppression. Critically, a previously uncharacterized ligand-receptor interaction has been discovered: [[igsf3]] expressed on certain tumor cells binds directly to TNFR2 on Tregs, activating the NF-κB pathway and amplifying Treg-mediated suppression.¹ This IGSF3-TNFR2 axis represents a key vulnerability in the immunosuppressive microenvironment that can be exploited therapeutically.

---

## Combination immunotherapy

Anti-CTLA-4 antibodies achieve their greatest clinical impact when combined with [[pd-1]] pathway blockade.² The two checkpoint pathways operate through distinct mechanisms — CTLA-4 suppresses T cell priming in lymph nodes and at tumor margins, while [[pd-1]] prevents T cell exhaustion within the tumor microenvironment — making their simultaneous inhibition synergistic. This anti-CTLA-4/anti-PD-1 combination has become the standard of care across multiple malignancies, with particular efficacy in cervical cancer, mismatch-repair-deficient (dMMR) or microsatellite-instability-high (MSI-H) metastatic colorectal cancer, and pleural mesothelioma.¹²³ However, clinical experience has revealed substantial heterogeneity in patient responsiveness, with 15–50% of patients experiencing primary or secondary resistance despite combination checkpoint blockade.²

---

## Colorectal cancer biomarkers and resistance

In dMMR/MSI-H metastatic colorectal cancer, response to anti-CTLA-4 plus anti-PD-1 therapy is highly variable. Recent multi-omics analysis has identified an eight-gene transcriptomic signature that, when combined with assessment of mucinous tumor phenotype, effectively predicts which patients will derive clinical benefit from the addition of anti-CTLA-4 to anti-PD-1 therapy.² Conversely, patients with the "StromalHIGH-ProliferationLOW" transcriptomic cluster show the poorest progression-free survival and are least likely to respond to combination checkpoint blockade. This gene signature-based stratification strategy enables rational patient selection and has potential to improve treatment outcomes by avoiding unnecessary toxicity in resistant populations.

## Pleural mesothelioma and methylation-based prediction

Pleural mesothelioma represents a particularly aggressive malignancy with limited standard therapeutic options beyond anti-CTLA-4/anti-PD-1 checkpoint blockade, yet outcomes remain unsatisfactory for many patients. Multi-omics analysis from the retrospective NIBIT-EPI-MESO study identified four distinct tumor DNA methylation subtypes that predict response to checkpoint immunotherapy.³ The demethylated and LOW methylation subtypes are enriched in responder patients and associated with significantly prolonged median overall survival, a higher three-year survival rate, and a T cell- and B cell-rich immune microenvironment. Conversely, the CpG island methylator phenotype (CIMP) subtype is enriched in non-responders with shorter survival and a markedly depleted immune microenvironment. A methylation-based probabilistic classification tool has been developed to predict immunotherapy outcomes, offering potential to guide treatment selection in pleural mesothelioma patients.

---

## Sequential immunotherapy strategy

A novel therapeutic approach combining targeted inhibition of the immunosuppressive IGSF3-TNFR2 axis with subsequent checkpoint blockade has emerged as particularly promising in cervical cancer.¹ In this strategy, patients are first treated with [[igsf3]] inhibitors — exemplified by purpurogallin, a small molecule identified through structure-based virtual screening that disrupts IGSF3-TNFR2 binding — to reduce Treg infiltration and function within the tumor microenvironment. Sequential treatment with anti-PD-1 blockade following [[igsf3]] inhibition yields prominent therapeutic efficacy superior to checkpoint inhibition alone, establishing a new paradigm for rational sequential immunotherapy design. This approach directly targets a key component of tumor immune evasion, reduces the immunosuppressive burden prior to checkpoint blockade, and demonstrates the therapeutic potential of rationally designed combination strategies that address multiple axes of immunosuppression.
