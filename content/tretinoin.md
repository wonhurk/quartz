**Tretinoin** (all-trans retinoic acid, ATRA) is a natural retinoid and immunomodulatory drug that functions as a key signaling molecule released from dying tumor cells to reprogram innate immunity against cancer. Acting through retinoic acid receptor alpha ([[rara|RARα]]), tretinoin directly targets [[cd38|CD38]] on tumor-associated macrophages to activate the transcription factor [[tfeb|TFEB]], triggering autophagy-dependent upregulation of MHC-II-mediated antigen presentation.¹ This mechanism links ferroptotic tumor cell death to enhanced anti-tumor immunity, creating a positive feedback loop that synergizes with [[pd1|anti-PD-1]] checkpoint blockade therapy.¹

---

## Mechanism

Tretinoin operates as a critical bridge between tumor cell death and immune activation. When tumor cells undergo [[ferroptosis|ferroptosis]], they release tretinoin as a diffusible factor that directly engages the CD38/RARα axis in infiltrating macrophages.¹ This ligand-receptor interaction activates TFEB, which controls transcriptional programs governing autophagy in macrophages. The resulting autophagy flux enhances the presentation capacity of the cell by upregulating surface expression of MHC-II, enabling these innate immune cells to more efficiently present tumor-derived antigens to CD4+ T cells.¹ This pathway establishes a self-reinforcing cycle: ferroptotic tumor cells produce tretinoin → macrophages become activated APCs → enhanced T cell priming → further immune-driven tumor cell ferroptosis.

---

## Cancer immunotherapy

Tretinoin-mediated macrophage reprogramming forms the mechanistic basis for synergy between ferroptosis-inducing agents and immune checkpoint inhibitors. In preclinical models, ferroptosis signature scores correlate with improved response to anti-PD-1 therapy, consistent with tretinoin's role in restoring MHC-II-dependent antigen presentation.¹ The positive feedback loop created by tretinoin release and macrophage activation is particularly potent when combined with PD-1 blockade, as activated macrophages suppress the inhibitory PD-L1 pathway while simultaneously promoting T cell effector functions. This suggests that ferroptosis-based cancer therapies engineered to release tretinoin may overcome resistance to checkpoint blockade by reprogramming the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment.¹

---

## Non-oncology applications

Tretinoin has been identified through network pharmacology approaches as a potential repurposed therapeutic agent for acute kidney injury (AKI). Molecular docking analysis demonstrated strong binding affinity of tretinoin to key hub proteins disrupted in AKI pathogenesis, specifically the metabolic enzymes [[aco2|ACO2]], [[fbp1|FBP1]], and [[pfkl|PFKL]], which are central to energy metabolism and oxidative stress responses in renal tissues.² These findings suggest tretinoin may address core pathological mechanisms in AKI, including inflammation, oxidative stress, and dysregulated cellular energy metabolism, though this application remains in the preclinical network pharmacology screening phase.
