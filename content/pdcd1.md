**PDCD1** encodes programmed death 1 (PD-1), an immune checkpoint receptor expressed on [[cd8-t-cells|CD8+ T cells]] that negatively regulates anti-tumor immunity and functions as a critical brake on T-cell effector function. Blockade of the PD-1 axis via [[immune-checkpoint-inhibitors|immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs)]] has revolutionized metastatic non-small cell lung cancer (mNSCLC) treatment; however, disease progression and therapeutic resistance remain significant clinical challenges.¹ The efficacy of PD-1 blockade is almost exclusively driven by CD8+ tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) bearing a distinctive phenotypic signature of exhaustion yet functional capacity—specifically, cells triple-positive for [[cd39|CD39]], [[cd103|CD103]], and [[ki67|Ki-67]]—which require direct physical cell-cell contact with cancer cells to exert cytotoxic activity.¹ The complex tumor microenvironment, enriched in immunosuppressive [[tam|tumor-associated macrophages]] and cancer-associated fibroblasts, shapes both therapeutic response and resistance.

---

## Mechanism and T-cell exhaustion

PD-1 engagement by its ligands PD-L1 and PD-L2 on tumor cells and immune cells delivers inhibitory signals that suppress T-cell receptor signaling and dampen effector function. The pathway acts as a molecular brake on CD8+ T cell proliferation, cytokine production, and cytotoxic activity, allowing tumors to escape immune surveillance. Chronic antigen exposure in the tumor microenvironment drives T-cell exhaustion—a distinct differentiation state characterized by elevated PD-1 expression alongside other inhibitory receptors. Yet not all PD-1-expressing cells are functionally inert; subset analysis reveals that CD39/CD103/Ki-67 triple positivity defines a population of exhausted TILs retaining the capacity to respond to checkpoint blockade.¹ This phenotype distinguishes functionally competent tumor-reactive cells from terminally exhausted populations unable to contribute to anti-tumor responses.

## Clinical efficacy in metastatic NSCLC

PD-1 targeting represents a paradigm shift in the treatment of mNSCLC, achieving durable responses in a subset of patients. However, the cellular basis underlying differential responses has remained incompletely understood. Comprehensive spatial single-cell proteotyping of pretreatment tumor tissue revealed that among the diverse intratumoral lymphocyte populations—including Th1 cells, regulatory T cells (Tregs), and natural killer cells—only [[cd8-t-cells|CD8+ TILs]] demonstrated a statistically significant association with ICI efficacy.¹ This finding underscore the primacy of CD8+ T cell biology in PD-1 blockade responses and argues against a broad lymphocyte activation model of tumor control.

---

## Spatial organization and direct cell contact

The architectural organization of the tumor microenvironment critically determines ICI efficacy. Computational tissue segmentation analysis demonstrated that direct physical interactions between CD8+ TILs and cancer cells are not merely incidental but essential for effective PD-1 checkpoint blockade.¹ Tumors in which CD8+ TILs are spatially segregated from cancer cells—whether by stromal barriers, fibroblast networks, or anatomical compartmentalization—show reduced responsiveness to anti-PD-1 therapy. This spatial requirement suggests that T cell cytotoxic granule delivery and direct cytolytic synapse formation remain fundamental to checkpoint blockade efficacy, even when PD-1-mediated inhibition is relieved.

---

## Tumor microenvironment and resistance

Resistance to PD-1 blockade emerges from multiple extrinsic factors within the tumor microenvironment independent of T cell-intrinsic defects. [[tam|Tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs)]] with an immunosuppressive phenotype represent an independent unfavorable prognostic factor, limiting the capacity of newly activated CD8+ TILs to establish anti-tumor responses.¹ Cancer-associated fibroblasts similarly constrain immunotherapy efficacy. Additionally, high [[cd73|CD73]] expression on tumor cells in EGFR/ALK-oncogene positive NSCLC was associated with resistance to PD-1 blockade, potentially through an axis involving M2-type TAM accumulation and aberrant angiogenesis that further isolates T cells from target cells.¹ These findings indicate that immunotherapy resistance is fundamentally a property of the entire tumor ecosystem rather than isolated tumor cell or T cell characteristics.

---

## T-cell evolutionary dynamics and response prediction

In hematologic malignancies treated with targeted therapy, variable T-cell evolutionary patterns correlate with differential immunotherapy response. Prospective profiling of patients with Waldenström macroglobulinemia receiving ibrutinib identified three distinct patterns of tumor clone evolution: evolution (early contraction followed by late expansion and increasing genomic complexity), devolution (early expansion followed by late contraction and genomic simplification), and no evolution (stable clonal architecture).² The devolution pattern strongly associated with durable clinical response, while evolution correlated with progressive disease. Notably, GZMB+ [[cd8-t-cells|CD8+ effector-memory T cells]] underwent preferential expansion in patients with progressive disease and coexisted with tumor evolution, suggesting that dysregulated CD8+ dynamics accompany therapeutic failure.² These observations in lymphoid malignancies raise the hypothesis that T-cell evolutionary fingerprints may predict checkpoint blockade efficacy across diverse cancer types.

## Enhancement through DGK-alpha/zeta inhibition

Dual inhibition of diacylglycerol kinases alpha and zeta (DGK-alpha/zeta) represents a rational combination strategy to augment PD-1 checkpoint blockade efficacy. DGK-alpha and DGK-zeta negatively regulate T-cell receptor (TCR) signaling by converting diacylglycerol to phosphatidic acid, thereby limiting T-cell effector function and anti-tumor immunity.³ The selective dual DGK-alpha/zeta inhibitor [[incb191358|INCB191358]] was engineered through structure-activity relationship optimization to achieve potent target inhibition and favorable pharmacokinetic properties.³ In syngeneic tumor models, INCB191358 demonstrated transient yet highly efficacious target engagement and significantly enhanced anti-tumor efficacy when combined with PD-1 blockade compared to monotherapy.³ This approach circumvents the need for alternative T-cell costimulation and instead amplifies intrinsic TCR signaling capacity, offering a mechanistically complementary strategy to immune checkpoint inhibition.
