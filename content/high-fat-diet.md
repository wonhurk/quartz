**High-fat diet (HFD)** is an established dietary risk factor for multiple chronic diseases, including breast cancer, metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH), type 2 diabetes, and cardiovascular disease.¹ The pathogenic effects of HFD extend beyond simple caloric excess, operating through interconnected mechanisms involving [[gut-microbiota|gut microbiota]] alterations, disruption of the [[chrono]]/[[bmal1]] molecular clock pathway, impaired glucose metabolism in skeletal muscle, and dysregulation of hepatic lipid and sphingolipid metabolism.² ³ These systemic metabolic disturbances create conditions that simultaneously elevate cancer risk and accelerate metabolic dysfunction across multiple organ systems.

---

## Mechanism

HFD initiates disease through several converging pathways. Chronic fat consumption fundamentally reshapes [[gut-microbiota|gut microbiota]] composition, promoting expansion of pathogenic species including [[streptococcus-mutans|Streptococcus mutans]].¹ These microbial alterations drive systemic metabolic dysfunction by altering the production of bacterial metabolites, particularly branched-chain amino acids like leucine, which accumulate and influence tissue-level glucose and lipid metabolism.¹ 

At the molecular level, HFD impairs the [[chrono]]/[[bmal1]] molecular clock pathway, a central regulator of metabolic homeostasis in skeletal muscle and other tissues.² This disruption cascades into dysregulation of glucose transporter trafficking — particularly reduced membrane localization of [[glut4|GLUT4]] — and downregulation of glycolytic and glycogen metabolic gene expression.² Furthermore, chronic HFD exposure dysregulates hepatic amino acid and mitochondrial metabolism, leading to pathological accumulation of ceramides and deoxy-ceramides, which elevate plasma and cardiac [[sphingolipid|sphingolipid]] levels and promote systemic inflammation.³

---

## Breast cancer promotion

HFD represents a significant dietary risk factor for breast cancer development, mechanistically operating through diet-induced [[gut-microbiota|microbiota]] alterations.¹ High-fat consumption promotes the overgrowth of [[streptococcus-mutans|Streptococcus mutans]], which produces elevated levels of the branched-chain amino acid leucine. This microbial metabolite accumulates systemically and enhances cancer stem cell activity, promoting tumor initiation and self-renewal capacity in mammary tissue.¹ This pathway demonstrates how dietary factors exert oncogenic effects through the [[gut-microbiota|microbiota]], establishing HFD as a modifiable risk factor amenable to intervention through dietary modification or microbiota-targeted therapeutics.

---

## Metabolic dysfunction and hepatocardiac disease

HFD drives a spectrum of metabolic disorders centered on hepatic lipid accumulation and lipotoxicity. At the muscular level, HFD-induced impairment of the [[chrono]]/[[bmal1]] molecular clock dysregulates glucose transporter trafficking, reducing [[glut4|GLUT4]] membrane localization and suppressing the expression of key glycolytic and glycogen metabolic enzymes, culminating in skeletal muscle glucose intolerance.² The liver bears even more severe metabolic burden under chronic HFD; hepatic steatosis progresses to metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH), characterized by hepatic inflammation, fibrosis, and profound disruptions in amino acid and mitochondrial metabolism.³

The metabolic disturbances in MASH extend beyond the liver through altered [[sphingolipid|sphingolipid]] metabolism. HFD-induced dysregulation of ceramide and deoxy-ceramide synthesis in hepatic tissue elevates their plasma concentrations and promotes their accumulation in heart tissue, driving significant increases in cardiovascular disease risk through metabolic cross-talk between the liver and heart.³ This hepatocardiac axis represents a direct mechanistic link between HFD-induced steatohepatitis and systemic cardiometabolic dysfunction.

---

## Exercise as a countermeasure

Aerobic exercise provides a countermeasure to HFD-induced metabolic dysfunction, but only when the [[chrono]]/[[bmal1]] molecular clock pathway functions normally.² In wild-type mice, 12 weeks of aerobic exercise ameliorates HFD-induced adiposity, dyslipidemia, and [[insulin-resistance|insulin resistance]] by promoting exercise-induced suppression of [[chrono]] and increased [[bmal1]] levels, enabling proper [[glut4|GLUT4]] membrane trafficking and upregulation of glycolytic gene expression.² However, this protective mechanism fails when [[chrono]] is overexpressed in skeletal muscle; despite elevated [[glut4|GLUT4]] protein levels, enhanced [[chrono]] expression prevents CHRONO-BMAL1 dissociation, blocking GLUT4 sarcolemmal colocalization and preventing the metabolic benefits of aerobic exercise.² These findings reveal that the efficacy of exercise-based interventions for HFD-induced metabolic disease critically depends on proper molecular clock signaling.

---

## Experimental models

The PWK/PhJ inbred mouse strain has emerged as a clinically relevant animal model for studying HFD-induced cardiometabolic disease.³ These mice rapidly develop hepatic fibrosis and cardiac dysfunction within 17 weeks of Western diet exposure, closely mimicking the human clinical and molecular phenotype of severe MASH and diet-induced cardiometabolic dysfunction.³ The PWK/PhJ model has proven particularly valuable for dissecting the hepatocardiac metabolic cross-talk underlying HFD-induced disease, identifying amino acid and [[sphingolipid|sphingolipid]] metabolism as critical nodes in the development of obesity-associated cardiovascular disease.
