**Sepsis** is a life-threatening syndrome characterized by dysregulated host inflammatory and immune responses to infection that drive multi-organ dysfunction, accounting for approximately 20% of all global deaths.¹ The pathophysiology involves dysregulated innate and adaptive immunity, excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, and uncontrolled pro-inflammatory [[il-6|cytokine release]], which simultaneously perpetuates tissue damage and drives organ failure. The primary and most fatal manifestation is [[acute-lung-injury]] (ALI), reflecting the lung's exquisite vulnerability to inflammatory injury during sepsis.

---

## Pathophysiology

Sepsis progresses through complex and heterogeneous inflammatory and immune pathways that resist conventional therapeutic targeting. The condition is initiated by pathogen-associated molecular patterns and damage-associated molecular patterns from both primary infection and tissue injury, triggering excessive activation of the innate immune system. Toll-like receptor signaling drives rapid upregulation of pro-inflammatory mediators including [[il-6]], TNF-α, and IL-1β, establishing a cytokine cascade that perpetuates vascular permeability and leukocyte infiltration.² Simultaneously, oxidative stress escalates as mitochondrial dysfunction and activated phagocytes generate excessive reactive oxygen species, overwhelming cellular antioxidant defenses. This dysregulation of redox homeostasis amplifies endothelial injury, increases vascular permeability, and paradoxically suppresses immune effector cell function, creating a simultaneous state of hyperinflammation and immunosuppression that characterizes sepsis.¹ The heterogeneity of sepsis pathogenesis—with patients exhibiting distinct immunological and metabolic profiles—complicates prognostic accuracy and therapeutic response, underscoring the need for multi-mechanistic intervention strategies.

---

## Diagnosis and prognostic monitoring

Early recognition and dynamic monitoring of sepsis severity depend critically on reliable biomarkers. The pro-inflammatory [[il-6|cytokine IL-6]] serves dual roles as a diagnostic indicator and prognostic marker of disease severity, with elevated levels correlating with multi-organ dysfunction and mortality risk.¹ The kidney injury marker neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin (NGAL) complements [[il-6]] measurement, providing organ-specific information on sepsis-driven renal dysfunction.¹ A DNA-engineered immunosensing platform (DEISP) enables ultrasensitive, simultaneous detection of these biomarkers with limits of detection reaching 0.025–0.026 pg/mL, achieving 91% accuracy in clinical serum and urine samples and supporting point-of-care diagnostic workflows.¹ Such advances in multi-marker detection systems facilitate rapid patient stratification and enable longitudinal monitoring of biomarker trajectories to assess treatment responsiveness.

## Acute lung injury and hepatic dysfunction

[[Acute-lung-injury]] represents both the most prevalent and most fatal manifestation of sepsis, resulting from dysregulated neutrophil recruitment, excessive cytokine-mediated vascular permeability, and tissue-resident immune cell dysfunction.² The development of ALI involves activation of the complement cascade and overwhelming of local antioxidant defenses by neutrophil-derived reactive oxygen species. Hepatic injury emerges as a second predominant and life-threatening manifestation during sepsis, driven by similar inflammatory and oxidative mechanisms. Toll-like receptor 4 signaling activated by circulating lipopolysaccharide drives hepatocyte apoptosis and failure of hepatic synthetic capacity, compounding multi-organ dysfunction.³ The liver's central role in systemic immunity and metabolic homeostasis makes hepatic dysfunction a critical determinant of sepsis severity and mortality.

---

## Current standard treatment

Current clinical management of sepsis relies on rapid administration of broad-spectrum antibiotics to control the underlying infection, coupled with aggressive organ-supportive therapy including vasopressor support, mechanical ventilation for [[acute-lung-injury]], and renal replacement therapy for acute kidney injury.¹ Despite these interventions, sepsis remains highly lethal and refractory to single-pathway targeted therapies. No FDA-approved drugs, cells, or biologics have been specifically designed for sepsis treatment, and preclinical strategies have frequently failed to translate to clinical benefit, reflecting the disease's pathogenic complexity and the inadequacy of targeting isolated mechanisms in a condition driven by simultaneous dysregulation across multiple organ systems.¹

## Emerging immunomodulatory therapies

Selenium nanoparticles represent a promising adjunctive immunotherapeutic approach to restore immune competence in sepsis patients. In a pilot randomized clinical trial, functional selenium nanoparticles (SeNPs) at 400 μg daily supplemented standard care and significantly restored immune function, as demonstrated by increased CD3+, CD4+, and CD8+ T cell subset counts and higher total lymphocyte numbers on days 4, 7, and 10 post-enrollment.⁴ Concurrently, SeNPs reduced pro-inflammatory [[il-6]] and HMGB1 levels, attenuated multi-organ dysfunction across liver, kidney, and lung, and decreased intensive care unit and hospital mortality without reported adverse events.⁴

[[Mesenchymal-stem-cells|Mesenchymal stem cell]]-based approaches, particularly [[mscargo|bioengineered hybrid spheroids (MSCargo)]] integrating mesenchymal stem cells with metformin-loaded microspheres, offer multi-mechanistic rescue of sepsis-driven organ injury. MSCargo enhances hepatocyte viability and protects against oxidative stress and cytokine-induced damage through synergistic upregulation of antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and immunomodulatory activities.³ When transplanted via the hepatic portal vein, MSCargo markedly reduced sepsis-associated organ dysfunction across liver, kidney, and lung through modulation of TLR4/NF-κB signaling and enhancement of hepatic regenerative capacity.³

[[pv-nps|Polymyxin B-antioxidant nanoassemblies (PV NPs)]] integrate multiple complementary mechanisms: direct polymyxin B-mediated antibacterial activity and endotoxin neutralization coupled with broad-spectrum reactive oxygen species scavenging through an ester-linked α-tocopherol–gallic acid conjugate.² In murine cecal ligation-and-puncture models of polymicrobial sepsis, PV NPs conferred a 75% survival rate, significantly outperforming either single component while reducing lung inflammation and oxidative stress in sepsis-induced [[acute-lung-injury]].² Critically, PV NPs mitigate the dose-limiting nephrotoxicity of polymyxin B monotherapy, enabling safe prolonged pulmonary retention and therapeutic efficacy with an improved safety profile.²

## Early-life vulnerability and microbiota development

Early-life gut microbiota disruption—particularly from cumulative antibiotic exposure in the neonatal intensive care unit—increases susceptibility to life-threatening infections including sepsis and necrotizing enterocolitis. A longitudinal multi-omics study of 186 preterm infants demonstrated that antibiotic exposure suppresses anaerobic colonization and microbial diversity in a cumulative, dose-dependent manner, with breastmilk feeding partially mitigating these deleterious effects.⁵ The stool metabolome correlates directly with microbial composition, with antibiotic-driven dysbiosis causing depletion in polyamine metabolism linked to anaerobe abundance.⁵ Host immune priming, reflected by fecal calprotectin levels, follows a biphasic pattern correlated with microbial diversity and polyamine metabolites; mediation analysis identified anaerobe suppression and polyamine depletion as key mechanistic drivers of antibiotic-associated immune dysregulation.⁵ These findings underscore the critical role of early-life microbiota in establishing mucosal barrier integrity and immune competence, establishing a direct mechanistic link between neonatal dysbiosis and enhanced vulnerability to sepsis during infancy and early childhood.
