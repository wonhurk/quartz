**Vimentin** is a canonical mesenchymal marker whose expression is dynamically regulated during epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), a reversible developmental program that drives metastatic competence and treatment resistance in cancer cells.¹ In colorectal cancer, vimentin is stabilized as a key structural component of the partial EMT state—a hybrid phenotype retaining both epithelial and mesenchymal properties—through signaling initiated by the calcium-activated chloride channel [[ano1|ANO1 (TMEM16A)]].¹ High vimentin expression correlates strongly with poor patient survival and aggressive biological behaviors including enhanced migration, invasion, and metastatic plasticity, establishing vimentin as both a critical functional effector and a powerful prognostic indicator of disease progression.

---

## Mechanism

Vimentin expression is functionally coupled to [[ano1|ANO1]]-dependent signaling cascades in metastatic cancer cells. The calcium-activated chloride channel [[ano1|ANO1]], frequently overexpressed in epithelial tumors, directly regulates vimentin levels through activation of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR and ERK pathways.¹ Proteomic analysis has revealed that [[ano1|ANO1]] depletion broadly downregulates cytoskeletal and adhesion networks, with vimentin serving as a critical structural nexus within these interconnected pathways.¹ The stabilization of vimentin by [[ano1|ANO1]] is essential for maintaining the spatially organized subcellular architecture characteristic of the partial EMT state, wherein metastatic cells simultaneously express both epithelial markers including E-cadherin and mesenchymal markers including vimentin, N-cadherin, and α-SMA.¹

---

## Role in metastatic EMT and cancer progression

Vimentin functions as a core structural and signaling component within the partial EMT state, a cellular phenotype that confers enhanced metastatic potential and treatment resistance. In colorectal cancer, [[ano1|ANO1]]-mediated vimentin stabilization is coordinated with simultaneous upregulation of additional mesenchymal markers and EMT transcription factors including Zeb1, Snail, and Twist.¹ Flow cytometry studies have demonstrated that [[ano1|ANO1]] knockdown selectively depletes the E-cadherin+/vimentin+ hybrid population while disrupting the organized subcellular architecture of the partial EMT state, indicating that vimentin destabilization directly collapses metastatic plasticity.¹ In gastric cancer, vimentin expression is positively regulated downstream of the cell surface protein [[kirrel1|KIRREL1]], with elevated vimentin levels coordinating with upregulation of the proteolytic enzymes MMP2 and MMP9 to drive cancer cell proliferation, migration, and invasion.²

---

## Clinical and prognostic significance

Vimentin expression serves as a robust and independent predictor of poor clinical outcomes in multiple malignancies. In colorectal cancer, elevated vimentin expression—driven by [[ano1|ANO1]] overexpression—correlates significantly with poor patient survival and represents a molecular signature of the aggressive metastatic phenotype.¹ In gastric cancer, high vimentin expression driven by [[kirrel1|KIRREL1]] is strongly associated with poor progression-free, disease-specific, and overall survival.² The consistent association between vimentin levels and adverse outcomes across these cancer types suggests that vimentin expression reflects a fundamental commitment to the invasive phenotype, positioning it as a valuable biomarker for stratifying patients at highest risk of disease progression and for monitoring therapeutic responses to metastasis-targeting agents.

---

## Therapeutic targeting

The functional dependence of vimentin stabilization on [[ano1|ANO1]] signaling provides a clear mechanistic rationale for targeting this pathway therapeutically. [[ano1|ANO1]] silencing induces robust downregulation of vimentin concurrent with potent suppression of cancer cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, while simultaneously triggering early apoptosis.¹ Depletion of [[ano1|ANO1]] causes selective loss of the E-cadherin+/vimentin+ partial EMT population and disruption of the organized hybrid cytoskeletal architecture, indicating that vimentin destabilization is mechanistically coupled to the disassembly of metastatic plasticity and restoration of epithelial differentiation.¹ These findings suggest that [[ano1|ANO1]] inhibitors represent a therapeutic strategy to suppress metastatic behavior by targeting vimentin-dependent structural remodeling and the maintenance of the partial EMT state, offering potential clinical utility across vimentin-high, metastatic epithelial malignancies.
