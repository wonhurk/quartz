**Multiple Sclerosis (MS)** is a chronic inflammatory and neurodegenerative disease characterized by immune-mediated destruction of myelin, progressive neurodegeneration, and episodic clinical relapses.¹ Two major disease subtypes—secondary progressive MS (SPMS) and relapsing MS (RMS)—present distinct pathological trajectories and therapeutic opportunities. In SPMS, chronic brain demyelination drives hippocampal neurodegeneration and cognitive decline through suppression of neural precursor cell neurogenesis, a process mediated by downregulation of the signaling protein [[neuregulin-1|neuregulin-1 (NRG-1)]].¹ Restoration of [[neuregulin-1|NRG-1]] reactivates neural progenitors and reverses cognitive impairment through enhanced neurogenesis, synaptogenesis, and remyelination—achieving superior reparative effects compared to [[siponimod]], the current standard disease-modifying therapy for SPMS.¹ In contrast, RMS is managed with immunomodulatory agents including [[diroximel-fumarate|diroximel fumarate]] and [[dimethyl-fumarate|dimethyl fumarate]], which suppress relapse frequency, with diroximel fumarate offering improved tolerability.²

## Pathology and disease heterogeneity

Multiple Sclerosis presents as two clinically and pathologically distinct diseases. Relapsing MS (RMS) is characterized by discrete episodes of inflammation and transient neurological symptoms followed by periods of remission, driven by focal immune infiltration and demyelination. Secondary progressive MS (SPMS) emerges in approximately 50% of RMS patients after 10 years and is defined by gradual neurological decline independent of relapses, reflecting chronic demyelination and progressive neuronal loss throughout the central nervous system.¹ The shift from relapsing to progressive disease involves a transition from peripheral immune cell infiltration to compartmentalized intrathecal inflammation and increasing neurodegeneration.

Cognitive impairment is a hallmark of SPMS pathology, affecting memory, processing speed, and executive function in up to 70% of progressive MS patients. The hippocampus—critical for memory consolidation—undergoes profound neurodegeneration in SPMS due to chronic demyelination of afferent inputs and loss of supporting neural circuits.¹ This neurodegeneration is mechanistically linked to suppression of neural precursor cell (NPC) neurogenesis, the process by which the hippocampus generates new neurons throughout life to support cognitive function.

## NRG-1 dysregulation and hippocampal neurodegeneration

Neural precursor cells within the hippocampus maintain cognitive function throughout adulthood by generating new neurons that integrate into memory circuits. In progressive MS, this neurogenic capacity is severely compromised. Recent work has identified [[neuregulin-1|neuregulin-1 (NRG-1)]] as a critical regulator of NPC self-renewal, activity, and neuronal differentiation.¹ NRG-1 downregulation occurs in chronic active demyelinating lesions characteristic of SPMS and directly correlates with reduced NPC proliferation, increased quiescence, and diminished hippocampal neurogenesis.¹

The consequence of NRG-1 deficiency is cognitive decline. Mice with chronic brain demyelination mimicking SPMS pathology show progressive memory impairments paralleling the loss of hippocampal neurogenesis and NPC dysfunction.¹ This mechanistic link between NRG-1 dysregulation, NPC quiescence, and cognitive symptoms identifies [[neuregulin-1|NRG-1]] as a tractable therapeutic target for reversing cognitive dysfunction in progressive MS.

---

## NRG-1 restoration as regenerative therapy

Restoring [[neuregulin-1|NRG-1]] signaling reactivates quiescent neural precursor cells and unleashes multifaceted repair mechanisms in the demyelinated hippocampus. NRG-1 treatment simultaneously promotes neurogenesis (the generation of new neurons), synaptogenesis (the formation of new synaptic connections), and remyelination (the restoration of myelin insulation around axons).¹ These complementary repair processes work synergistically: newly generated neurons integrate into functional memory circuits, enhanced synaptic connectivity strengthens neural communication, and remyelination restores rapid signal conduction in damaged pathways.

The result is reversal of cognitive decline. Mice receiving NRG-1 restoration show restoration of memory function, recovery of hippocampal volume, and enhanced neuronal complexity—indicating rebuilding of neural networks.¹ Critically, [[neuregulin-1|NRG-1]] therapy achieves greater and more multifaceted reparative effects than [[siponimod]], the current standard disease-modifying therapy for SPMS.¹ While [[siponimod]] suppresses peripheral immune infiltration and slows progression, it does not directly reactivate neurogenesis or promote remyelination, highlighting the regenerative potential of [[neuregulin-1|NRG-1]]-based approaches for addressing the progressive neurodegeneration underlying SPMS cognitive impairment.

## Immunomodulatory therapy in relapsing MS

Relapsing MS (RMS) is managed with disease-modifying therapies that suppress immune activation and reduce relapse frequency. [[Diroximel fumarate|Diroximel fumarate (DRF)]] and [[dimethyl fumarate|dimethyl fumarate (DMF)]] are first-line immunomodulatory agents belonging to the fumarate class, both of which reduce relapse rates through mechanisms involving Nrf2 pathway activation and immune cell modulation.² Both agents demonstrate efficacy in preventing relapses and slowing disability progression in RMS populations.

The clinical distinction between the two agents lies in gastrointestinal tolerability. [[Dimethyl fumarate|DMF]] causes significant flushing and gastrointestinal side effects in a substantial proportion of patients, limiting long-term adherence and treatment persistence.² [[Diroximel fumarate|DRF]] was specifically designed to improve the tolerability profile while maintaining immunomodulatory efficacy.² Real-world evidence from large US insurance claims databases confirms that [[diroximel fumarate|DRF]] demonstrates improved gastrointestinal tolerability compared to [[dimethyl fumarate|DMF]], potentially translating into better long-term treatment adherence and relapse control in RMS.²
