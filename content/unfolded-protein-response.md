**The unfolded protein response (UPR)** is an adaptive cellular survival pathway activated by endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress, a hallmark condition induced by cancer therapeutics. The UPR coordinates multiple signaling axes to restore proteostasis and promote cell survival, principally through the [[perk|PERK]]-[[eif2a|eIF2α]]-[[atf4|ATF4]] axis and the [[ire1a|IRE1α]] branch, though these mechanisms often paradoxically confer resistance to targeted therapies and foster immune evasion in malignant tumors.¹

---

## Mechanism

The UPR operates as a multi-tiered stress-sensing system. When unfolded proteins accumulate in the ER, the [[perk|PERK]] kinase phosphorylates [[eif2a|eIF2α]], attenuating global protein translation while inducing selective translation of stress-response transcription factors, particularly [[atf4|ATF4]].¹ This branch initiates adaptive programs aimed at restoring cellular proteostasis by upregulating chaperones and promoting autophagy. In parallel, the [[ire1a|IRE1α]] endoribonuclease cleaves the [[xbp1|XBP1]] transcript, generating an active transcription factor that further orchestrates the ER unfolded protein response. However, these adaptive mechanisms are frequently exploited by cancer cells to survive the cytotoxic stress imposed by targeted therapies, creating a critical vulnerability in the treatment of drug-resistant malignancies.

---

## Immune evasion in therapy-resistant NSCLC

In [[egfr|EGFR]]-TKI-resistant non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), activation of the UPR, particularly through the [[ire1a|IRE1α]] signaling axis, drives immunosuppressive transcriptional programs that limit the efficacy of immune checkpoint blockade. Afatinib-resistant NSCLC cells exhibit elevated [[pd-l1|PD-L1]] expression coupled with downregulation of shared immune-modulated gene sets, a cytokine profile skewed toward immunosuppression (elevated IL-8, decreased IL-6 and IFN-γ), and minimal changes in MHC class I presentation.² This IRE1α-dependent reprogramming of immune gene expression represents a key mechanism by which resistant tumors evade both cytotoxic T cell recognition and anti-PD-1/PD-L1 therapeutic intervention. Notably, pharmacological inhibition of [[ire1a|IRE1α]] signaling partially reverses this immunosuppressive transcriptional signature, suggesting that targeting the UPR may restore immunotherapy sensitivity in therapy-resistant NSCLC.

---

## Colorectal cancer ADC resistance

The anti-[[trop2|TROP2]] antibody-drug conjugate [[immu132|IMMU-132]], which delivers the topoisomerase I inhibitor SN-38 for direct DNA damage induction, concurrently activates the [[perk|PERK]]-[[eif2a|eIF2α]]-[[atf4|ATF4]] axis of the UPR as an adaptive survival mechanism in colorectal cancer.¹ This ER stress-driven response enables malignant cells to tolerate the initial cytotoxic insult and establishes a barrier to clinical efficacy. The UPR additionally suppresses the oncogenic [[wnt|Wnt]]/[[beta-catenin|β-catenin]] pathway as part of its adaptive restructuring, though this suppression is incomplete and insufficient to durably prevent tumor progression. The convergence of these survival mechanisms — direct DNA damage response, UPR-mediated cell survival, and residual Wnt/β-catenin signaling — collectively underpins resistance to TROP2-directed ADCs in advanced CRC.

---

## Dual targeting strategy

The synergistic combination of [[immu132|IMMU-132]] with the [[perk|PERK]] inhibitor GSK2606414 overcomes ADC resistance in colorectal cancer by simultaneously suppressing multiple survival pathways.¹ The PERK inhibitor blocks the [[perk|PERK]]-[[eif2a|eIF2α]]-[[atf4|ATF4]] adaptive response, preventing the canonical UPR-mediated cell survival, while [[immu132|IMMU-132]] delivers direct DNA damage. Critically, co-inhibition of [[perk|PERK]] further suppresses the [[wnt|Wnt]]/[[beta-catenin|β-catenin]] oncogenic axis beyond what the ADC achieves alone, dismantling two distinct driver pathways in therapy-resistant CRC. This dual-targeting strategy has demonstrated potent synergy across multiple preclinical colorectal cancer models and provides a rational framework for clinical combination therapy development, suggesting broader applicability to other malignancies where the UPR drives resistance to targeted agents.
