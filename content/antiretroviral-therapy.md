**Antiretroviral therapy (ART)** is a potent combination drug regimen that suppresses [[hiv-1|HIV replication]] to clinically undetectable levels in most individuals living with HIV, representing one of the most significant advances in modern infectious disease management. However, ART does not eliminate integrated [[hiv-1|HIV proviruses]] from infected cells, making it fundamentally a suppressive rather than curative approach.¹ The therapy functions by blocking sequential steps in the viral lifecycle—reverse transcription, integration, and protease-mediated maturation—thereby halting active replication while dormant proviruses persist within the host genome indefinitely. ART enables immune reconstitution through [[cd4-t-cells|CD4+ T-cell]] recovery, a critical mechanism of clinical benefit, though this recovery is frequently impaired by autoimmune responses, particularly elevated anti-CD4 autoantibodies associated with [[staphylococcus-aureus]] translocation from the gastrointestinal tract.²

---

## Mechanism of Viral Suppression

ART typically comprises a combination of antiretroviral drugs from multiple mechanistic classes—nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors, non-nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors, protease inhibitors, integrase inhibitors, and entry inhibitors—that collectively target sequential steps in the [[hiv-1|HIV]] replication cycle. In patients with adequate medication adherence and drug-susceptible virus, ART reduces plasma viral load below the limit of clinical detection (typically <50 copies per milliliter), achieving what is termed "undetectable = untransmittable" status.¹ This suppression is transformative not only for individual health and longevity, but also for eliminating sexual transmission risk—a principle that has fundamentally reshaped HIV prevention and public health strategies globally.

---

## Persistent Integrated Proviruses and Latent Reservoirs

Despite the remarkable clinical success of ART, a defining limitation persists: antiretroviral drugs target only actively replicating virus and cannot eradicate integrated proviruses—DNA copies of the viral genome inserted into host cell chromosomes.¹ These proviruses remain indefinitely within long-lived [[cd4-t-cells|CD4+ T-cell]] reservoirs, particularly in cells with minimal or absent active viral transcription, forming a latent archive that sustains infection. This persistence explains why discontinuation of ART invariably leads to rapid viral rebound in nearly all patients, as quiescent proviruses reactivate and resume replication once drug-mediated suppression is removed. The size, cellular distribution, and reactivation kinetics of these reservoirs remain central questions in HIV cure research and strategies aimed at achieving "functional cure" or sustained "ART-free remission."

---

## Non-suppressible Viremia and Defective Proviruses

In a subset of ART-treated individuals, detectable plasma viremia persists despite optimal medication adherence, unchanged drug susceptibility, and therapeutic regimen modifications—a phenomenon termed non-suppressible viremia (NSV).¹ Recent molecular characterization reveals that NSV often originates from clonally expanded [[cd4-t-cells|CD4+ T cells]] harboring genetically identical, replication-impaired proviruses bearing critical defects, particularly deletions within the [[hiv-1|HIV]] major splice donor site. These defective proviruses cannot produce infectious viral particles, yet they retain capacity for viral protein expression, allowing infected cells to evade immune destruction while generating clinically detectable but non-infectious viremia.¹ The integration of such defective proviruses into minimally differentiated [[cd4-t-cells|CD4+ T-cell]] subsets enables them to establish long-lived populations spanning the full spectrum of memory and effector cell phenotypes while maintaining immune-evasive protein expression patterns—a cellular strategy that explains their protracted survival in the face of ART.

---

## Impaired Immune Reconstitution and Microbial Translocation

Although ART enables [[cd4-t-cells|CD4+ T-cell]] recovery in most patients, this reconstitution is frequently blunted by active autoimmune processes. Elevated anti-CD4 autoantibodies are detected in ART-treated individuals and inversely correlate with [[cd4-t-cells|CD4+ count]] recovery; moreover, these antibodies appear to directly promote apoptosis of [[cd4-t-cells|CD4+ T cells]] and reduce their frequency within gut-associated lymphoid tissues.² The source of anti-CD4 autoantibody production traces to [[staphylococcus-aureus]] translocation from the gastrointestinal tract—a consequence of [[hiv-1|HIV]]-driven intestinal barrier dysfunction that persists even during viral suppression. In experimental models, [[staphylococcus-aureus]] peptidoglycan induces anti-CD4 IgG production, reduces [[cd4-t-cells|CD4+ T-cell]] frequency in gut tissue, and promotes surface IgG binding and apoptosis in [[cd4-t-cells|CD4+ T cells]].² This mechanism suggests that microbial translocation perpetuates autoimmune-mediated immune dysfunction and may explain why a substantial fraction of patients achieve only partial [[cd4-t-cells|CD4+ T-cell]] recovery despite sustained viral suppression on ART.

---

## HIV Rebound Upon ART Interruption

When ART is discontinued, [[hiv-1|HIV]] rebounds in virtually all individuals, though the kinetics, magnitude, and tempo of rebound vary considerably.³ Analysis of data from multiple clinical trials has identified specific [[cd4-t-cells|CD4+ T-cell]] and natural killer cell subsets associated with delayed viral rebound, alongside the discovery of [[hiv-1|HIV]] silencing factors—genes whose elevated expression correlates with prolonged suppression even after treatment discontinuation. [[ddit4|DDIT4]] and [[znf254|ZNF254]] emerged as key silencing factors linked to delayed rebound phenotypes.³ The antidiabetic drug [[metformin]] was found to induce [[ddit4|DDIT4]] expression and suppress [[hiv-1|HIV]] replication in vitro, suggesting that pharmacological enhancement of [[hiv-1|HIV]] silencing factors may extend the interval between ART interruption and viral rebound—a finding with direct implications for "ART-free remission" research and potential therapeutic strategies targeting viral latency.³

## Public Health Applications: Option B+ Policy

Beyond individual clinical management, ART has emerged as a transformative public health tool for preventing [[hiv-1|HIV]] transmission from pregnant women to infants during pregnancy, labor, and breastfeeding. In 2015, South Africa implemented [[option-b-plus|Option B+]], a policy extending lifelong ART to all pregnant women living with [[hiv-1|HIV]] regardless of baseline [[cd4-t-cells|CD4 count]], eliminating CD4-dependent treatment decisions and ensuring universal viral suppression during the perinatal period.⁴ This approach has prevented thousands of vertical transmission events and provided sustained maternal health benefits beyond pregnancy. However, postpartum disengagement from HIV care remains a critical challenge; women who discontinue ART or lose clinical contact after delivery face substantially elevated risks for adverse maternal health outcomes, treatment failure, drug resistance, and ongoing [[hiv-1|HIV]] transmission through breastfeeding.⁴ Postpartum retention in care is thus essential for both individual survival and population-level epidemic control, yet many programs continue to struggle with care continuity during this vulnerable transition period.
