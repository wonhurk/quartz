**Influenza** is a seasonal respiratory viral infection caused by infection with influenza viruses (H1, H3, and influenza B) that triggers protective immune responses against surface antigens [[hemagglutinin|hemagglutinin (HA)]] and [[neuraminidase|neuraminidase (NA)]]. Protection from infection and disease severity depends on both systemic and mucosal antibody responses, with [[antineuraminidase-antibodies|antineuraminidase (anti-NA) antibodies]] emerging as a critical but historically underappreciated correlate of protection.¹ Multiple vaccine delivery platforms generate distinctly different immune profiles: recombinant vaccines elicit superior serum antibody titers, while intranasal live-attenuated formulations preferentially establish local mucosal immunity in the upper respiratory tract with long-lived memory B cell responses.²

---

## Immune response to infection and vaccination

Effective protection against influenza requires antibody recognition of the immunodominant surface antigens HA and NA.¹ However, vaccine platforms differ markedly in their capacity to generate balanced immune responses. [[riv4|Recombinant influenza vaccine (RIV4)]] induces substantially higher serum antibody responses compared to traditional [[iiv4|egg-based inactivated vaccine (IIV4)]], particularly in individuals with prior low antibody responses to egg-derived formulations.³ Conversely, [[flumist|live-attenuated intranasal vaccine (FluMist)]] is specifically engineered to stimulate mucosal immunity and generates robust upper respiratory tract responses despite inducing lower systemic immunogenicity in adults.²

---

## Antineuraminidase antibodies and protection

[[antineuraminidase-antibodies|Antineuraminidase antibodies]] have been identified as an independent correlate of protection against influenza infection and warrant greater emphasis in vaccine development.¹ While most vaccine strategies emphasize the hemagglutinin head domain, enhanced influenza vaccine formulations markedly improve antineuraminidase antibody responses and neuraminidase inhibition (NAI) activity in older adults, indicating that dual antigen targeting improves protective immunity in vulnerable populations.¹

---

## Local mucosal immunity and memory persistence

[[flumist|FluMist intranasal vaccination]] induces substantial expansions of upper respiratory tract HA-specific memory B cells with an activated CD27+CD21− phenotype, encompassing responses to H1, H3, and influenza B surface antigens.² These activated memory cells persist in the upper airway for at least six months post-vaccination and are associated with circulating T follicular helper cells, establishing durable local surveillance at the portal of viral entry.² In contrast, [[riv4|intramuscular recombinant]] and [[iiv4|inactivated vaccines]] preferentially elicit systemic antibody responses against the HA head domain with minimal local memory B cell establishment in the upper airway, despite robust circulating HA-specific memory B cells.² This spatial dichotomy between mucosal and systemic immunity reflects fundamentally different immunological niches engaged by intranasal versus parenteral vaccination.
