**Heart disease** remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality, driven by both intrinsic cardiac pathology and systemic metabolic dysfunction. Emerging therapeutic strategies address this complexity through complementary approaches: direct [[rna-therapeutics|RNA-based gene editing]] of cardiomyocytes via optimized [[lipid-nanoparticles|lipid nanoparticles (LNPs)]], and metabolic intervention targeting hepatic dysfunction. The discovery that [[mash|metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH)]] significantly elevates cardiovascular disease risk through disrupted amino acid and [[sphingolipid]] metabolism has revealed hepatic-cardiac cross-talk as a critical therapeutic window.¹

---

## Genetic correction via RNA therapeutics

[[Rna-therapeutics|RNA therapeutics]] have emerged as a promising modality for direct cardiac genetic repair, yet their clinical translation has been constrained by insufficient cardiomyocyte transfection. Recent advances in [[lipid-nanoparticles|LNP]] chemistry have overcome this barrier. Screening of chemically diverse LNP libraries using human induced pluripotent stem cell-derived cardiomyocytes identified 18:1 TAP10 as a lead cardiotropic LNP, exhibiting potent human cardiac transfection capability in both primary cells and in vivo models.¹ Intramyocardial injection of 18:1 TAP10 LNPs achieves remarkable efficacy: 36% transfection at the injection site and 13% distal to the injection, substantially surpassing other administration routes. In contrast, intravenous and intracoronary delivery primarily transfect non-cardiomyocyte populations (up to 40% in endothelial cells), demonstrating the critical importance of route optimization.¹

The therapeutic potential of this platform has been demonstrated through [[adenine-base-editor|adenine base editor (ABE)]] delivery to Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD) patient-derived cardiomyocytes, achieving 80% on-target gene correction and restoration of dystrophin expression.¹ In humanized DMD mouse models, intramyocardial administration of LNP-ABE complexes successfully generated dystrophin-positive cardiomyocytes in the injected left ventricle, establishing proof-of-concept for cardiac genetic correction in vivo.¹

## Metabolic dysfunction and hepatic-cardiac cross-talk

Beyond intrinsic cardiac pathology, systemic metabolic dysfunction represents a major driver of cardiovascular disease. [[Mash|MASH]] is strongly associated with elevated cardiovascular risk, a relationship mediated through disrupted hepatic amino acid and [[sphingolipid]] metabolism.² Western diet exposure in the PWK/PhJ mouse strain, a robust model of severe MASH, triggers a hierarchical cascade of metabolic alterations: early changes include elevated cholesterol and dysregulated transcript profiles in translation and lipid metabolism pathways.² Chronic Western diet exposure exacerbates hepatic lipid accumulation, inflammation, and fibrosis, while simultaneously disrupting amino acid and mitochondrial metabolism.²

This metabolic dysfunction directly impacts cardiac tissue through aberrant [[sphingolipid]] synthesis. Hepatic synthesis of [[ceramide]] and deoxy-ceramides increases significantly under Western diet conditions, elevating [[sphingolipid]] levels in both plasma and myocardial tissue.² These circulating and locally accumulated sphingolipids drive cardiac dysfunction through mechanisms involving altered membrane composition, mitochondrial dysfunction, and inflammation. The establishment of cardio-metabolic cross-talk in this model—where hepatic metabolic derangement translates to cardiac pathology—identifies metabolic intervention as a complementary therapeutic strategy to direct cardiac gene editing.²

## Delivery route optimization

The efficacy of cardiac [[rna-therapeutics|RNA therapeutics]] depends critically on the spatial distribution and cellular tropism achieved by different delivery routes. Four primary administration pathways have been systematically evaluated using the 18:1 TAP10 LNP platform: intramyocardial (IM) injection achieves the highest cardiomyocyte transfection (36% at injection site, 13% distally) but requires direct cardiac intervention.¹ Intracoronary delivery enables more distributed cardiac coverage but preferentially targets non-cardiomyocyte populations.¹ Intrathoracic and intravenous routes provide broader systemic distribution, yet with lower overall cardiac specificity.¹ The choice of delivery route thus represents a critical optimization parameter balancing transfection efficiency, invasiveness, and distribution pattern according to the intended therapeutic application.
