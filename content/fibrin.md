**Fibrin** is a fibrous protein that forms the structural matrix of blood clots and hemostatic plugs, generated during the coagulation cascade through thrombin-mediated conversion of soluble fibrinogen. As the primary target of thrombolytic therapy, fibrin networks are selectively degraded by [[rt-pa|recombinant tissue plasminogen activator (rt-PA)]] and [[tenecteplase]] to restore blood flow in thromboembolism and intracerebral hemorrhage.¹² Beyond hemostasis, fibrin has emerged as a powerful biological scaffold for precision medicine: fibrin-targeting [[creka|CREKA peptides]] enable site-directed drug accumulation within thrombotic sites, while covalent crosslinking via activated coagulation factor [[factor-xiii|XIIIa (FXIIIa)]] anchors nanoparticles within tumors for exceptional selectivity and therapeutic efficacy.³

---

## Mechanism

Fibrin polymerization occurs when [[thrombin]] cleaves fibrinogen into fibrin monomers, which spontaneously associate into protofibrils that laterally pack into thick fibrin fibers. These fiber networks form the mechanical scaffold of clots, interacting with platelets and circulating blood cells to establish the hemostatic plug. Thrombolytic agents including [[rt-pa]] and [[tenecteplase]] target fibrin by promoting activation of [[plasminogen]] to the protease plasmin, which then cleaves fibrin polymers into soluble degradation products.¹ The selectivity of thrombolytic agents for fibrin versus other plasma proteins varies; third-generation agents like [[tenecteplase]] demonstrate enhanced fibrin selectivity and improved penetration into dense, platelet-rich clots compared to earlier thrombolytic generations.²

The [[creka|CREKA peptide]], a fibrin-targeting ligand, binds to fibrin-fibronectin complexes at sites of vascular injury and thrombosis with high affinity. When conjugated to drug carriers including [[chitosan]] microcapsules, CREKA peptides confer prolonged circulation time and preferential accumulation at thrombotic sites, overcoming margination effects that typically limit microcapsule extravasation.¹

## Thrombolytic applications

Fibrin degradation is essential for treating thromboembolic disease and intracerebral hemorrhage. Free [[rt-pa]] has revolutionized acute ischemic stroke therapy by dissolving arterial thrombi, yet suffers from short drug half-life, rapid systemic clearance, and off-target effects that elevate bleeding risk. A self-sacrificing templating strategy overcomes these limitations: hollow [[chitosan]] microcapsules preloaded with [[rt-pa]] are surfaced with [[creka|CREKA peptides]], producing rt-PA@CSMs-CREKA constructs with microscale dimensions optimized for vascular transport. In vitro fibrin clot degradation assays and in vivo thrombotic models demonstrate that CREKA-targeted microcapsules achieve superior fibrin network disruption and enhanced thrombus penetration compared with free [[rt-pa]], while downregulating key procoagulant factors including FXa and PAI-1.¹ In acute intracerebral hemorrhage, [[tenecteplase]] combined with neuronavigation-assisted stereotactic minimally invasive puncture offers a complementary strategy: the enhanced fibrin selectivity of [[tenecteplase]] enables efficient dissolution of platelet-rich intracerebral clots without excessive bleeding at remote sites.²

## Tumor-targeted drug delivery

Fibrin generation within tumors provides a biological targeting anchor for precision cancer therapy. [[Ultrasound]] applied to solid tumors induces localized vascular disruption, triggering in situ activation of the coagulation cascade and fibrin deposition within the tumor microenvironment. Concurrently, [[ultrasound]] activates the zymogen [[factor-xiii|factor XIII]] to its active protease form (FXIIIa), which catalyzes covalent isopeptide crosslinking between lysine and glutamine residues on nanoparticle carriers and fibrin polymers.³ This transglutaminase-mediated coupling is highly specific and irreversible, enabling exceptional tumor enrichment: a coagulation-targeting peptide-modified nanoparticle ([[aminp|AMINP]]) achieving FXIIIa-catalyzed fibrin crosslinking increased intratumoral active drug concentration 52.1-fold compared to non-targeted controls, resulting in 98.9% tumor suppression with no recurrence in rechallenge models.³ The spatiotemporal controllability of this approach — controlled by [[ultrasound]] timing to create binding targets and enhance local hypoxia for prodrug activation — positions fibrin crosslinking as a robust platform for selective cancer eradication.
