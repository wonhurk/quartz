**Hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT)** is a curative cellular therapy for hematologic malignancies, inborn errors of immunity, and bone marrow failure syndromes, in which a patient's diseased or dysfunctional hematopoietic system is replaced with donor-derived or autologous stem cells capable of reconstituting normal blood and immune function. Despite its transformative clinical potential, HSCT outcomes remain constrained by two life-threatening complications: [[graft-failure|graft failure]], in which donor cells fail to engraft or are rejected, and [[chronic-graft-versus-host-disease|chronic graft-versus-host disease (cGVHD)]], an immune-mediated syndrome that profoundly affects post-transplant morbidity and mortality.¹ Recent advances include [[vla-4-agonist|VLA-4 agonist]] therapy to enhance engraftment, donor-derived [[tregs|regulatory T-cell (Treg)]] infusions to control steroid-refractory cGVHD, and thrombopoietin receptor agonists as bridge therapy for patients without suitable donors.

---

## Engraftment mechanisms

Successful HSCT depends on the homing, engraftment, and self-renewal of donor hematopoietic stem cells within the recipient bone marrow niche. The integrin very late antigen-4 (VLA-4) has emerged as a pivotal regulator of this process, governing both the self-renewal capacity of transplanted stem cells and their differentiation into lymphoid progenitors.² [[vla-4-agonist|VLA-4 agonists]] enhance donor HSC engraftment and promote long-term hematopoietic reconstitution by activating extracellular signal-regulated kinase 1/2 (ERK1/2) phosphorylation pathways, which regulate stem cell function without inducing leukemogenic gene expression.² These agonists also facilitate early immune reconstitution by augmenting T-cell differentiation from HSCs, yielding reconstituted immune cells with potent antiviral activity while notably not exacerbating acute graft-versus-host disease — a critical safety milestone for clinical translation.²

## Chronic GVHD management

[[chronic-graft-versus-host-disease|Chronic graft-versus-host disease]] remains the principal barrier to successful allogeneic HSCT, arising from impaired recovery of [[tregs|regulatory T cells]] in transplant recipients. To address steroid-refractory or steroid-dependent cGVHD, donor-derived Treg infusions — freshly isolated via CliniMACS purification from leukapheresis products — have demonstrated marked clinical efficacy in phase 1/2 trials.¹ Global symptom responses were achieved in 71% of patients, with 52% experiencing at least a 2-point improvement on the cGVHD severity scale.¹ Critically, symptom improvement enabled reductions in corticosteroid use in 58% of patients, discontinuation of calcineurin inhibitors in 75%, and reductions in ruxolitinib and mycophenolate in 83% and 33%, respectively.¹ Infused Treg clonotypes were detectable in circulation up to 12 months post-infusion, accompanied by increases in serum interleukin-7 and interferon-gamma and decreases in soluble CD13, suggesting sustained immunomodulatory activity.¹

## Graft failure salvage

[[graft-failure|Graft failure]] after allogeneic HSCT — classified as primary (never achieving engraftment) or secondary (loss of established graft) — is a life-threatening complication requiring urgent intervention. In pediatric patients with non-SCID inborn errors of immunity, second conditioned allogeneic HSCT represents a viable salvage strategy, achieving 5-year overall survival of 78% and event-free survival of 69%.³ However, primary graft failure carries a significantly worse prognosis than secondary failure, with 5-year overall survival of 69% versus 81% and event-free survival of 52% versus 75%.³ Conditioning intensity critically affects outcomes: myeloablative or reduced-toxicity conditioning regimens confer superior event-free survival (74%) compared with nonmyeloablative conditioning (50%), though overall survival differences were not significant.³ Upon multivariable analysis, cord blood emerged as the only independent negative predictor of event-free survival after second transplant, with a hazard ratio of 4.9.³ The cumulative incidence of repeat graft failure after the second HSCT was 13% at one year, and whole-blood donor chimerism exceeded 90% in 84% of survivors at last follow-up.³

## Stem cell sources

Umbilical cord blood has gained regulatory approval as a hematopoietic stem cell source for over 80 diseases, offering unique advantages in HLA-mismatched transplantation despite lower cell yields compared with mobilized peripheral blood.⁴ Transplant success depends critically on [[total-nucleated-cells|total nucleated cell (TNC)]] and [[cd34|CD34+ cell]] content, making predictive selection models for cord blood units of paramount importance to public stem cell banks tasked with cryopreserving only high-quality units.⁴ Processing outcomes — particularly isolation yield of TNC and CD34+ cells — are key determinants of clinical utility, guiding donor selection algorithms that maximize engraftment potential while optimizing finite cryostorage capacity.⁴

---

## Alternative approaches

For patients with [[severe-aplastic-anemia|severe aplastic anemia (SAA)]] who lack suitable HSCT donors, thrombopoietin receptor agonists (TPO-RAs) have emerged as effective frontline or bridging therapy. A multicenter retrospective study of 57 treatment-naïve children demonstrated that TPO-RA monotherapy or TPO-RA combined with cyclosporine A achieved remission in 68% of patients, with no severe adverse events attributable to the TPO-RA.⁵ Median hemoglobin, platelet counts, and absolute neutrophil counts all increased significantly in both treatment arms.⁵ TPO-RA monotherapy yielded a higher remission rate (92%) than combination therapy (59%), with a shorter median time to remission of 6 months versus 12 months.⁵ These findings support prospective evaluation of TPO-RAs as definitive therapy when HSCT is not readily available, potentially shifting the treatment paradigm for pediatric SAA.⁵
