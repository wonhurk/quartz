**Cisplatin** is a platinum-based chemotherapy agent that induces cancer cell death primarily through DNA cross-linking and double-strand breaks, with emerging recognition of its ability to simultaneously trigger ferroptosis and immunogenic cell death.¹ Widely employed in the treatment of triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), cervical cancer, and high-grade serous ovarian cancer (HGSOC), cisplatin remains a cornerstone of oncology despite significant challenges with acquired resistance and dose-limiting toxicities.²,³ Contemporary research has revealed that its efficacy can be enhanced through dual-targeting strategies that engage both DNA and redox regulators such as [[thioredoxin-reductase|thioredoxin reductase (TrxR)]], as well as combination approaches that modulate survival signaling and metabolic pathways.¹,⁴

---

## Mechanism of action

Cisplatin enters cells and undergoes aquation, forming reactive platinum complexes that bind to DNA purine bases, primarily forming intrastrand cross-links that distort the DNA helix and trigger apoptosis. Beyond classical DNA damage, cisplatin increasingly acts as a ferroptosis inducer by depleting intracellular glutathione (GSH), accumulating reactive oxygen species (ROS), and deactivating glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4).¹ This dual mechanism positions cisplatin within a broader category of agents capable of inducing immunogenic cell death — a form of apoptosis that releases damage-associated molecular patterns, activates dendritic cells, and potentiates anti-tumor immunity.¹ When combined with copper-based cuproptosis inducers, cisplatin can suppress the [[wnt-beta-catenin-pathway|Wnt/β-catenin pathway]] via endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress and calcium overload, further expanding its mechanistic repertoire.³

---

## Cancer applications

Cisplatin is a first-line agent in TNBC, where next-generation Pt(IV) prodrugs such as [[complex-6b|complex 6b]] demonstrate remarkable activity against both cisplatin-sensitive and cisplatin-resistant cells by simultaneously targeting [[thioredoxin-reductase|TrxR]] and DNA.¹ In cervical cancer, cisplatin efficacy is compromised by caveolin-1 (CAV1)-mediated activation of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, which drives chemoresistance; simvastatin can restore sensitivity by downregulating CAV1 expression and inhibiting downstream survival signaling.⁴ In ovarian cancer, particularly HGSOC, cisplatin remains standard therapy, though resistance frequently emerges through ferroptosis inhibition mediated by RUNX2 palmitoylation and activation of the YAP1/GLS1 axis.⁵

---

## Resistance mechanisms

Resistance to cisplatin arises through diverse molecular adaptations. In ovarian cancer, the palmitoyltransferase ZDHHC14 drives S-palmitoylation of RUNX2, which transcriptionally activates YAP1 and the downstream GLS1 axis, suppressing ferroptosis and enhancing chemoresistance.⁵ In cervical cancer, elevated CAV1 expression activates PI3K/AKT signaling, conferring cisplatin resistance that can be reversed by statins or CAV1 knockdown.⁴ Additionally, aberrant activation of the [[wnt-beta-catenin-pathway|Wnt/β-catenin pathway]] confers intrinsic tolerance to cuproptosis, limiting the efficacy of combination strategies that incorporate copper-based agents alongside cisplatin; this tolerance can be overcome by ER stress-mediated pathway suppression.³

---

## Chemosensitization strategies

Statins, particularly simvastatin, demonstrate synergistic anti-cancer effects when combined with cisplatin in resistant cervical cancer models.⁴ Simvastatin downregulates CAV1, PI3K, and phosphorylated AKT, thereby inhibiting cell viability and migration while promoting apoptosis in cisplatin-resistant SiHa-DDP and C33a-DDP cell lines. Metallic polymer nanoparticles containing both Pt(IV) and copper components enable on-demand release of cisplatin and copper ions, synergizing chemotherapy with cuproptosis induction.³ These nanoparticles suppress the [[wnt-beta-catenin-pathway|Wnt/β-catenin pathway]] through ER stress-triggered calcium overload, acting as type II immunogenic cell death inducers and demonstrating superior tumor suppression in orthotopic TNBC models.³

---

## Novel platinum agents

Second-generation Pt(IV) prodrugs such as [[complex-6b|complex 6b]] overcome cisplatin resistance by targeting both [[thioredoxin-reductase|TrxR]] and DNA, inducing ferroptosis through GSH depletion, lipid peroxidation accumulation, and GPX4 deactivation.¹ These agents trigger excessive ROS accumulation that activates ER stress and immunogenic cell death, offering a chemoimmunotherapeutic strategy for TNBC. For platinum-resistant ovarian cancer, heterobimetallic Pd(II)/Sn(II) η³-allyl complexes bearing N-heterocyclic carbene ligands provide an alternative scaffold with potent cytotoxicity across cisplatin-sensitive, cisplatin-resistant, and HGSOC cell lines.⁶ Lead compounds retain activity in patient-derived tumor organoids, including platinum-resistant models, while remaining inactive in non-malignant fallopian tube epithelium, highlighting a favorable therapeutic window through mitochondria-targeted apoptosis rather than direct DNA damage.⁶

---

## Adverse effects

Cisplatin induces acute kidney injury (AKI) through aberrant activation of the STING (stimulator of interferon genes) pathway, driving excessive type I interferon and pro-inflammatory cytokine production.² Small-molecule STING inhibitors such as KSI-028 attenuate renal and hepatic injury in cisplatin-induced AKI mouse models by suppressing STING-dependent signaling and downregulating inflammatory gene expression, establishing STING as a therapeutic target for mitigating cisplatin nephrotoxicity.²
