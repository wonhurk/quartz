**Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE)** is a severe autoimmune disease characterized by immune dysregulation, chronic inflammation, hypertension, and renal injury, driven in part by impairment of the [[acetylcholinesterase|cholinergic anti-inflammatory pathway]].¹ The disease manifests through abnormal B and T cell activation, loss of self-tolerance, and the production of pathogenic autoantibodies that damage multiple organ systems. [[Lupus-nephritis|Lupus nephritis]], which develops in a substantial proportion of SLE patients, represents one of the most severe and rapidly progressive manifestations, often culminating in renal impairment or end-stage renal disease, particularly in children.²

---

## Pathophysiology

SLE pathogenesis involves profound dysregulation of the adaptive and innate immune systems, leading to a feed-forward cycle of chronic inflammation and tissue injury. Central to disease progression is impairment of the [[acetylcholinesterase|cholinergic anti-inflammatory pathway]], a neuroimmune regulatory system that normally suppresses excessive immune activation and inflammation. This dysfunction permits unchecked production of pro-inflammatory cytokines and activation of pathogenic B and T cell populations. The resulting chronic inflammatory state drives multiple organ manifestations, including the characteristic renal injury associated with lupus nephritis, as well as systemic [[hypertension]] and [[renal-fibrosis]].¹ Studies using female NZBWF1 mice—a well-established preclinical model of SLE—have elucidated the central role of neuroimmune dysregulation in disease pathogenesis, demonstrating that circulating levels of soluble [[tnfr1|TNFR1]], a marker of systemic inflammation, are markedly elevated in SLE and correlate with cardiovascular pathology.

---

## Lupus Nephritis as a Critical Manifestation

[[Lupus-nephritis|Lupus nephritis]] represents one of the most severe and clinically consequential manifestations of childhood SLE, with the potential for rapid progression to renal impairment or complete renal failure.² In pediatric populations particularly, the disease exhibits aggressive clinical behavior, necessitating immediate recognition and treatment escalation. The pathological remodeling of renal tissue in lupus nephritis includes both inflammatory infiltration and progressive [[renal-fibrosis|fibrosis]], the latter of which irreversibly compromises renal function. Early diagnosis and precise longitudinal assessment of disease activity are therefore essential for guiding therapeutic decisions and optimizing clinical outcomes in affected children.²

---

## Targeting the Cholinergic Anti-Inflammatory Pathway

Enhancement of the [[acetylcholinesterase|cholinergic anti-inflammatory pathway]] through pharmacological acetylcholinesterase inhibition has emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy for reducing SLE-driven inflammation and renal injury. Systemic administration of [[galantamine]], an acetylcholinesterase inhibitor that potentiates cholinergic signaling and neuroimmune communication, improves survival and substantially attenuates multiple indices of SLE-related pathology in preclinical models.¹ Long-term galantamine treatment lowers [[hypertension|blood pressure]], reduces key [[renal-injury|renal injury markers]] including urinary albumin, kidney injury molecule-1, and neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin, and importantly decreases the accumulation of [[renal-fibrosis|renal fibrosis]].¹ Beyond renal effects, galantamine treatment reduces circulating [[tnfr1|soluble TNFR1]] to normal levels, indicating suppression of systemic inflammation, and concurrently diminishes both splenic CD19+ B cells and kidney-infiltrating CD8+ T cells—cellular populations central to SLE pathogenesis.¹ These findings suggest that restoration of neuroimmune balance through acetylcholinesterase inhibition may offer disease-modifying benefits across multiple organ systems in SLE.

---

## Diagnosis and Biomarker Assessment

Conventional serological and urinary biomarkers, although widely employed in clinical practice for SLE screening and lupus nephritis assessment, exhibit limited sensitivity and specificity, compromising both diagnostic accuracy and longitudinal disease monitoring.² Renal biopsy remains the diagnostic gold standard for lupus nephritis but is invasive and unsuitable for repeated assessments during treatment.

Surface-enhanced Raman spectroscopy (SERS), a noninvasive analytical platform, has emerged as a superior alternative for molecular profiling of SLE and lupus nephritis.² SERS-based metabolic fingerprinting of dual-modal serum and urine samples achieved substantially higher sensitivity, specificity, and overall diagnostic accuracy compared to single-modal approaches in a cohort encompassing healthy controls, SLE patients without nephritis, and lupus nephritis patients.² The dual-modal SERS platform identified key biomolecular alterations associated with immune activation, inflammatory responses, and renal injury, providing mechanistic insights into the pathophysiological basis of childhood SLE and lupus nephritis.² This approach enables both accurate initial diagnosis and non-invasive longitudinal monitoring of disease activity, directly addressing a critical clinical gap in the assessment of childhood lupus nephritis.
