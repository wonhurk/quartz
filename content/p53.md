**Tumor protein p53** (TP53) is a critical tumor suppressor gene whose inactivating mutations define particularly aggressive and therapy-refractory cancer subtypes across hematologic and solid malignancies. TP53-mutated acute myeloid leukemia exhibits a distinctive genomic signature featuring recurrent deletions on chromosome 3p encompassing ribosomal protein genes that, when co-occurring with del(5q), establish a ribosomopathy-like state characterized by global impairment of protein synthesis and selective vulnerability to [[hsp90|HSP90]] inhibition.¹ In solid tumors, TP53 mutations co-enrich with [[ras|RAS pathway]] alterations and establish progenitor-like transcriptional programs supported by a senescent, [[upar|uPAR]]-positive stromal niche exploitable by [[car-t-cell|CAR T cell]] therapy.²

---

## Mechanism and tumor suppressor function

TP53 encodes a transcription factor fundamental to the DNA damage response, cell cycle arrest, and apoptosis, serving as a critical barrier to malignant transformation. Loss of p53 function through mutation, deletion, or other mechanisms abolishes these cellular safeguards, permitting cells to tolerate genomic instability and acquire additional oncogenic alterations. TP53 mutations are among the most frequent somatic alterations in human cancer, yet their functional consequences are context-dependent, shaped by cooperating genomic events and the tissue-specific tumor microenvironment. In many malignancies, TP53 inactivation cooperates with activating mutations in growth-promoting pathways to establish distinctive cellular states with specific therapeutic vulnerabilities.

## TP53-mutated acute myeloid leukemia

TP53-mutated acute myeloid leukemia represents a particularly refractory disease subtype with poor prognosis and limited therapeutic options. Beyond TP53 itself, this disease is characterized by a recurrent and previously underappreciated genomic signature: deletions on the short arm of chromosome 3 occurring in more than 20% of TP53-mutated cases, frequently co-occurring with del(5q).¹ These del(3p) events encompass multiple ribosomal protein genes comprising components of both 40S and 60S ribosomal subunits. The consequence is global downregulation of ribosomal network gene expression and profound reduction in protein synthesis capacity—a ribosomopathy-like phenotype. This phenotype is most pronounced when del(3p) and del(5q) occur together, indicating that loss of ribosomal protein genes on both chromosomes establishes a cooperative oncogenic mechanism exceeding what either deletion alone would produce.¹

Discovery of this ribosomopathy-like state in TP53-mutated AML immediately suggests therapeutic opportunities. Chemical screening identified [[hsp90|HSP90]] inhibition as a selective vulnerability in AML with low ribosomal protein gene expression.¹ HSP90 functions as a chaperone essential for stability of numerous client proteins involved in cell survival, and its inhibition may prove particularly lethal in the context of compromised protein synthesis capacity. This finding establishes a context-dependent treatment strategy for this otherwise refractory patient population, enabling more precise therapeutic targeting of a molecularly defined AML subset.

## Solid tumors and the stromal ecosystem

In solid tumors, TP53 mutations are frequently co-enriched with alterations in the [[ras|RAS pathway]], a pattern observed across multiple malignancy types.² These tumors adopt a distinctive progenitor-like transcriptional state characterized by increased stemness and reduced differentiation signals. Critically, these tumors reside within a specialized microenvironment: a niche of senescent, pro-fibrotic stromal cells expressing high levels of the urokinase plasminogen activator receptor ([[upar|uPAR]]). The uPAR-positive stromal compartment—comprising fibroblasts and other stromal populations with senescence features—actively sustains tumors through growth factor secretion and extracellular matrix remodeling.²

This stromal ecosystem creates a broad vulnerability exploitable through cellular immunotherapy. [[car-t-cell|uPAR-directed CAR T cells]] simultaneously eliminate tumor cells and their stromal support, inducing durable regressions across diverse solid tumor models.² These cells achieve robust antitumor activity through dual targeting: direct CAR T engagement of uPAR on senescent stromal cells disrupts the pro-fibrotic niche while simultaneously killing uPAR-expressing tumor cells. CAR T activity is further potentiated by senescence-inducing therapies, indicating that pharmacologic stromal senescence induction enhances immunotherapy efficacy. Importantly, uPAR-targeted CAR T cells demonstrate significant antitumor activity without sustained myelosuppression in humanized mouse models, supporting clinical translation and distinguishing this approach from conventional chemotherapy.

## Biomarkers in metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer

In metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer, the p53 pathway emerges as a powerful prognostic signal. Pathway analysis of plasma extracellular vesicle proteomics from 100 mCRPC patients receiving ¹⁷⁷Lu-PSMA-617 identified that p53 upregulation associates with poor progression-free and overall survival.³ This finding suggests that elevated p53 pathway activity—potentially reflecting either mutant p53 accumulation or wild-type p53 activation in response to genotoxic stress—identifies particularly aggressive tumor biology predictive of inferior treatment outcomes. Concurrent analysis identified additional prognostic biomarkers including surface antigen expression patterns and circulating tumor cell subpopulations, yet p53 pathway upregulation emerged as a key independent prognostic signal. Integration of such liquid biopsy proteomics into biomarker-driven trials may enable improved patient stratification and treatment selection in mCRPC.

## Senescence and intestinal homeostasis

Beyond cancer biology, p53-mediated senescence plays unexpected roles in tissue homeostasis and aging. In the intestinal epithelium, senescence-associated p53 activation impairs epithelial regeneration and barrier function, contributing to age-related and inflammation-associated gut dysfunction. Dietary supplementation with N-acetylneuraminic acid (Neu5Ac), a sialic acid enriched in bird's nest and human milk, mitigated senescence-associated p53 activation in both colitis and aging models.⁴ By suppressing excessive p53-driven senescence, Neu5Ac promoted epithelial regeneration and maintained intestinal barrier integrity, demonstrating that modulation of p53-dependent senescence yields therapeutic benefits outside the cancer context. This finding underscores the biological complexity of p53: while p53-driven senescence acts as a potent tumor suppressor, its sustained activation in non-malignant tissues can contribute to age-related pathology and tissue dysfunction.
