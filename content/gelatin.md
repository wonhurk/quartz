**Gelatin** is a natural protein biopolymer that functions as a critical structural and functional platform in advanced biocomposite materials and multifunctional hydrogels. Its inherent elasticity, biodegradability, and capacity for water retention make it particularly suited for engineering dual-network composite hydrogels with exceptional mechanical performance and environmental responsiveness.¹ When combined with synthetic polymers such as poly(vinyl alcohol) and acrylic acid, along with biocompatible components including [[chitosan|quaternary ammonium chitosan]] and tannic acid, gelatin enables the fabrication of conductive hydrogels achieving tensile strength of 5.49 MPa and electrical conductivity of 0.83 S/m while maintaining freeze resistance and long-term stability.¹

---

## Structure and Properties

Gelatin integrates into composite hydrogels through photopolymerization and solvent-exchange strategies inspired by nacre-like "brick-and-mortar" architectural design. In dual-network systems, gelatin provides structural support while [[chitosan]] enhances mechanical resilience, and acetylene black dispersal confers electrical conductivity. The resulting composite exhibits an elongation at break of 736%, toughness of 22.07 MJ/m³, and Young's modulus of 0.69 MPa.¹ Its water-absorptive nature and biocompatibility render it suitable for integration into aerogel matrices reinforced with [[nanocellulose]], where Schiff-base crosslinking with dialdehyde starch creates dimensionally stable structures with superior compressibility and elasticity.²

## Flexible Wearable Sensors

Gelatin-based conductive hydrogels have emerged as promising materials for real-time human motion monitoring in flexible electronic sensor applications. The pearl-inspired composite hydrogel architecture achieves outstanding cyclic stability over 2000 stress-strain cycles and a rapid dynamic response of 336 milliseconds, enabling precise capture of biomechanical signals across multiple body sites.¹ Remarkably, gelatin-reinforced hydrogels maintain functional performance even under cryogenic conditions, facilitating real-time tracking of finger, wrist, elbow, and knee joint movements as well as gesture recognition and dynamic writing trajectory capture during freezing temperatures.¹ This freeze-resistance property, combined with excellent water retention, positions gelatin-based systems as candidates for continuous health monitoring in diverse environmental conditions and wearable device applications.

## Postharvest Food Preservation

Gelatin serves as a biodegradable matrix foundation in aerogel-based active packaging systems designed for extending the shelf life and maintaining the quality of highly perishable fruits. When combined with [[nanocellulose]] reinforcement through freeze-drying and Schiff-base crosslinking with dialdehyde [[starch]], gelatin aerogels achieve superior cushioning properties while remaining fully compostable.² The aerogel structure enables sustained release of bioactive volatile compounds; trans-2-hexenal (E2H)-loaded gelatin aerogels demonstrate first-order kinetics release kinetics with robust mechanical performance and significant inhibitory effects against Penicillium citrinum, the primary postharvest fungal pathogen.² When integrated with [[1-mcp|1-methylcyclopropene (1-MCP) treatment]], the synergistic system provides ethylene inhibition, antifungal activity, and mechanical cushioning, extending the commercial shelf life of Chinese bayberry from 8 days to 18 days at 4°C while preserving firmness and nutritional quality.² Moreover, gelatin aerogels degrade completely in soil within two weeks, confirming their suitability as environmentally responsible sustainable packaging alternatives to petroleum-based materials.

## Polymer Composite Engineering

Gelatin's versatility as a protein biopolymer is further evident in its capacity to function as a load-bearing component in advanced composite hydrogels. When chemically integrated alongside [[chitosan]] and reinforced through tannic acid cross-linking, gelatin contributes to materials exhibiting exceptional toughness of 22.07 MJ/m³ and sustained mechanical resilience through over 2000 stress-strain cycles.¹ The three-component polymer network—comprising gelatin, [[starch|dialdehyde starch]], and [[nanocellulose]]—demonstrates tunable mechanical properties correlated to nanocellulose content, with optimal formulations balancing compressibility, elasticity, and degradation kinetics for functional applications.² These diverse composite systems underscore gelatin's role as a platform biopolymer for engineering multifunctional materials that combine mechanical robustness, thermal stability, and environmental sustainability across healthcare, electronics, and food preservation domains.
