**PARP inhibitors** are a class of small-molecule anticancer agents that target [[parp|poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP)]], a critical enzyme in DNA damage repair pathways. By suppressing PARP activity, these inhibitors induce genomic instability and synthetic lethality in tumor cells, particularly those with defects in homologous recombination repair.¹ PARP inhibitors have demonstrated robust efficacy in platinum-sensitive pancreatic cancer patients harboring pathogenic variants in DNA repair genes including [[brca1]], [[brca2]], [[atm]], [[palb2]], and [[chek2]].¹ In [[tnbc|triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC)]], PARP inhibitors achieve enhanced efficacy when combined with [[rnase-h2|RNase H2]] inhibition, generating a synergistic dual mechanism that amplifies replication stress, DNA damage accumulation, and innate immune activation.²

---

## Mechanism

PARP enzymes catalyze the transfer of ADP-ribose groups to target proteins, essential for detecting and repairing single-strand breaks and base excision damage throughout the genome. PARP inhibition blocks this DNA damage response, forcing tumor cells to rely on alternative, error-prone repair pathways or undergo cell death if those pathways are compromised. In cells with pre-existing defects in homologous recombination — such as those carrying [[brca1|BRCA1]] or [[brca2|BRCA2]] mutations — PARP inhibition creates synthetic lethality, rendering the combination of two independently tolerable defects lethal.¹ The therapeutic concept has been extended beyond germline BRCA mutations to encompass other pathogenic DNA repair variants including [[atm|ATM]], [[palb2|PALB2]], and [[chek2|CHEK2]], broadening the applicability of PARP inhibitors across diverse tumor populations.

---

## Pancreatic cancer applications

PARP inhibition has proven efficacious in platinum-sensitive advanced pancreatic cancer patients carrying pathogenic variants in a spectrum of DNA repair genes. [[niraparib]], a clinical-stage PARP inhibitor, is undergoing Phase II evaluation in patients with germline mutations in [[brca1]], [[brca2]], [[atm]], [[palb2]], and [[chek2]].¹ This expanded patient selection beyond classical BRCA1/2 carriers reflects growing evidence that pathogenic variants in [[atm|ATM]] — a master coordinator of DNA damage sensing — and [[palb2|PALB2]] confer synthetic lethality with PARP inhibition. The feasibility and clinical benefit of PARP inhibitor expansion into broader pancreatic cancer patient populations lacking identified pathogenic DNA repair variants remains under active investigation.¹

---

## TNBC efficacy and synergistic strategies

[[tnbc|Triple-negative breast cancer]], defined by absence of estrogen receptor, progesterone receptor, and HER2 amplification, represents a particularly aggressive cancer subtype with limited targeted therapeutic options and poor prognosis. PARP inhibitors exhibit baseline activity in TNBC through induction of DNA damage and genomic instability. However, synergistic combinations dramatically elevate therapeutic potential. Co-inhibition of PARP and [[rnase-h2|RNase H2]] — an enzyme responsible for removing misincorporated ribonucleotides from genomic DNA — amplifies replication stress and DNA damage accumulation.² RNase H2 is overexpressed in TNBC tumors and correlates with poor survival; its inhibition selectively impairs TNBC cell viability while sparing non-malignant mammary epithelial cells, demonstrating therapeutic selectivity.² The combination further elevates replication fork instability when paired with [[atr]] inhibitors, compounding DNA damage burden and forcing tumor cells toward apoptosis.²

---

## Immunotherapy integration

PARP inhibition combined with RNase H2 inhibition triggers accumulation of cytosolic single-stranded DNA, activating innate immune sensors and driving type I interferon-mediated immune activation. This dual-inhibition strategy upregulates T cell-recruiting chemokines, reshaping the tumor microenvironment toward enhanced lymphocyte infiltration and activation.² The synergistic immunological remodeling significantly enhances the efficacy of [[immune-checkpoint-blockade]] agents such as anti-PD-L1 antibodies.² This multi-layered approach — combining direct DNA damage amplification with immune system engagement — represents an emerging paradigm in precision oncology, particularly for immunologically cold tumors such as TNBC where conventional targeted therapies are unavailable.
