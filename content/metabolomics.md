**Metabolomics** is a high-throughput analytical technology that quantifies the complete complement of small-molecule metabolites in biological samples using [[mass-spectrometry|mass spectrometry]], enabling rapid identification of metabolic signatures associated with disease severity, viral variants, and population-level health differences. The field has undergone dramatic acceleration through direct-infusion platforms that eliminate liquid chromatography bottlenecks, allowing single-injection analysis of over 9,000 metabolite features simultaneously with quantification of thousands of proteins.¹ Metabolomics reveals disease-specific disruptions in metabolic pathways—such as cholesterol dysregulation in chronic viral infection or polyamine depletion in antibiotic-exposed infants—and when integrated with [[proteomics]], [[lipidomics]], and [[microbiomics]], unlocks multivariate biomarkers that distinguish clinical subtypes and predict disease progression.

---

## Technology platform

The recent breakthrough in metabolomics throughput stems from single-injection multi-omics analysis by direct infusion (SMAD), a platform leveraging [[ion-mobility-mass-spectrometry|ion mobility mass spectrometry]] and optimized software to quantify metabolite and protein signals from a single biological sample without time-consuming liquid chromatography.¹ SMAD enables quantification of over 9,000 metabolite m/z features and over 1,300 proteins in less than five minutes, drastically reducing per-sample analysis cost and enabling large-scale population studies previously infeasible. This capability was validated across diverse biological contexts: polarized and senescent murine macrophages, human cell drug screening at pilot scale, and scaled 96-well plate high-throughput platforms, demonstrating both analytical reliability and practical scalability.¹

## Disease biomarkers and viral variants

Metabolomics has emerged as a powerful tool for decoding the molecular basis of chronic infection. In a multi-omics profiling study of 108 chronically infected patients, serum metabolomics and proteomics distinguished between wild-type hepatitis B virus (HBV) and three variant groups characterized by specific mutations (G1896A, A1762T/G1764A, and the double mutant A1762T/G1764A + G1896A).² These variant groups exhibited distinct molecular signatures correlated with elevated liver transaminases (ALT and AST) and clinical disease severity. Pathway analysis across 226 to 242 altered metabolites per variant group consistently implicated cholesterol metabolism as the most perturbed metabolic process.² Six key proteins and metabolites from the cholesterol pathway emerged as combined biomarkers capable of distinguishing between variant groups, demonstrating how metabolomics transforms host-pathogen interactions into quantitative, clinically actionable signatures linking viral genotype to disease outcome.

## Microbiome–metabolome coupling in early-life development

Longitudinal metabolomics has revealed how environmental stressors in the neonatal intensive care unit reprogram the developing gut ecosystem and immune maturation in preterm infants. A prospective study of 186 preterm infants (<32 weeks gestation or <1,500 g birth weight) examined 1,153 stool samples using metabolomics and microbial profiling, uncovering antibiotic-driven suppression of anaerobic bacteria and concurrent disruption of polyamine metabolism.³ Polyamine biosynthesis by commensal anaerobes represents a critical interface between microbial ecology and infant immune priming; antibiotic exposure depleted these metabolites in a cumulative, dose-dependent manner. Stool metabolomic patterns correlated tightly with microbial colonization state and with host fecal calprotectin (S100A8/A9), a marker of mucosal inflammation and intestinal barrier integrity.³ Breastmilk feeding partially mitigated antibiotic-driven dysbiosis, preserving anaerobes and polyamine levels. Mediation analysis revealed that antibiotic-induced suppression of anaerobes and polyamine depletion were the primary drivers of reduced calprotectin levels, implicating polyamine metabolism as a key molecular bridge linking microbial diversity loss to impaired immune development during critical early-life windows.

## Population-level metabolic variation and ancestry

A large-scale multiomics study of 322 healthy individuals across European, East Asian, and South Asian ancestry groups revealed striking ethnicity- and geography-associated differences in metabolic phenotypes.⁴ Metabolomics profiling, integrated with genomics, [[transcriptomics]], [[proteomics]], [[lipidomics]], and [[microbiomics]], identified ancestry-specific variations in lipid metabolism, immune regulation, drug metabolite production, and markers of biological aging. Notably, biological age—inferred from multiomics profiles—varied by geographic location independent of chronological age: East Asians exhibited lower biological age when sampled in their ancestral regions, whereas Europeans showed lower biological age in North American cohorts compared to European sampling sites.⁴ Diet-microbiome-metabolite interactions displayed ethnicity-specific patterns, with differential contributions of microbial metabolism to circulating and fecal metabolite pools across populations. These findings highlight how metabolomics, when deployed at population scale and integrated with other omics modalities, uncovers molecular underpinnings of health disparities, ancestry-environment interactions, and population-specific precision medicine signatures.

## Multi-omics integration and machine learning discovery

Metabolomics serves as the quantitative bridge connecting genomic variation, gene expression, protein abundance, and phenotypic outcomes. The SMAD platform demonstrated this integrative capability by simultaneous quantification of proteomics and metabolomic data from identical samples, enabling machine learning discovery of protein-metabolite relationships.¹ In the multiomics ancestry study, integration of metabolomic signatures with transcriptomics and genomics revealed how specific genetic variants associated with lipid metabolism and immune pathways manifested as coordinated shifts in gene expression, protein levels, and circulating metabolite profiles.⁴ These integrated analyses move metabolomics beyond descriptive phenotyping toward mechanistic understanding: metabolite alterations can be traced to upstream genetic and transcriptional changes, while downstream effects on immunity, aging, and drug metabolism become quantifiable and predictive.

## High-throughput drug screening applications

The scalability of direct-infusion metabolomics has enabled rapid phenotypic screening of drug effects at cellular and tissue levels. SMAD was applied to comprehensive drug screening in human mammalian cell lines using standard 96-well plate formats, with analysis of each plate completed in under five minutes.¹ This throughput allows simultaneous assessment of drug-induced changes across thousands of metabolite species and protein targets, identifying off-target metabolic perturbations and unveiling cell-type-specific drug responses. Combined metabolomic and proteomic readouts enable researchers to distinguish between intended pharmacological effects and toxic metabolic derangements, accelerating preclinical candidate selection and de-risking translational programs.
