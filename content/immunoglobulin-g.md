**Immunoglobulin G (IgG)** is the most abundant antibody class in human serum and the principal mediator of long-term humoral immunity following vaccination and natural infection. IgG functions by neutralizing pathogens, marking them for phagocytosis through opsonization, and activating complement cascades to eliminate infected cells. Its four subclasses—IgG1, IgG2, IgG3, and IgG4 in humans (IgG1, IgG2a/c, IgG2b, IgG3 in mice)—exhibit distinct effector functions and tissue distributions that are dynamically regulated by the immunological context, including the maternal immune environment during fetal development.¹ IgG responses serve as reliable biomarkers of immune activation and prior pathogen exposure, with serum titers distinguishing acute primary infections from secondary exposures across viral and bacterial pathogens.²

---

## Background

IgG is produced by plasma cells following B cell activation and class-switch recombination from IgM. The molecule consists of two heavy chains and two light chains forming a Y-shaped structure, with the variable region recognizing specific antigens and the constant Fc region mediating effector functions. Memory B cells expressing surface IgG persist for months to years after antigen exposure, providing rapid anamnestic responses upon re-encounter with the pathogen.³ These memory populations establish both systemic immunity in circulation and local mucosal protection at sites of antigen entry, including the upper respiratory tract where they display an activated CD27⁺CD21⁻ phenotype.³

The distribution of IgG subclasses is not fixed but rather shaped by developmental and environmental factors. The maternal immune environment during gestation imprints offspring IgG subclass patterns, with embryos gestated in immunologically distinct surroundings exhibiting altered IgG1 and IgG2c distributions that persist into adulthood and influence subsequent autoimmune disease susceptibility.¹

## Infection diagnostics

IgG titers serve as critical diagnostic markers for distinguishing infection stage and immune history. In viral infections such as [[dengue]] and influenza, elevated IgG indicates prior exposure and secondary immune responses, whereas elevated IgM marks acute primary infection.² The IgM/IgG ratio provides superior diagnostic accuracy: in pediatric dengue, an IgM/IgG ratio below 0.85 differentiates secondary infection from primary infection with high sensitivity and specificity (AUROC 0.793), outperforming higher ratio cutoffs.² High IgG titers (≥35) in dengue correlate with severe clinical manifestations including third spacing, thrombocytopenia, and elevated transaminases, reflecting the antibody-dependent enhancement phenomenon characteristic of secondary dengue infections.²

Following bacterial infections, pathogen-specific IgG responses target diverse surface antigens. Immunization with [[extracellular-vesicles|Group A Streptococcus-derived extracellular vesicles]] induces robust IgG responses against conserved bacterial lipoproteins including MtsA, BMP, PrsA1, PrsA2, MetQ, MalX, and the glutamine transporter GlnP.⁴ These antigens are recognized by sera from both healthy individuals and patients with necrotizing soft tissue infection, highlighting their immunodominance across diverse clinical contexts.⁴

## Autoimmune diseases

Dysregulated IgG responses and aberrant subclass distributions characterize multiple autoimmune conditions. In type 1 diabetes, the maternal immunological environment during fetal development exerts lasting effects on offspring IgG profiles.¹ NOD mouse embryos gestated in non-autoimmune C57BL/6 mothers exhibit approximately 50% reduced diabetes incidence compared to controls, accompanied by significantly increased IgM titers against insulin, DNA, and GAD65, along with altered IgG subclass distributions including elevated IgG1 and IgG2c levels.¹ UMAP and ROC analyses confirm that distinct IgG subclass patterns are influenced by the maternal environment and correlate with disease phenotype.¹

In IgA nephropathy, therapeutic modulation of B cell activity with telitacicept reduces both serum IgA and IgG levels, with reductions correlating with decreased proteinuria and improved renal function.⁵ This demonstrates that IgG levels serve not only as biomarkers but as therapeutic targets in antibody-mediated autoimmune pathology.⁵

## Vaccine-induced immunity

Intranasal vaccination with live-attenuated influenza virus ([[flumist|FluMist]]) induces substantial local IgG⁺ memory B cell responses in the upper respiratory tract, despite minimal systemic antibody production.³ These mucosal IgG⁺ memory B cells specific for H1, H3, and influenza B virus hemagglutinin remain elevated at six months post-vaccination and display an activated CD27⁺CD21⁻ phenotype, associated with the presence of circulating T follicular helper cells.³ This local IgG memory compartment provides durable protection at the primary site of viral entry, complementing systemic immunity generated by intramuscular vaccines.³ In contrast, intramuscular vaccination elevates circulating antibodies and systemic memory B cells but fails to establish detectable activated memory populations in the upper airway.³

## Therapeutic antibodies

Monoclonal antibodies engineered as IgG variants represent the predominant format for therapeutic antibodies due to their favorable pharmacokinetics, Fc effector functions, and established manufacturing platforms. However, IgG stability is vulnerable to environmental stressors during biomanufacturing. During routine buffer exchange into phosphate-buffered saline at physiological pH, trace catalytic copper leached from disposable chromatography columns coordinates to the antibody hinge region, driving highly localized Fenton-like oxidative cleavage.⁶ Complex IgG architectures such as 2+1 CrossMabs exhibit significantly higher susceptibility than standard IgG formats, suggesting that steric crowding enhances accessibility of the metal-binding site.⁶ This copper-mediated fragmentation can be effectively mitigated by EDTA-based column conditioning protocols that eliminate the leachable catalyst.⁶

Physical characterization of therapeutic IgG solutions, including viscosity measurements critical for syringeability and patient experience, has been accelerated by imaging-based viscometry platforms capable of analyzing IgG1 and IgG2 solutions at concentrations up to 150 mg/mL with minimal sample volume requirements.⁷
