**Crohn's disease** is a chronic inflammatory bowel disease characterized by dysregulated immune signaling in the gastrointestinal tract. The pathogenesis involves proinflammatory [[tl1a|TL1A]] signaling via [[dr3|DR3]] and [[il-23|IL-23]] signaling as key disease drivers.¹ Dysregulated [[regulatory-t-cells|regulatory T cell]] biology perpetuates chronic intestinal inflammation, a mechanism recently illuminated through spatial transcriptomics profiling.³ Novel monoclonal antibody therapies targeting [[tl1a|TL1A]] (SPY002 and SPY072) and selective IL-23 p19 inhibitors ([[risankizumab]]) have demonstrated clinical efficacy, particularly in patients refractory to multiple biologic therapies, marking significant therapeutic progress in this complex disease.¹²

---

## Pathogenic immune circuits

Crohn's disease pathogenesis is driven by dysregulated [[tl1a|TL1A]] signaling, a proinflammatory cytokine in the TNF superfamily that signals through [[dr3|DR3]] on T helper cells, innate lymphoid cells, and fibroblasts.¹ This dysregulated signaling promotes pathogenic inflammation within the intestinal mucosa and sustains local immune activation across multiple cell types. Concurrently, [[il-23|IL-23]] signaling through the p19 subunit drives Th17 differentiation and sustains innate lymphoid cell activation, perpetuating chronic intestinal inflammation.¹ The convergence of dysregulated [[tl1a|TL1A]]/[[dr3|DR3]] and [[il-23|IL-23]] pathways creates a pathogenic immune microenvironment resistant to single-pathway interventions, necessitating therapeutic approaches that target distinct molecular mechanisms.

---

## Anti-TL1A monoclonal antibodies

[[tl1a|TL1A]]-targeting monoclonal antibodies represent a novel therapeutic strategy for Crohn's disease management. SPY002 and SPY072 are two extended half-life anti-[[tl1a|TL1A]] monoclonal antibodies engineered to achieve selective, high-affinity binding to human [[tl1a|TL1A]] and potent functional inhibition of [[dr3|DR3]] signaling.¹ Through rational Fc engineering, both antibodies exhibit attenuated Fc effector function and enhanced FcRn binding at acidic pH, resulting in markedly extended pharmacokinetic profiles that support quarterly or biannual dosing schedules.¹ This extended half-life represents a significant clinical advantage, enabling simplified treatment regimens compared to conventional therapeutic antibodies requiring more frequent dosing. Preclinical safety studies demonstrated no drug-related adverse effects at exposures greater than ten-fold those anticipated in clinical trials, providing reassuring pharmacovigilance data.¹ In rodent models of colitis and psoriasis, anti-[[tl1a|TL1A]] treatment achieved comparable efficacy to established TNF antagonists and anti-[[il-23|IL-23]] agents, validating the therapeutic potential of [[tl1a|TL1A]] inhibition for immune-mediated inflammatory diseases.¹

---

## IL-23 p19 inhibition and risankizumab

[[risankizumab]], a selective inhibitor of the [[il-23|IL-23]] p19 subunit, has recently been approved for treatment of moderate-to-severe Crohn's disease.² Risankizumab demonstrates particular efficacy in patients refractory to multiple biologic therapies, where conventional TNF antagonists and integrin inhibitors have proven ineffective.² Systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials and real-world evidence confirms the efficacy and favorable tolerability of [[risankizumab]] across diverse patient populations.² The selectivity for the p19 subunit enables targeted blockade of [[il-23|IL-23]] while preserving IL-12 signaling — a mechanistic distinction that may contribute to improved safety and efficacy profiles compared to pan-[[il-23|IL-23]] inhibitors.

## Tissue-resolved immune characterization

Spatial transcriptomics platforms have transformed understanding of Crohn's disease by revealing cell-type-specific and tissue-resolved immune dysregulation. A comprehensive spatially resolved transcriptomics atlas constructed from over 100 intestinal tissue sections across multiple inflammatory bowel disease consortia, encompassing over three million cells, systematically benchmarked two leading imaging-based spatial platforms: [[cosmx|CosMx]] and [[xenium|Xenium]].³ [[cosmx|CosMx]] achieved higher detection efficiency compared to [[xenium|Xenium]] across both Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis tissues, whereas [[xenium|Xenium]] performance was modulated by tissue type, block quality, and panel size.³ Critically, spatial multi-omics analysis identified dysregulated [[regulatory-t-cells|regulatory T cell]] biology as a hallmark of both disease subtypes, with findings validated through independent laboratory experiments and multi-plex spatial profiling.³ These tissue-resolved insights enable more reliable application of single-cell-resolution spatial technologies in translational disease research and support development of diagnostic strategies that capture the heterogeneous immune dysregulation underlying individual patient disease phenotypes.

---

## Management of refractory disease

Crohn's disease management remains particularly challenging in patients refractory to multiple biologic therapies, where conventional TNF antagonists, integrin inhibitors, and other established agents have proven ineffective. The emergence of [[tl1a|TL1A]]-targeting monoclonal antibodies and selective [[il-23|IL-23]] p19 inhibitors represents a significant therapeutic advancement, offering mechanistically distinct pathways for patients with treatment-resistant disease.¹² These novel agents expand the therapeutic arsenal and enable sequential or combination strategies informed by advancing molecular characterization of intestinal tissue, potentially improving outcomes in this difficult-to-treat patient population.
