**Nano-prodrugs** are self-assembled nanoparticulate systems that integrate drug, response, and modification modules into coherent nanosystems designed to enhance targeted delivery while minimizing systemic toxicity. The topology of modification module conjugates—whether linear, branched, or cyclic—critically governs assembly kinetics, drug release profiles, and antitumor bioactivity, enabling systematic optimization of the efficacy-toxicity balance.¹ These nanosystems have emerged as a powerful platform for cancer nanotherapeutics, particularly for oral delivery to the colorectum, where precise site-specific release is essential.

---

## Mechanism

Nano-prodrugs are formed through self-assembly of prodrug conjugates, where an active drug such as [[docetaxel]] is covalently linked to fatty acid-based modification modules composed of [[orthoester]] and [[carboxymethyl-chitosan]]. The molecular architecture of these modification modules determines how the nanoparticles assemble: linear topologies facilitate optimal hydrophobic interactions and local energetic environments for rapid and uniform nanoparticle formation, while branched and cyclic variants modulate these interactions differently, leading to distinct assembly mechanisms.¹ Once assembled, the [[orthoester]] components enable progressive hydrolysis in response to physiological pH gradients, triggering controlled drug release at targeted sites.² The polysaccharide-based matrix provides protection against gastric acid degradation and enables dynamic size changes through protonation and deprotonation mechanisms, enhancing intestinal absorption and selective accumulation at tumor sites.²

---

## Colorectal cancer applications

Nano-prodrugs have demonstrated remarkable efficacy in [[colorectal-cancer]] therapy when delivered through an oral route using a dynamic polysaccharide-based hydrogel matrix. The hydrogel protects the nanoparticles from degradation in the acidic stomach environment while allowing progressive pore expansion triggered by pH changes in the small intestine and colon.² This approach enables precise site-specific release of [[docetaxel]]-based nano-prodrugs directly at tumor sites, where they accumulate preferentially through dynamic size-change mechanisms and charge modulation.² The result is enhanced selective cytotoxicity against colorectal cancer cells with reduced systemic exposure, minimizing off-target toxicity compared to conventional intravenous chemotherapy.

---

## Topology-performance relationships

The molecular topology of nano-prodrug modification modules encodes a clear structure-function relationship between assembly properties, drug release kinetics, and biological activity.¹ Linear nano-prodrugs show superior performance across key functional metrics: they exhibit the fastest and most efficient assembly kinetics, the most controlled and complete drug release profiles, and the greatest antitumor efficacy both in vitro and in vivo.¹ In contrast, cyclic nano-prodrugs prioritize safety, achieving maximal reduction in systemic toxicity despite displaying reduced potency compared to their linear counterparts.¹ Branched nano-prodrugs occupy an intermediate position, balancing assembly efficiency, release kinetics, and therapeutic performance between the linear and cyclic extremes.¹ This topology-dependent relationship provides a rational design strategy for cancer nanomedicine, where the choice of modification module topology can be deliberately selected to either maximize efficacy for aggressive tumors or prioritize safety for chemotherapy-sensitive patients.
