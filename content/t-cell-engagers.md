**T-cell engagers (TCEs)** are bispecific antibody-based immunotherapies engineered to cross-link [[cd3]]-expressing T cells directly to tumor-associated antigens, enabling potent and targeted T-cell-mediated cytotoxicity.¹ Unlike conventional monoclonal antibodies that rely on Fc effector mechanisms, TCEs function through direct physical bridging of the adaptive immune compartment to malignant cells, placing the quality and functional state of the T-cell response at the center of therapeutic efficacy. The clinical landscape has been fundamentally transformed by TCEs in hematological malignancies, with [[glofitamab]] exemplifying the class's potential in relapsed or refractory B-cell non-Hodgkin lymphoma, while next-generation approaches targeting solid tumor antigens such as [[egfr]] promise to extend TCE utility beyond blood cancers.

---

## Mechanism

TCEs function by simultaneously binding two distinct antigens: a tumor-associated antigen on malignant cells and the CD3 epsilon chain on T cells. This dual engagement creates a physical synapse between effector and target, recruiting T cells into intimate contact with tumor cells regardless of T-cell receptor specificity or class I major histocompatibility complex matching. The bispecific architecture can be engineered in multiple stoichiometric configurations — including 1+1 (one arm per antigen) and 2+1 (two arms for tumor antigen, one for CD3) formats — allowing fine-tuning of avidity and functional properties.² Once recruited, T cells are activated through CD3 cross-linking and release cytotoxic granules containing perforin and granzyme, inducing target cell apoptosis. Unlike conventional checkpoint inhibition, which requires pre-existing tumor-reactive T cells, TCEs function through antigen-directed recruitment and can mobilize the broader T-cell repertoire to recognize otherwise immunologically silent tumor antigens.

## Hematologic malignancies and clinical transformation

TCEs have recently transformed the treatment of relapsed or refractory B-cell non-Hodgkin lymphoma, a setting historically characterized by dismal prognosis and limited options. [[glofitamab]], a CD19-directed TCE, demonstrated potent clinical activity in this population, establishing TCEs as a new standard-of-care approach.¹ The clinical efficacy of TCEs in this indication depends critically on the immune cell composition and functional states of T cells at the time of treatment initiation and during early therapy. Maintenance of naïve-like, "fresher" T-cell populations — particularly fresher cytotoxic CD8+ T cells — at early treatment timepoints correlates strongly with durable clinical responses and metabolic complete remission.¹ Conversely, T-cell populations skewed toward exhaustion or activation-induced dysfunction are associated with progressive disease despite TCE exposure. This mechanistic insight has immediate implications for patient selection and timing of TCE administration, suggesting that earlier intervention — before irreversible T-cell exhaustion develops — may optimize therapeutic benefit.

## Solid tumors and EGFR targeting

Extension of TCE technology to solid tumors has been hampered by the challenge of identifying suitable tumor-associated antigens and by the immunologically "cold" microenvironment characteristic of many epithelial malignancies. [[epidermal-growth-factor-receptor|EGFR]] has emerged as an attractive target due to its surface expression, role in driving oncogenic signaling, and clinical validation as a therapeutic target in multiple carcinomas. However, intrinsic and acquired resistance to EGFR-directed antibodies such as [[cetuximab]] — particularly through selection of missense mutations in the EGFR extracellular domain — has limited the durability of monotherapy approaches. Novel bispecific EGFR×[[cd3]] TCEs have been engineered to overcome this resistance through strategic epitope selection: these agents bind to regions of the EGFR extracellular domain overlapping but distinct from the cetuximab-binding site, thereby retaining high affinity for clinically relevant escape variants including S492R and G465R.² Importantly, these next-generation EGFR TCEs induce robust T-cell-mediated cytotoxicity against both wild-type EGFR-expressing tumor cells and those bearing escape variants, offering a mechanistic path toward overcoming acquired resistance. The optimization of stoichiometric architecture — with 1+1 and 2+1 configurations showing enhanced T-cell engagement and tumor control — provides additional leverage for maximizing efficacy in solid tumors.

## T-cell exhaustion and resistance mechanisms

Despite initial clinical responses, TCE resistance frequently emerges through accumulated T-cell dysfunction within the tumor microenvironment. T-cell exhaustion — characterized by upregulation of inhibitory receptors such as [[pd-1|PD-1]] and [[tim-3|TIM-3]], loss of effector cytokine production, and metabolic reprogramming toward a quiescent state — represents a major mechanism of TCE resistance.¹ Chronically stimulated T cells in the tumor compartment become progressively dysfunctional, limiting their capacity to sustain cytotoxic responses even when TCE-mediated engagement is maintained. Additionally, in the case of EGFR-targeted TCEs, tumor cell-autonomous escape through selection of missense mutations in the EGFR extracellular domain can restore T-cell engagement capacity if the escape variant preserves the TCE epitope.² Understanding and mitigating these resistance mechanisms remains a critical frontier for improving durable benefit from TCE therapy.

## Immune profiling and response prediction

Single-cell RNA sequencing has emerged as a powerful tool for characterizing T-cell heterogeneity and predicting patient response to TCE therapy. Longitudinal scRNA-seq analyses of peripheral blood mononuclear cells from TCE-treated patients have revealed that responders maintain higher proportions of naïve-like and central memory T cells throughout treatment, while nonresponders exhibit progressive skewing toward exhausted or dysfunctional states.¹ These transcriptional signatures identify distinct T-cell clusters with predictive value for clinical outcomes, enabling prospective stratification of patients most likely to benefit from TCE therapy. Integration of functional assays — including T-cell proliferation and cytokine release assays — with transcriptomic profiling provides comprehensive insight into both the potential and the limitations of the T-cell response at treatment initiation, informing decisions about timing, patient selection, and potential combination strategies with agents targeting T-cell exhaustion.
