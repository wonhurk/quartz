**ALK** (ALK receptor tyrosine kinase) is a receptor tyrosine kinase gene frequently mutated or rearranged in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), defining a clinically important disease subset termed ALK-positive NSCLC.¹ Patients with ALK-positive disease are treated with selective ALK inhibitors such as [[alectinib]], which suppress ALK-driven oncogenic signaling. However, alectinib therapy is associated with concurrent hepatotoxicity and dermatotoxicity that limit clinical utility. These toxicities arise through a previously unknown mechanism involving alectinib-triggered selective autophagy of hepatic [[biotinidase|biotinidase (BTD)]], leading to systemic biotin deficiency that drives both hepatocyte apoptosis and skin barrier dysfunction.¹ Biotin supplementation effectively mitigates both toxicities, offering a strategy for safer ALK inhibitor therapy. Additionally, the composition of immune infiltrates within the tumor microenvironment—particularly CD8+ T-cell density—influences the efficacy of combination ALK-directed and immunotherapy approaches.

---

## Biology and mutations in NSCLC

ALK is a receptor tyrosine kinase whose somatic mutations and chromosomal rearrangements occur in approximately 3–5% of non-small cell lung cancers, creating a distinct molecular subtype termed ALK-positive NSCLC.¹ These rearrangements result in constitutively active fusion proteins that drive proliferation and survival through aberrant receptor signaling. ALK-positive NSCLC is diagnosed primarily in adenocarcinomas and carries distinct clinical features including younger age at diagnosis, advanced-stage disease at presentation, and exquisite sensitivity to ALK-targeted small-molecule inhibitors.

---

## ALK-positive NSCLC and targeted therapy

Alectinib serves as a standard first-line ALK inhibitor for patients with metastatic ALK-positive NSCLC, selectively suppressing ALK kinase activity and preventing downstream oncogenic signaling.¹ The drug achieves clinical responses in the majority of treatment-naïve patients, with durable remission in some cases. However, the utility of alectinib is substantially limited by hepatotoxicity and dermatotoxicity, whose mechanisms remained unclear until recently.

---

## Alectinib-induced toxicity: the biotin deficiency mechanism

A critical mechanism underlying alectinib-induced hepatotoxicity and dermatotoxicity was recently unveiled through integrated multi-omics analysis. Alectinib triggers selective macroautophagy of hepatic biotinidase (BTD), the enzyme responsible for biotin recycling and bioavailability.¹ This selective degradation is mediated by increased phosphorylation of the autophagy adaptor NBR1 at a previously uncharacterized Ser656 site, which confers protein stability and directs BTD for autophagic degradation.¹ The ensuing systemic biotin deficiency drives hepatocyte apoptosis, manifesting clinically as elevated liver enzymes and hepatotoxicity. Dermatotoxicity—evident as skin rashes and barrier dysfunction—is secondary to hepatotoxicity, arising through the same biotin deficiency mechanism and consequent impairment of skin epithelial cell function.¹

Exogenous biotin supplementation concurrently mitigates both alectinib-induced hepatotoxicity and dermatotoxicity, providing a clinically applicable mitigation strategy.¹ This discovery exemplifies a broader principle of drug-induced multi-organ toxicity driven by dysregulated inter-organ crosstalk, in which liver dysfunction propagates systemic biotin deficiency with cascading consequences across distant tissues.

## Immunotherapy context and tumor microenvironment

ALK-positive NSCLC patients are increasingly treated with combination approaches integrating [[alectinib|ALK inhibition]] and immune checkpoint inhibitors targeting the PD-1 axis. The efficacy of this combination strategy depends critically on the composition and phenotype of immune infiltrates within the tumor microenvironment. Spatial single-cell proteotyping of metastatic NSCLC has revealed that [[cd8-t-cells|CD8+ tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes]] (TILs) are the only lymphocyte subset consistently associated with durable immunotherapy response, displaying a CD39/CD103/Ki-67+ exhausted-yet-functional phenotype.² Conversely, high expression of [[cd73|CD73]] on cancer cells confers tolerance to immune checkpoint inhibitors specifically in EGFR/ALK-oncogene-positive NSCLC, potentially through accumulation of M2-polarized tumor-associated macrophages and aberrant angiogenesis.² This observation underscores the importance of characterizing the immunosuppressive microenvironment in ALK-positive disease when designing combination ALK inhibitor and immunotherapy strategies.
