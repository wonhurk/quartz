**Antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs)** are a class of targeted cancer therapeutics that combine target-selective [[monoclonal-antibody|monoclonal antibodies]] with potent cytotoxic [[payload|payloads]], enabling selective delivery of lethal agents directly to tumor cells while minimizing systemic toxicity to healthy tissue.¹ The technology integrates three critical components—the antibody, the [[linker]], and the cytotoxic payload—whose multiparameter optimization profoundly affects therapeutic efficacy, stability, tolerability, and resistance. Central to ADC performance is the [[drug-to-antibody-ratio|drug-to-antibody ratio (DAR)]], which must be balanced to maximize cytotoxic potential while maintaining biophysical stability and minimizing off-target effects and aggregation.

---

## Mechanism and Architecture

ADCs function through a multi-step cascade: the [[monoclonal-antibody|antibody]] recognizes and binds its cognate tumor-associated antigen, mediating receptor-mediated internalization into tumor cells; intracellular proteolytic or reductive cleavage of the [[linker]] then releases the potent [[payload|cytotoxic payload]], which induces cell death through target-dependent mechanisms.¹ The [[linker]] must meet competing demands: it must be sufficiently stable in circulation to prevent premature payload release and systemic toxicity, yet readily cleaved upon internalization to maximize local drug concentration. Protease-cleavable peptide linkers, particularly dipeptide constructs such as Ala-Ala, balance these requirements while enabling controlled intracellular drug release.²

[[Conjugation]] site selection profoundly influences ADC structure and stability. Site-specific conjugation to engineered [[cysteine]] residues preserves the antibody's higher-order structure better than conjugation to naturally occurring interchain cysteines, as revealed by ion mobility-mass spectrometry and collision-induced unfolding studies.³ The chemistry of linker attachment is equally important; self-hydrolyzing [[maleimide]] moieties with α-branched PEGylated amide architecture improve linker stability while reducing aggregation below 5%.²

---

## Payload Development and Design

[[Camptothecin]]-derived payloads have emerged as particularly potent and modular scaffolds for ADC development. [[Exatecan]] analogs, modified at the amine position and optimized for protease-cleavability and permeability, demonstrate exceptional potency combined with favorable biophysical properties and in vivo pharmacokinetics.¹ α-Amino-isobutyramido-exatecan constructs, synthesized through iterative optimization of p-aminobenzyl carbamate (PABC) linker variants, achieve low aggregation, maintained DAR stability, and robust tumor regression across multiple antibody-antigen pairs.¹ Butanediol exatecan-amide payloads further refine this class, providing stable high-DAR ADCs—up to full drug loading—without loss of conjugate integrity in circulation or intracellularly.²

---

## Structural Characterization and Biophysical Assessment

Ion mobility-mass spectrometry (IM-MS) combined with collision-induced unfolding (CIU) has become essential for elucidating how [[linker]]-[[payload]] structure influences ADC higher-order structure and thermal stability.³ CIU measurements reveal that different linker-payload architectures differentially affect gas-phase stability of the Fc domain, and a direct correlation exists between gas-phase stability (CIU50) and bulk thermal stability (Tm).³ This approach also detects position-specific effects: conjugation at interchain versus site-specific engineered cysteines produces distinct conformational signatures, with interchain conjugation causing greater structural disruption.³ Moreover, IM-MS resolves positional isomers arising from stochastic conjugation, demonstrating that within a given [[drug-to-antibody-ratio|DAR]], positional heterogeneity subtly alters stability and likely efficacy.³

---

## Development Challenges and Resistance

Resistance to established ADCs represents a major clinical challenge driving ongoing optimization. Resistance mechanisms include reduced antigen expression, impaired antibody internalization, alterations in linker-cleavage efficiency, and dysregulated intracellular payload metabolism. The multiparameter nature of ADC design—simultaneously optimizing the antibody, linker, payload, [[drug-to-antibody-ratio|DAR]], and conjugation site—creates a complex optimization landscape where improvements in one dimension may compromise another (e.g., increased [[drug-to-antibody-ratio|DAR]] may improve potency but reduce stability). Addressing these challenges requires integrated computational, synthetic, and analytical approaches to navigate structure-activity relationships and predict in vivo performance.
