**CD3** is a surface antigen expressed on mature T lymphocytes that serves as a critical component of the [[T-cell receptor|T-cell receptor (TCR) complex]], enabling T-cell identification and enumeration. Beyond its fundamental role in T-cell biology, CD3 has emerged as a pivotal therapeutic target in modern oncology through bispecific T-cell engagers (TCEs)—engineered antibody constructs that simultaneously bind tumor-associated antigens and CD3 to redirect cytotoxic T cells against malignant cells.¹ The CD3 antigen is also established as a central biomarker for assessing immune function recovery and T-cell reconstitution across diverse disease contexts, including immunocompromised states and sepsis.

---

## T-cell receptor and identification

CD3 forms part of the multi-subunit T-cell receptor complex on the surface of mature T lymphocytes, making it one of the most reliable markers for T-cell identification and enumeration in clinical and research settings.¹ The CD3+ population encompasses distinct functional subsets—CD3+CD4+ helper T cells and CD3+CD8+ cytotoxic T cells—which together constitute the adaptive immune response to infections and malignancies. Quantification of absolute CD3+ T cell counts and their major subsets provides essential information about immune competence, particularly in patients recovering from immunosuppression or undergoing immune-modulating therapies.

---

## Bispecific T-cell engagers

CD3 has become a standard T-cell engagement domain in bispecific antibody engineering. These TCEs bridge the physical gap between T cells and tumor cells by binding CD3 on one arm and a tumor-associated antigen on the other, effectively redirecting T-cell killing capacity toward malignant targets.² This approach has proven particularly valuable in overcoming resistance to conventional monoclonal antibodies. Mosunetuzumab, a [[cd20]]/CD3 bispecific TCE, demonstrated clinical efficacy in [[relapsed-refractory]] hematologic malignancies, achieving an overall response rate of 40% and a complete response rate of 20%, with durable remissions exceeding 20 months in heavily pretreated patients.³ The manageable safety profile—characterized primarily by low-grade cytokine release syndrome—positions CD3-directed TCEs as increasingly feasible options across multiple patient populations.

---

## EGFR-resistant malignancies

CD3-directed bispecific engagers have emerged as a strategy to overcome acquired resistance to [[EGFR]]-targeted therapies. Wild-type EGFR tumors initially respond to [[cetuximab]] and other EGFR-blocking antibodies; however, mutations in the extracellular domain—particularly S492R and G465R escape variants—render tumors resistant to standard therapies. Novel EGFR-specific antibodies engineered into EGFR×CD3 bispecific TCEs bind epitopes distinct from cetuximab while retaining high affinity for both wild-type and mutant EGFR variants.² These engineered TCEs efficiently induced T cell-mediated cytotoxicity against tumor cells expressing resistant EGFR mutations and demonstrated anti-tumor activity in syngeneic tumor models, establishing a promising pathway to restore therapeutic efficacy against otherwise refractory solid tumors.

---

## Immune recovery and biomarker role

CD3+ T cell subsets serve as critical biomarkers for assessing immune function, recovery, and treatment efficacy in immunosuppressed patients. In sepsis-induced immunosuppression, restoration of immune function is a primary therapeutic goal. Selenium nanoparticles administered as adjunctive therapy in septic patients significantly increased absolute counts of CD3+, CD4+, and CD8+ T cells, reflecting successful immune reconstitution alongside reductions in pro-inflammatory cytokines and improved organ function.¹ The enumeration of CD3+ cells and their subsets on days 4, 7, and 10 following intervention provided measurable evidence of immune recovery, underscoring the utility of CD3 quantification as a pragmatic clinical endpoint in conditions where immune restoration is essential to patient survival.
