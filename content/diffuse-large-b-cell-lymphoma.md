**Diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL)** is a heterogeneous hematologic malignancy of mature B cells characterized by profound epigenetic dysregulation and molecular complexity that drives pathogenesis, prognosis, and treatment response.¹ Its pathobiology is shaped by dysregulation of key oncogenic drivers including [[myc]] and [[bcl2]], with [[myd88]] and [[tp53]] alterations defining molecularly and clinically distinct disease subtypes. The disease exhibits remarkable organotropic behavior — malignant B cells preferentially invade extranodal sites in patterns that correspond to embryonic germ layer development, a phenomenon with major prognostic implications.² Beyond standard rituximab-based chemotherapy (R-CHOP), emerging therapeutic strategies target specific molecular subtypes through selective [[histone-deacetylase]] inhibition and bispecific T-cell-engaging antibodies.

---

## Mechanism and molecular heterogeneity

DLBCL arises from profound dysregulation of the epigenetic landscape, fundamentally altering gene expression programs that normally constrain B-cell proliferation and enforce cellular identity. This epigenetic perturbation intersects with recurrent oncogenic mutations in genes including [[myc]], [[bcl2]], [[myd88]], [[tp53]], [[pim1]], [[tet2]], and [[tbl1xr1]], which collectively establish the molecular foundation for lymphomagenesis. The complexity of the DLBCL genome is not random — it is spatially organized according to developmental principles, with malignant B cells acquiring mutations and invasive behaviors that mirror embryonic organotropic migration patterns. Single-cell transcriptomic analysis reveals that malignant B cells follow a developmental trajectory reflecting germ layer specification, with distinct differentiation branches characterized either by upregulated B-cell receptor signaling or sustained T-cell activation.²

## MYC/BCL2 double-expressor lymphoma

A clinically significant DLBCL subtype is defined by coexpression of the oncogenic drivers [[myc]] and [[bcl2]], termed MYC/BCL2 double-expressor lymphoma (DEL).¹ This population exhibits distinctly poor prognosis when treated with standard R-CHOP immunochemotherapy, reflecting the synergistic pro-survival and proliferative signals conferred by dual MYC and BCL2 activity. The epigenetic dysregulation underlying DEL creates a therapeutic vulnerability: histone deacetylase activity becomes essential for sustaining the malignant phenotype. Tucidinostat (also known as chidamide), an oral selective [[histone-deacetylase]] inhibitor, has demonstrated promising activity when combined with R-CHOP in DEL.¹ This approach exploits the disease's dependence on epigenetic machinery, offering a mechanistically distinct improvement over chemotherapy alone and suggesting that DEL may be amenable to targeted epigenetic therapy rather than dose escalation of conventional agents.

## Richter transformation

Richter transformation represents an aggressive clinical evolution of underlying [[chronic-lymphocytic-leukemia]] or small lymphocytic lymphoma, in which low-grade B-cell disease abruptly acquires the morphologic and clinical features of DLBCL. The transformed disease is marked by chemorefractory behavior, driving rapid clinical deterioration and poor outcomes with standard chemoimmunotherapy. Mosunetuzumab, a bispecific antibody simultaneously engaging [[cd20]] on malignant B cells and [[cd3]] on cytotoxic T lymphocytes, has emerged as an effective strategy for relapsed/refractory Richter transformation.² In a cohort of chemotherapy-resistant patients, mosunetuzumab achieved an overall response rate of 40% and a complete response rate of 20%, with responses being durable — including patients sustaining complete remission for over 20 months without further therapy and others proceeding to allogeneic stem cell transplant while in remission with no subsequent relapse. The toxicity profile was favorable, with cytokine release syndrome occurring in 65% of patients but predominantly limited to grade 1–2 severity during the initial treatment cycle, and no adverse events prompting treatment discontinuation.

## Extranodal invasion and germ layer-dependent prognosis

A striking pattern has emerged linking DLBCL's organotropic behavior to embryonic developmental processes. Extranodal invasion (ENI) — the spread of lymphoma beyond lymph nodes to solid organs and tissues — follows a germ layer-dependent specification, with distinct molecular, temporal, and prognostic features corresponding to embryonic origin.² Mesoderm-derived extranodal sites (ENI-mesoderm), including muscle, bone, fat, and connective tissue, carry the worst prognosis following R-CHOP therapy, with significantly inferior progression-free and overall survival compared to ectoderm-derived sites (ENI-ectoderm, including nervous system and skin) or endoderm-derived sites (ENI-endoderm, including gastrointestinal and pulmonary organs).

The molecular landscape of extranodal invasion is organized by germ layer origin. Ectoderm-originating ENI is characterized by mutations in [[myd88]], [[pim1]], and [[tbl1xr1]]. Endoderm-originating ENI harbors alterations in [[tp53]] and [[tet2]]. Mesoderm-originating ENI combines mutations in [[myd88]], [[pim1]], [[tbl1xr1]], and [[cd79b]], a distinctly aggressive mutational constellation.² Notably, organotropic migration exhibits temporal patterns matching embryonic germ layer development, with disease first arising in ectoderm-derived organs, subsequently spreading to endoderm-derived sites, and finally involving mesoderm-derived tissue — a migration sequence that recapitulates the developmental hierarchy of germ layer specification. This observation suggests that malignant B cells acquire developmental programs driving site-selective invasion, fundamentally reshaping our understanding of DLBCL as a disease that exploits embryonic logic for organotropic dissemination.
