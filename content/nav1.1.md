**Nav1.1** is the α-subunit (α1) of voltage-gated sodium channels, encoded by the [[scn1a]] gene and essential for neuronal excitability and action potential generation. In [[interneuron|GABAergic inhibitory interneurons]], Nav1.1 mediates rapid sodium influx during the upstroke of action potentials, enabling high-frequency firing and sustained inhibitory transmission.¹ Loss-of-function mutations in [[scn1a]] impair GABAergic signaling and cause severe inherited epileptic disorders, including [[gefs-plus|generalized epilepsy with febrile seizures plus (GEFS+)]] and [[dravet-syndrome]], a devastating neurodevelopmental condition with drug-resistant seizures and high mortality risk.² Recent advances in in vivo genome editing using prime editing and adenine base editing have achieved highly efficient correction of pathogenic [[scn1a]] alleles in mouse models, restoring neuronal function and ameliorating multiple disease phenotypes.

---

## Mechanism

Nav1.1 is a voltage-gated sodium channel with a pore-forming α-subunit and regulatory β-subunits. Upon membrane depolarization, the channel rapidly opens in response to voltage changes, allowing sodium ions to flow down their concentration gradient and generate the inward current necessary for action potential initiation and propagation. The channel exhibits rapid inactivation, which limits sodium influx and enables repolarization. In [[gaba|GABAergic]] inhibitory interneurons, Nav1.1 function is particularly critical for rapid, repetitive firing required to suppress excitatory neural circuits and maintain the balance of excitation and inhibition. Loss-of-function mutations in [[scn1a]] reduce or eliminate Nav1.1 activity, impairing the ability of inhibitory neurons to generate action potentials and release GABA. This disrupts circuit balance, leading to unopposed excitatory drive and neuronal hyperexcitability—ultimately manifesting as seizures.¹

## Generalized Epilepsy with Febrile Seizures Plus (GEFS+)

GEFS+ is an inherited epileptic syndrome predominantly caused by loss-of-function mutations in [[scn1a]], characterized by febrile seizures typically beginning in early childhood and often progressing to generalized tonic-clonic seizures in later childhood or adolescence. The disorder follows an autosomal-dominant inheritance pattern with variable penetrance. The SCN1A-K1270T mutation is a recurrent pathogenic variant in GEFS+ that substantially reduces Nav1.1 expression and sodium channel function. Prime editing has emerged as a promising strategy to directly correct these mutations at the genomic level. In the Scn1aKT/+ mouse model of GEFS+, intein-split prime editor systems delivered via adeno-associated viral (AAV) vectors to the developing brain achieved 34.7% correction of the mutant allele in cortical DNA and 81.2% correction in mRNA, resulting in restoration of inhibitory neuron function and dramatic improvement in disease phenotypes.¹ Treated mice showed 100% survival compared to 80% in control-treated animals, increased inhibitory postsynaptic current frequencies (from 0.32 to 1.32 Hz), and dramatically reduced febrile seizure frequency (from 78.6% to 13.3%).¹

## Dravet Syndrome

Dravet syndrome is a severe neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by early-onset, drug-resistant seizures, temperature-sensitive seizure exacerbation, cognitive impairment, and a high incidence of sudden unexpected death in epilepsy (SUDEP). The condition is caused by loss-of-function variants in [[scn1a]] that markedly reduce Nav1.1 channel activity. The recurrent R613X nonsense mutation is a common pathogenic variant in Dravet syndrome, introducing a premature stop codon that produces truncated, non-functional Nav1.1 protein. Adenine base editing (ABE) offers a complementary genome-editing approach to prime editing, converting pathogenic adenine-thymine base pairs to guanine-cytosine without creating double-strand breaks. In the Scn1aR613X/+ mouse model, dual-AAV serotype 9 vectors delivered an optimized ABE system to neonatal mice, achieving 59% DNA correction and 97% mRNA correction in the neocortex.² This editing restored parvalbumin-expressing inhibitory neuron excitability and sodium current to wild-type levels.² ABE treatment suppressed both spontaneous and temperature-induced seizures and achieved a 3.3-fold improvement in 45-day survival (90% in treated versus 27% in vehicle-treated mice).² Notably, ABE treatment applied at 12 days postnatal still conferred a 3.0-fold survival advantage over vehicle controls, demonstrating efficacy even in older developing brain.

## Genome Editing as Therapeutic Strategy

The success of both prime editing and adenine base editing in preclinical models demonstrates the feasibility of correcting SCN1A mutations at their source, offering the prospect of genuine disease-modifying therapy. Prime editing excels for diverse mutation types—insertions, deletions, and point mutations—and achieved restoration of seizure control and neuronal physiology in GEFS+.¹ Adenine base editing, while restricted to adenine-to-guanine conversions, achieved superior mRNA correction rates in the Dravet model and profound improvements in survival.² Notably, both approaches achieved editing in vivo in the developing brain via AAV delivery, and both minimized off-target effects and indel formation. Future work will focus on optimizing delivery to relevant brain regions in mature animals, improving editing efficiency in adult neurons, further reducing off-target activity, and transitioning these strategies toward human clinical trials for SCN1A-related epilepsies.
