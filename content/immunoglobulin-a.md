**Immunoglobulin A (IgA)** is a dimeric antibody critically positioned at the interface between host immunity and tissue pathology, serving dual roles as a frontline defense at mucosal surfaces and a pathogenic mediator in kidney disease. At mucosal barriers, IgA orchestrates protective humoral responses through the induction of durable memory B cell populations, particularly when vaccine antigens are delivered via intranasal routes.¹ Conversely, in IgA nephropathy (IgAN)—the most common primary glomerulonephritis worldwide—aberrant IgA deposition in the kidney mesangium activates complement and drives destructive inflammatory injury.² Both roles are governed by coordinated interactions with the [[complement-system|complement]] system and local B and T cell populations, making IgA a key therapeutic target across vaccine development and renal autoimmunity.

---

## Mechanism and B cell responses

IgA production is initiated following pathogenic antigen exposure, particularly at mucosal surfaces where specialized antigen-presenting cells and tissue-resident lymphocytes coordinate adaptive immunity. In response to intranasal immunization with live-attenuated influenza vaccine ([[flumist|FluMist]]), human upper respiratory tissues generate robust IgA-specific memory B cells bearing an activated CD27+CD21- phenotype.¹ These cells persist as stable populations for at least six months post-vaccination and are distinguished by their localization in nasopharyngeal tissue, contrasting sharply with intramuscular vaccination strategies that elicit primarily systemic but not local airway responses.¹ The generation of these mucosal IgA responses is coordinated with circulating T follicular helper cell populations, indicating tight synchronization between local and systemic adaptive immunity.¹

When pathogen-derived antigens—such as those from [[extracellular-vesicles|Group A Streptococcus extracellular vesicles]]—are delivered intranasally, they trigger the simultaneous induction of both IgA and IgG antibodies alongside robust T helper cell responses including Th17 and Th1 polarization.² This coordinated humoral and cellular response extends systemic immunity while establishing durable memory at the initial site of pathogen encounter.

---

## Mucosal vaccination and respiratory immunity

The intranasal administration of [[flumist|FluMist]], a live-attenuated influenza vaccine, generates substantial increases in upper respiratory tract IgA+ memory B cells specific to hemagglutinin antigens from circulating viral strains.¹ Despite minimal systemic antibody responses compared with intramuscular vaccination, intranasal FluMist recipients develop robust and persistent local IgA memory responses that remain elevated at six months and are associated with elevated circulating T follicular helper cells.¹ This finding underscores that effective mucosal vaccination operates through fundamentally distinct immunological mechanisms than parenteral vaccination, prioritizing local barrier immunity over systemic titers.

[[Group-A-Streptococcus|Group A Streptococcus]]-derived extracellular vesicles represent an emerging vaccine platform capable of inducing potent pathogen-specific IgA responses when administered via the intranasal route.² The immunogenic proteins identified within these vesicles—including lipoproteins MtsA, BMP, PrsA1, PrsA2, MetQ, and MalX—are conserved across diverse bacterial strains and stimulate IL-17A production from lung-resident and splenic immune cells.² Recognition of these antigens by serum from both healthy individuals and patients with invasive GAS disease suggests that IgA responses to these targets may contribute to natural or vaccine-induced protection.

---

## IgA nephropathy: pathophysiology and complement activation

IgA nephropathy is characterized by aberrant deposition of IgA-containing immune complexes within the kidney mesangium, triggering pathogenic complement activation predominantly through the alternative pathway.² Unlike the protective role of IgA at mucosal barriers, glomerular IgA deposition is associated with progressive kidney injury, proteinuria, and eventual loss of renal function. Complete blockade of complement—through genetic C3 deficiency—paradoxically worsens disease by increasing glomerular IgA deposition and macrophage infiltration, demonstrating that complement activation, while contributing to inflammatory injury, also facilitates clearance of circulating IgA immune complexes.² This dual role of complement necessitates therapeutic strategies that selectively modulate rather than eliminate complement activity.

Patients with higher circulating complement factor H (CFH) levels exhibit reduced mesangial IgA and C3 deposition alongside elevated circulating C3, indicating that CFH-mediated regulation of the alternative pathway enhances immune complex clearance while limiting glomerular inflammatory damage.² This protective mechanism provides a rationale for CFH supplementation as a disease-modifying approach.

---

## Therapeutic strategies in IgA nephropathy

Two complementary therapeutic strategies have emerged to reduce pathogenic IgA deposition and preserve renal function. [[Telitacicept]], a fusion protein targeting B lymphocyte stimulator and proliferation-inducing ligand, reduces circulating IgA levels and proteinuria when administered weekly.³ Notably, the combination of telitacicept with glucocorticoids achieves superior proteinuria reduction (84.1% mean decrease at six months) compared with telitacicept monotherapy (71.4% reduction), while both approaches similarly modestly preserve estimated glomerular filtration rate (approximately 8% improvement).³ Urinary soluble CD163, a biomarker of disease activity reflecting macrophage involvement, significantly decreases following telitacicept treatment, providing evidence that the drug attenuates local renal inflammation.³

Alternatively, [[complement-factor-h|complement factor H supplementation]] directly enhances macrophage-mediated clearance of circulating IgA immune complexes and reduces mesangial IgA and C3 deposition in preclinical models.² This approach avoids the potential risk of over-suppressing protective immune responses by specifically enhancing the natural regulatory capacity of the alternative pathway. The complementary mechanisms of B cell suppression (via telitacicept) and complement-pathway regulation (via CFH supplementation) suggest that combined therapeutic approaches may yield superior outcomes in resistant or advanced disease.
