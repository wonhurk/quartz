**Polyethylenimine (PEI)** is a cationic polymer widely deployed as a versatile surface coating and functional component in advanced therapeutic nanoplatforms, particularly for [[mesoporous-silica-nanoparticles|mesoporous silica nanoparticles]] (mSiO₂-NPs). Its positively charged backbone facilitates electrostatic binding of anionic drug molecules and enables sustained, pH-responsive drug release, making it a powerful tool for controlled drug delivery.¹ However, PEI's high cationic charge imposes a significant efficiency-toxicity trade-off: while it achieves exceptional drug loading capacity—up to 83% drug encapsulation efficiency (DEE)—it simultaneously induces pronounced cytotoxicity in both cancer cells and immune cells. This limitation has been substantially mitigated through strategic surface modification, particularly [[mannose|mannose]] conjugation, which preserves drug loading efficiency while markedly reducing toxicity and enhancing selective cellular uptake.¹ Beyond conventional drug carriers, PEI also functions as a bioactive component in multifunctional therapeutic platforms, where its capacity to scavenge cell-free DNA (cfDNA) contributes to inflammation suppression in immune-mediated diseases.²

---

## Mechanism

PEI operates as a surface-modifying agent through electrostatic interactions between its positively charged amine groups and the negatively charged surface of [[mesoporous-silica-nanoparticles|mesoporous silica nanoparticles]]. This cationic coating creates an electrostatically favorable microenvironment for drug loading, enabling efficient capture and retention of anionic or weakly charged therapeutic molecules. The pH-responsive behavior of PEI arises from its ionizable amine groups: at physiological pH and below, these groups remain protonated, maintaining strong electrostatic drug interactions; however, in the acidic microenvironment of endosomal compartments (pH ~5.5), the increased protonation state favors sustained drug release. For instance, PEI-coated mesoporous silica carriers loaded with nicotinic acid exhibit 41% cumulative drug release over 24 hours at pH 5.5, demonstrating a tunable, stimuli-responsive release profile.¹

Beyond drug delivery, PEI serves an additional bioactive function through cfDNA scavenging. The cationic charge of PEI enables electrostatic binding and adsorption of cell-free DNA, a pathogenic molecule in immune-mediated diseases where accumulated cfDNA perpetuates inflammatory pathways. In two-dimensional nanoplatforms incorporating both [[mxene|MXene]] and PEI, the polymer component exhibits a three-fold higher cfDNA adsorption capacity compared to unmodified MXene carriers alone, establishing PEI as an effective anti-inflammatory agent through nucleic acid sequestration.²

## Drug delivery applications

PEI-functionalized [[mesoporous-silica-nanoparticles|mesoporous silica nanoparticles]] represent an advanced platform for enhanced drug delivery, particularly in cancer therapeutics. The high drug loading achieved with PEI coating—reaching 83% DEE for model drugs—substantially exceeds that of alternative surface chemistries, offering a significant advantage for therapeutic efficacy. When loaded with nicotinic acid, a pleiotropic molecule with anti-cancer and anti-inflammatory properties, PEI-coated carriers maintain sustained pH-responsive release kinetics suitable for targeting acidic tumor microenvironments and endosomal compartments within cancer cells.¹ However, the clinical translation of these carriers is limited by PEI-induced cytotoxicity, which manifests prominently in breast cancer cell lines such as MCF-7, compromising therapeutic selectivity and tolerability.¹

---

## Toxicity mitigation through surface modification

The cationic nature of PEI, while essential for drug loading and release control, presents a major barrier to clinical application due to pronounced cytotoxicity in both malignant and non-malignant cell populations. Notably, PEI-coated carriers induce significant toxicity not only in cancer cells but also in peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs), raising concerns regarding off-target immune activation and systemic tolerability. Addressing this fundamental limitation, [[mannose|mannose]] conjugation—a biofunctional sugar moiety that engages carbohydrate-binding receptors on target cells—substantially reduces PEI-mediated toxicity while preserving drug loading efficiency and enhancing cellular uptake.¹ Mannose-functionalized PEI carriers exhibit selective targeting to monocyte subsets, indicating receptor-mediated endocytosis via carbohydrate recognition pathways, and importantly, they do not trigger immune activation despite enhanced cellular internalization. This surface modification strategy therefore resolves the efficiency-toxicity trade-off, enabling the rational design of safer, more selective nanocarriers for clinical use.¹

## Dermatological applications

Beyond oncology, PEI-containing nanoplatforms have demonstrated significant efficacy in treating immune-mediated dermatological diseases, particularly [[psoriasis|psoriasis]]. In multifunctional [[mxene|MXene]]-based nanocarriers incorporating both PEI and reactive oxygen species (ROS) scavenging functionality, the synergistic combination of cfDNA sequestration and antioxidant activity addresses the multifactorial pathogenesis of psoriasis. In imiquimod-induced psoriatic mouse models, such dual-function platforms achieved approximately 50% reduction in the Psoriasis Area and Severity Index (PASI) score, along with a 37% decrease in epidermal thickness relative to disease controls.² These improvements significantly exceeded those achieved by monotherapies targeting either ROS or cfDNA individually, establishing the importance of combinatorial approaches in dermatological nanomedicine.

The therapeutic efficacy of PEI in this context derives from its ability to intercept cfDNA—a damage-associated molecular pattern (DAMP) released from keratinocytes and immune cells during inflammation—thereby disrupting the feed-forward inflammatory loop characteristic of psoriasis. Concurrent suppression of oxidative stress via MXene-mediated ROS scavenging synergizes with cfDNA removal to broadly inhibit inflammatory signaling pathways, including TNF and IL-17 axes, which are central to psoriatic pathogenesis.²
