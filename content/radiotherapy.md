**Radiotherapy** is a mainstream [[cancer]] treatment modality that induces DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs) and generates reactive oxygen species (ROS) to trigger [[apoptosis]] in malignant cells across diverse tumor types including [[glioblastoma]], [[melanoma]], cervical cancer, and [[oropharyngeal-squamous-cell-carcinoma]].¹⁻³ Despite its widespread clinical application, intratumoral heterogeneity and adaptive resistance mechanisms—including lysosomal cholesterol accumulation, cellular senescence, mechanical dormancy in polyploid giant cancer cells, tumor-stromal exosomal signaling, and activation of [[prmt5|PRMT5]]-YAP and HDAC6-dependent DNA repair pathways—substantially compromise therapeutic efficacy and drive disease recurrence.¹⁻⁴

---

## Mechanism of action

Radiotherapy exerts its cytotoxic effects primarily through the generation of DSBs and ROS that overwhelm cellular repair capacity, leading to mitochondrial dysfunction, genomic instability, and programmed cell death.⁵ In [[glioblastoma]], radiation activates the [[prmt5|PRMT5]]-YAP-SLC3A2/7A5 signaling axis as a survival response, where PRMT5-mediated symmetrical dimethylation of YAP at arginine 124 protects against LATS-mediated inhibitory phosphorylation, facilitating nuclear translocation and transcriptional activation of methionine transporters that drive tumor methionine addiction.⁴ Similarly, in [[melanoma]] and [[uveal-melanoma|uveal melanoma]], HDAC6 interacts with DNA damage repair proteins DDB2 and DEK to suppress radiation-induced genomic damage accumulation, establishing HDAC6 expression as a predictive biomarker for radiation response.⁶

---

## Resistance mechanisms

Multiple adaptive pathways converge to confer radioresistance across tumor types. Lysosomal cholesterol deposition and cellular senescence emerge with complicated interrelation in irradiated cancer cells, with abnormal cholesterol accumulation triggering senescence-associated secretory phenotype that promotes treatment failure.¹ Polyploid giant cancer cells—a therapy-resistant subpopulation characterized by multinucleation, genetic instability, and stem-like features—adopt a distinct mechanical dormancy state marked by cortical actin remodeling, nuclear enlargement, and biomechanical stiffening, representing a latent therapeutic threat in malignant breast cancer.³ In cervical cancer, cancer-associated fibroblast-derived exosomes transfer [[bone-morphogenetic-protein-4|BMP4]] to activate the Nrf2-HO-1 antioxidant defense axis while simultaneously suppressing cuproptosis regulators, establishing a dual-pathway mechanism for treatment resistance.² Knockdown of exosomal BMP4 effectively restored radiosensitivity in vitro and significantly enhanced radiotherapy efficacy in vivo, uncovering a previously unrecognized tumor-stroma communication axis.²

## Enhancement strategies

Rational combinatorial approaches have demonstrated marked improvements in radiotherapy outcomes across multiple disease contexts. Prophylactic sequential drug administration using nanomedicines capable of stepwise release of U18666A and β-cyclodextrin in acidic, glutathione-rich tumor microenvironments inhibits abnormal lysosomal cholesterol accumulation and reduces cellular senescence, achieving potent radiosensitization and prolonged survival in preclinical models.¹ Integration with [[aav|adeno-associated vector]] platforms encoding inducible IL-12 under interferon-responsive promoters exploits radiation-enhanced AAV tumor transduction, generating highly immunostimulatory tumor microenvironments that produce robust local and systemic antitumor responses in an IFNγ- and FAS-dependent manner without significant toxicity.⁷ Pharmacological inhibition of HDAC6 with the selective inhibitor WT-161 disrupts its interaction with DNA damage repair proteins and suppresses gene expression in repair pathways, leading to accumulation of irradiation-induced damage and tumor regression in [[melanoma]].⁶ Similarly, targeting the [[prmt5|PRMT5]]-YAP-SLC3A2/7A5 axis in combination with radiotherapy impairs intracranial [[glioblastoma]] xenograft growth and results in significant survival extensions.⁴

## Neurological toxicity and mitigation

A major constraint of cranial radiotherapy is the low tolerance of normal brain tissue to radiation, with [[radiation-induced-brain-injury|cranial irradiation-induced injury]] manifesting as inflammatory responses and neurodegeneration that jointly contribute to progressive cognitive decline.⁸ Immunohistochemical analysis reveals that irradiation activates [[microglia]] for up to 84 days after exposure, generating a sustained pro-inflammatory microenvironment that restricts delivery of optimal therapeutic doses and compromises quality of life.⁸ Water-soluble fullerene derivatives, particularly C60-ser, have emerged as neuroprotective agents capable of mitigating this toxicity. Oral administration of C60-ser 24 hours after cranial irradiation of mice restored spatial working memory in Y-maze testing and markedly alleviated microglial activation, with CD68 reactivity restored to levels similar to unirradiated controls, suggesting that carbon-based nanomaterials can suppress neuroinflammation and preserve cognitive function.⁸

## De-escalation in HPV-positive disease

Patients with human papillomavirus-driven [[oropharyngeal-squamous-cell-carcinoma]] demonstrate superior locoregional control rates compared to HPV-negative disease, suggesting that they are likely being overtreated with standard adjuvant radio(chemo)therapy protocols according to national guidelines.⁹ This clinical observation has prompted investigation of safe dose reduction strategies to minimize late treatment toxicity while maintaining therapeutic efficacy, with ongoing phase I trials designed to establish the extent to which adjuvant radiotherapy can be safely de-escalated in this molecularly defined patient population.⁹

---

## Comparative cytotoxicity

Direct comparison of therapeutic modalities in pancreatic cancer models demonstrates that CRISPR/Cas9-induced DSBs are approximately three-fold more cytotoxic than radiation-induced DSBs, with targeting of nine sites using cancer-specific guide RNAs achieving more than 90% tumor growth inhibition.⁵ Bioinformatics and cytogenetics analyses revealed that CRISPR/Cas9-induced DSBs triggered ongoing chromosomal rearrangements, with 87% of structural variants not directly produced from initial DSBs, and chromosomal instability peaking before cell death.⁵ Pancreatic cancer cells that survived CRISPR/Cas9 targeting retained susceptibility to subsequent targeting at different genomic sites with more than 87% growth inhibition, supporting the therapeutic potential of gene-editing technologies as cancer-specific alternatives to radiation.⁵
