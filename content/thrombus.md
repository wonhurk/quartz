**Thrombus** (or thrombosis) is an abnormal blood clot that forms pathologically within blood vessels, a major global health threat that constitutes a principal cause of [[acute-coronary-syndrome|acute coronary syndrome (ACS)]] and [[acute-ischemic-stroke|acute ischemic stroke]].¹ The condition arises from uncontrolled [[platelet]] aggregation and fibrin cross-linking, leading to vascular occlusion and tissue ischemia. Current thrombus management relies upon multiple therapeutic modalities—including rapid antiplatelet therapy, direct thrombolytic agents, and emerging delivery systems designed to overcome poor drug penetrability and systemic toxicity.

---

## Mechanism

Thrombus formation initiates when [[platelet|platelets]] become activated at sites of vascular injury, triggering aggregation mediated by platelet glycoproteins and adhesion molecules. The coagulation cascade is simultaneously engaged, producing thrombin that cross-links soluble fibrinogen into an insoluble [[fibrin]] mesh, stabilizing the developing clot. Under pathological conditions—particularly in the coronary and cerebral circulation—this hemostatic response becomes dysregulated, generating occlusive thrombi that obstruct blood flow and precipitate acute ischemic events.

---

## Acute coronary syndrome

In [[acute-coronary-syndrome|acute coronary syndrome]], thrombus formation within the coronary artery occurs rapidly, necessitating emergent therapeutic intervention to restore perfusion. The standard approach employs dual antiplatelet therapy, with [[clopidogrel]] serving as a cornerstone agent that irreversibly inhibits [[platelet|ADP-mediated platelet]] activation. However, the oral formulation of clopidogrel is hampered by delayed gastrointestinal absorption and hepatic cytochrome P450-dependent bioactivation, limiting its utility in acute settings requiring immediate platelet inhibition during emergency percutaneous coronary intervention. Recent advances have addressed this limitation through engineering of hepatotropic micellar formulations that promote rapid liver-directed delivery and metabolic activation.² Such formulations achieve accelerated generation of the active metabolite, enabling high peak concentrations and shorter time-to-onset compared with oral administration, thereby improving clinical outcomes in acute coronary intervention scenarios.

---

## Acute ischemic stroke

Acute ischemic stroke presents a distinct thrombus-related challenge requiring multimodal therapeutic approaches. Endovascular thrombectomy—mechanical retrieval of the thrombus—represents the primary intervention for acute ischemic stroke in many clinical centers, yet its efficacy is substantially enhanced when combined with adjunctive pharmacological agents. Glenzocimab, a humanized monoclonal antibody fragment targeting [[glycoprotein-vi|platelet glycoprotein VI]], exemplifies this synergistic strategy by inhibiting thrombus growth while minimizing hemorrhagic risk—a critical distinction from conventional antiplatelet agents.³ The GREEN trial demonstrated that glenzocimab combined with endovascular thrombectomy, with or without intravenous thrombolysis with [[tissue-plasminogen-activator|tissue plasminogen activator (rt-PA)]], achieves improved reperfusion rates and reduced risk of symptomatic hemorrhagic transformation, establishing the therapeutic benefit of targeting specific platelet pathways during acute stroke management.

---

## Therapeutic challenges

Current thrombolytic approaches face substantial limitations that restrict their efficacy in clinical practice. Free rt-PA exhibits a short circulating half-life and poor penetrability into established thrombi, compounded by systemic off-target effects that increase bleeding risk and limit dosing flexibility.² The high local thrombus burden in acute ischemic stroke or massive venous thromboembolism means that conventional drug concentrations cannot adequately dissolve fibrin networks, necessitating either higher systemic doses—with attendant hemorrhagic complications—or alternative delivery strategies.

---

## Advanced delivery systems

Recent innovations have engineered sophisticated delivery platforms designed to overcome the penetrability and pharmacokinetic limitations of standard thrombolytic therapy. Chitosan microcapsules loaded with rt-PA and decorated with fibrin-targeting CREKA peptides achieve site-specific accumulation within thrombi while maintaining prolonged circulation time.² The microcapsule architecture overcomes margination effects that would otherwise exclude larger carriers from the thrombotic zone, enabling superior drug penetration into dense fibrin networks and enhanced thrombolytic efficacy. These rt-PA–loaded microcapsules demonstrate robust fibrin degradation and clot dissolution in both in vitro and in vivo models, while simultaneously downregulating key coagulation factors such as FXa and PAI-1, creating a synergistic antithrombotic effect that exceeds free rt-PA monotherapy.² Parallel approaches employ hepatotropic micellar platforms engineered to selectively recruit liver-affinity apolipoproteins (including ApoM, ApoH, and ApoA1), thereby directing drug candidates to hepatic metabolic machinery and enhancing bioactivation kinetics for rapid-onset antiplatelet therapy.

---

## Glycoprotein VI antagonism

Beyond conventional platelet inhibition, targeting specific platelet surface receptors provides a refined therapeutic approach with reduced bleeding liability. [[glycoprotein-vi|Glycoprotein VI]] serves as a key collagen receptor whose blockade inhibits both platelet activation and thrombus propagation.³ Monoclonal antibodies targeting this pathway—exemplified by glenzocimab—offer the distinctive advantage of suppressing pathological thrombus growth while preserving hemostatic function, thereby enabling their use as adjuncts to mechanical thrombectomy and thrombolytic therapy in acute stroke settings without the hemorrhagic penalties associated with conventional antiplatelet escalation.
