**Ultrasound** is a non-invasive acoustic technology employing high-frequency sound waves for therapeutic intervention and diagnostic imaging across oncology, neurology, hepatology, and tissue engineering. Unlike ionizing radiation modalities, ultrasound achieves spatiotemporal precision through cavitation dynamics, acoustic droplet vaporization, and piezoelectric field generation, enabling controlled drug release, scaffold fabrication, and neural circuit modulation.¹⁻⁴ The technology operates through dual-frequency transducer systems—typically pairing megahertz-range imaging transducers with low-frequency therapeutic transducers—to achieve both real-time visualization and localized mechanical effects.²

---

## Mechanism

Ultrasound exerts therapeutic effects through multiple biophysical mechanisms. Acoustic cavitation—the formation, oscillation, and collapse of microbubbles—generates localized mechanical forces that disrupt tissue barriers, enhance vascular permeability, and trigger phase transitions in responsive materials.² When applied at 3.5 MHz, ultrasound induces acoustic droplet vaporization of [[perfluoropentane]]-core microdroplets, transitioning them into gaseous microbubbles; subsequent low-frequency irradiation at 200 kHz drives bubble oscillation and implosion, creating precise spatial patterns of pore formation.² The technology's therapeutic window is substantially widened by dual-frequency approaches, which reduce pressure thresholds for cavitation compared to single-frequency strategies.

In neuromodulatory applications, ultrasound couples with piezoelectric nanoparticles to convert mechanical acoustic energy into electromagnetic fields with temporal precision matching neural oscillatory frequencies.⁴ This mechano-electric transduction enables non-genetic, spatially targeted modulation of neuronal activity patterns without requiring viral vector delivery or optogenetic constructs.

## Oncology drug delivery

Ultrasound-guided nanomedicine represents a precision delivery paradigm that overcomes limitations of passive tumor accumulation. When combined with coagulation-targeting peptide-modified nanoparticles, ultrasound irradiation induces localized vascular disruption that triggers in situ [[fibrin]] generation and coagulation factor XIII activation within the tumor microenvironment.¹ The activated factor XIIIa catalyzes covalent crosslinking between circulating nanoparticles and the newly formed fibrin matrix, achieving exceptional tumor enrichment—52.1-fold increase in active drug concentration compared to non-targeted controls—and 98.9% tumor suppression with durable responses in rechallenge experiments.¹ This spatiotemporally controllable system addresses both targeting specificity and prodrug activation, as ultrasound simultaneously creates binding substrates and enhances hypoxic conditions conducive to bioreductive drug activation.

For photothermal applications, ultrasound serves as the delivery trigger for [[indocyanine-green]]-conjugated [[gas-vesicles]], biosynthetic protein nanostructures derived from photosynthetic microorganisms.³ Acoustic cavitation of these ICG-loaded gas vesicles enables trimodal imaging—ultrasound, photoacoustic, and near-infrared fluorescence—for real-time visualization of delivery, while prolonging indocyanine green circulation half-life and increasing area-under-curve pharmacokinetics.³ Upon laser irradiation following ultrasound-triggered accumulation, tumors reach temperatures exceeding 60°C, achieving complete regression in murine [[bladder-cancer]] xenograft models without detectable systemic toxicity.³

## Tissue engineering

Dual-frequency ultrasound provides noninvasive control over scaffold architecture for regenerative medicine applications. Phase-shift microdroplets embedded within collagen hydrogels serve as ultrasound-responsive cavitation nuclei: a 3.5 MHz imaging transducer triggers acoustic droplet vaporization, converting liquid [[perfluoropentane]] cores into gaseous microbubbles, followed by 200 kHz therapeutic ultrasound that induces bubble oscillation and collapse to generate localized pores.² This sequential dual-frequency approach operates at reduced pressure thresholds compared to conventional acoustic droplet vaporization, with microbubble dynamics and resulting pore dimensions accurately predicted by the Marmottant theoretical model.² Ultrasound-treated scaffolds exhibit porosity of 56.53 ± 3.91% with pore diameters of 39.42 ± 10.28 μm, compared to minimal porosity in untreated controls, while rheological analysis confirms enhanced elasticity and tunable mechanical properties suitable for diverse tissue engineering applications.²

## Neuromodulation in Alzheimer's disease

Ultrasound-mediated neuromodulation addresses the therapeutic gap in [[alzheimers-disease|Alzheimer's disease]], where amyloid-β and tau-targeting strategies have failed to restore synaptic function. Focused ultrasound combined with piezoelectric Ba₀.₈₅Ca₀.₁₅Zr₀.₁Ti₀.₉O₃ nanoparticles generates targeted gamma-frequency electromagnetic fields in the hippocampal CA3 subregion of early-stage Alzheimer's mouse models, restoring impaired gamma oscillations and enhancing synaptic plasticity.⁴ This rhythmic stimulation remodels memory-related network connectivity, as validated by local field potential recordings, patch-clamp electrophysiology, and functional MRI.⁴ Mechanistically, NF-κB transcription factor activation during periodic stimulation regulates AMPAR trafficking by balancing synaptic internalization and delivery, with concurrent upregulation of P300-mediated histone acetylation.⁴ The approach establishes a non-genetic, spatially precise paradigm for neural circuit repair that bypasses the limitations of optogenetic or electrical deep brain stimulation methods.

## Diagnostic imaging

Ultrasound serves as the first-line imaging modality for assessing [[chronic-liver-disease]], liver cirrhosis, and [[hepatocellular-carcinoma]] globally, owing to its accessibility, absence of ionizing radiation, and real-time capabilities.⁵ Image quality degradation from aging equipment poses diagnostic challenges, addressable through deep learning-based enhancement algorithms. CycleGAN-based unsupervised neural networks trained on low-quality images from 12-year-old devices and high-quality targets from contemporary equipment significantly improve brightness, contrast, and overall diagnostic quality of liver ultrasound examinations.⁵ Weighted kappa values for image quality and diagnostic performance indicate substantial agreement between enhanced images and ground truth, extending the clinical utility of legacy ultrasound infrastructure.⁵

In thyroid disease management, multimodal ultrasound combined with serological markers enables predictive modeling of thermal ablation efficacy in benign thyroid nodules. [[Nomogram]] models incorporating preoperative ultrasound features and ablation volume ratios predict 1-year volume reduction outcomes, guiding patient selection and procedural planning for minimally invasive ablation therapies.⁶
