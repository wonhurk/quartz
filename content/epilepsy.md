**Epilepsy** is a chronic neurological disorder characterized by recurrent spontaneous seizures arising from abnormal synchronized electrical activity in the brain. As a progressively recognized comorbidity in [[neurocognitive-disorders|neurocognitive disorders]], particularly [[dementia]], epilepsy presents unique therapeutic challenges requiring careful selection of [[antiseizure-medications|antiseizure medications (ASMs)]]. Management is primarily pharmacological, though the specific ASM chosen significantly influences clinical outcomes including survival, especially in aged populations with dementia.¹ Recent drug development has focused on novel multitarget agents that address comorbid seizure and pain disorders simultaneously, exemplified by 3-methoxypropanamide derivatives acting through [[htr2c|5-HT2C receptor]] agonism and [[vgsc|voltage-gated sodium channel]] inhibition.²

---

## Seizure mechanisms

Seizures are generated by pathologically synchronized neuronal firing arising from disrupted balance between excitatory and inhibitory neurotransmission. Under normal conditions, [[vgsc|voltage-gated sodium channels]] and inhibitory GABAergic neurotransmission maintain neuronal excitability within controlled limits. When this balance is disrupted—through genetic mutations, acquired brain injury, metabolic disturbances, or structural lesions—hyperexcitable neuronal networks can generate seizures. The progressive nature of seizure susceptibility in some epilepsy syndromes reflects underlying neuroinflammatory processes and molecular remodeling of ion channels and synaptic receptors.

---

## Clinical management with antiseizure medications

[[antiseizure-medications|Antiseizure medications]] remain the cornerstone of epilepsy management, controlling seizures in the majority of newly diagnosed patients. Multiple drug classes are available, targeting distinct mechanisms including sodium channel blockade, enhancement of GABAergic inhibition, and modulation of synaptic vesicle release. However, the selection of a specific ASM is not merely a matter of efficacy; emerging evidence demonstrates that different ASMs carry distinct safety and tolerability profiles, with significant implications for survival and quality of life, particularly in vulnerable populations such as elderly patients with concurrent [[dementia]].

---

## Epilepsy and dementia comorbidity

[[epilepsy|Epilepsy]] is increasingly recognized as an important comorbidity in [[neurocognitive-disorders|neurocognitive disorders]], occurring at elevated rates in persons with [[dementia]] compared to the general population. The pathophysiological link between dementia and seizures remains incompletely understood but likely involves shared mechanisms of neuroinflammation, synaptic dysfunction, and neurodegeneration. The clinical significance of this comorbidity extends beyond seizure control: mounting evidence indicates that the choice of ASM is associated with differences in survival outcomes in persons with both epilepsy and dementia.¹ This observation underscores the importance of individualized medication selection in aging populations, where drug-drug interactions, altered metabolism, and comorbid conditions compound the complexity of antiseizure therapy.

## Novel multitarget agents

Recent medicinal chemistry efforts have yielded novel 3-methoxypropanamide derivatives that expand the therapeutic arsenal beyond traditional monotherapy approaches. The lead compound, designated [[r-46|(R)-46]], demonstrates broad-spectrum antiseizure activity across multiple preclinical seizure models with potent in vivo efficacy.² In the maximal electroshock seizure (MES) model, [[r-46|(R)-46]] achieves an ED₅₀ of 35.6 mg/kg; in the 6 Hz kindling paradigm at 32 mA, an ED₅₀ of 8.4 mg/kg; and at 44 mA, an ED₅₀ of 19.1 mg/kg, with these doses significantly elevating seizure thresholds without impairing grip strength.² Chronic administration in the pentylenetetrazol (PTZ) kindling model suppresses seizure progression, with minimal alterations to hippocampal inflammatory markers and amino acid profiles, suggesting favorable safety in long-term use.²

Beyond seizure suppression, [[r-46|(R)-46]] exhibits antinociceptive effects across multiple pain models including formalin-, capsaicin-, oxaliplatin-, and streptozotocin-induced pain, addressing the unmet clinical need in patients with comorbid seizure and pain disorders.² The compound's dual mechanism involves [[htr2c|5-HT2C receptor]] agonism and inhibition of [[vgsc|voltage-gated sodium channels]], distinct from the primary targets of most established ASMs. Pharmacokinetic and absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion (ADME) studies indicate favorable drug-like properties, supporting advancement toward clinical development.²
