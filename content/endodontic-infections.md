**Endodontic infections** are bacterial infections of the root canal system characterized by the formation of resilient [[oral-biofilms|biofilms]] that contribute to pulpitis and progressive disease. Unlike simple bacterial colonization, endodontic pathogens establish complex microbial communities protected by extracellular matrix components, rendering them extraordinarily resistant to conventional disinfectants and antimicrobial agents.¹ The intricate anatomy of root canals—with lateral canals, apical ramifications, and dentinal tubules—provides biofilms with refuge from therapeutic agents, resulting in high treatment failure and recurrence rates despite decades of refinement of conventional endodontic therapy.² Recent advances employing nanotechnology-based platforms have begun to overcome these limitations through multifunctional strategies that combine biofilm disruption, controlled drug delivery, enzymatic matrix degradation, and enhanced antimicrobial activity at substantially reduced doses of conventional agents.

---

## Mechanism and clinical challenge

Endodontic infections initiate when oral bacteria, particularly *Streptococcus mutans* and *Enterococcus faecalis*, colonize the pulp chamber following caries progression or trauma. Within the root canal environment, these bacteria form mature biofilms consisting of microbial aggregates embedded in self-produced polysaccharide matrices. The protective matrix of these [[oral-biofilms|biofilms]] shields bacterial cells from antimicrobial agents and host immune defenses, enabling persistence despite exposure to conventional disinfectants such as chlorhexidine and sodium hypochlorite.¹ The tortuous anatomy of root canals—including lateral canals and dentinal microtubules—creates regions inaccessible to conventional instruments and irrigants, allowing bacteria to survive in these protected microenvironments. As a result, conventional endodontic treatment often fails to achieve complete bacterial elimination, necessitating repeated interventions and leaving patients at high risk of recurrent infection and tooth loss.²

## Nanomotor-based biofilm disruption

A transformative approach employs self-propelled Janus [[platinum-mesoporous-silica-nanomotors|platinum mesoporous-silica nanomotors]] that combine enzymatic biofilm matrix degradation with controlled antimicrobial delivery.¹ These nanodevices are loaded with low-concentration chlorhexidine and functionalized with a pH-dependent molecular gate bound to ficin protease, which acts as an enzymatic drill to degrade biofilm matrix components. Upon exposure to hydrogen peroxide in the acidic root canal environment, the nanomotors self-propel through chemotaxis, actively migrating toward biofilms and penetrating dentinal microtubules. The acidic microenvironment then triggers protease release, which enzymatically degrades the biofilm matrix and undermines its structural integrity. Remarkably, H₂O₂-activated nanomotors carrying chlorhexidine at ten times lower concentrations than commercial formulations exhibited potent antimicrobial effects against *Streptococcus mutans* biofilms on dentinal sections, whereas chlorhexidine alone showed minimal impact.¹ This dramatic dose reduction decreases toxicity to periapical tissues and host cells while maintaining or enhancing bactericidal efficacy—a critical advantage for endodontic applications.

## Photothermal nanozyme strategy

A complementary approach employs a multifunctional hybrid nanozyme designated [[pb-mos2-au|PB@MoS₂@Au]], consisting of a Prussian blue nanocube core decorated with molybdenum disulfide shells and gold nanoparticles.² This nanozyme integrates enhanced peroxidase-like catalytic activity with exceptional photothermal conversion efficiency of 37.4% within the mild-hyperthermia window (approximately 50°C). When combined with low-concentration hydrogen peroxide and near-infrared irradiation, the PB@MoS₂@Au nanozyme exhibited bactericidal rates exceeding 95% against seven representative oral bacterial species and achieved bacterial killing rates exceeding 90% within biofilms. Most strikingly, in an ex vivo root canal infection model, this combined photochemical-photothermal treatment eliminated over 99.5% of *Enterococcus faecalis* biofilms from within root canals in minutes.² The dual catalytic and thermal mechanisms work synergistically: peroxidase activity generates reactive oxygen species that damage bacterial cell membranes, while photothermal heating augments enzymatic activity and disrupts biofilm structural integrity. This approach overcomes the limitation of previous photothermal strategies that relied on high temperatures incompatible with vital pulp preservation.

## Vital pulp therapy with bioactive nanoparticles

Beyond traditional root canal disinfection, endodontic treatment increasingly emphasizes pulp preservation through vital pulp therapy, wherein infected pulp tissue is partially removed but remaining vital tissue is treated to promote self-repair and long-term tooth retention. [[ga-mbgn|Gallium-doped mesoporous bioactive glass nanoparticles (Ga-MBGNs)]] represent a multifunctional platform that simultaneously controls bacterial infection, suppresses excessive inflammation, and promotes regenerative dentin formation.³ These nanoparticles directly inhibit endodontic pathogens while activating anti-inflammatory NRF2-HO-1 and NF-κB-suppressing pathways in host cells, reducing reactive oxygen species and attenuating the inflammatory response. Mechanistically, sustained release of gallium ions (Ga³⁺) from the mesoporous architecture provides long-term antimicrobial activity while promoting odontoblastic differentiation of human dental pulp cells in vitro. In mouse vital pulp capping models, Ga-MBGNs significantly induced reparative dentin formation, maintained pulp vitality, and reduced inflammatory infiltration—outcomes unattainable with conventional materials that focus solely on disinfection without addressing regeneration.³ This immunomodulatory and regenerative capacity establishes a new therapeutic paradigm distinct from traditional endodontic approaches, prioritizing tissue preservation and long-term functional recovery over complete pulp removal.
