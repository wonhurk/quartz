**HIV-1** is a retrovirus that establishes persistent infection in [[cd4-t-cells|CD4+ T cells]]—the central regulators of adaptive immune responses—through irreversible genomic integration of its proviral DNA into the host chromosome. Although [[antiretroviral-therapy|antiretroviral therapy (ART)]] suppresses viral replication to clinically undetectable levels, integrated proviruses persist indefinitely and retain the capacity to reactivate, preventing cure.¹ The virus systematically disables immune function through multiple mechanisms: the viral accessory protein Nef disrupts the actin cytoskeleton by inhibiting the WAVE2-ARP2/3 complex, impairing T-cell migration and immune synapse formation,² while the heavily glycosylated envelope protein evades broadly neutralizing antibodies such as [[vrc01|VRC01]] through site-specific glycosylation mutations.³

---

## Mechanisms of immune dysfunction

HIV-1 Nef and Gag proteins fundamentally compromise the cytoskeletal architecture of infected [[cd4-t-cells|CD4+ T cells]], rendering them unable to execute normal immune functions. Nef inhibits the WAVE2 complex—specifically through inhibitory phosphorylation of WASF2—which in turn blocks the ARP2/3 complex responsible for nucleating branched actin filaments at the leading edge of migrating cells.² This produces characteristic lamellipodial abnormalities observable by ultrastructural microscopy and impairs both directional migration toward sites of infection and formation of stable immune synapses with antigen-presenting cells. Direct chemical inhibition of ARP2/3 recapitulates the morphological defects seen in Nef-expressing cells, confirming that WAVE2-ARP2/3 inhibition at the lamellipodia represents a central mechanism by which HIV disables T-cell function.²

## Antiretroviral therapy and viral persistence

Despite potent suppression of HIV replication by [[antiretroviral-therapy|ART]], the virus persists in long-lived memory CD4+ T-cell subsets harboring transcriptionally silent integrated proviruses. Treatment interruption reliably triggers viral rebound in the vast majority of individuals, typically within two to four weeks.¹ The latent reservoir is maintained in minimally differentiated CD4+ T-cell subsets that can give rise to the full spectrum of memory and effector populations, ensuring proviral persistence across diverse cellular compartments.⁴ Multiomic analysis of ART-interruption cohorts reveals that delayed rebound is not associated with the size of the intact HIV reservoir but instead correlates with cohort-specific immune effectors and cell-intrinsic HIV silencing mechanisms.¹

## Non-suppressible viremia and clonal persistence

A subset of individuals on [[antiretroviral-therapy|ART]] experience non-suppressible viremia (NSV)—persistent low-level viremia that is not attributable to poor adherence, suboptimal drug levels, or drug resistance and that does not resolve with regimen modification. NSV can originate from clonally expanded CD4+ T cells harboring genetically defective proviruses, particularly those with deletions in the major splice donor (MSD) site that render the virus replication-incompetent yet still capable of producing detectable viremia for years.⁴ These defective proviruses persist by integrating into minimally differentiated T-cell subsets and exhibiting an HIV protein expression profile that evades both cellular and humoral immune recognition, allowing infected clones to expand and produce sustained low-level viremia without immune clearance.⁴

## HIV silencing factors and remission strategies

RNA sequencing of CD4+ T cells from individuals with delayed viral rebound after ART interruption identified [[ddit4|DDIT4]]—an mTOR pathway inhibitor—and the zinc finger protein [[znf254|ZNF254]] as transcriptional correlates of prolonged viral suppression.¹·⁵ In vitro and in vivo validation demonstrated that both factors directly suppress HIV expression, establishing them as bona fide HIV silencing factors. Critically, the widely available diabetes drug metformin induces [[ddit4|DDIT4]] expression and suppresses HIV transcription in primary cells and in CD4+ T cells isolated from ART-suppressed people with HIV, suggesting a repurposing strategy to achieve deeper viral silencing and potentially ART-free remission.¹·⁵ These findings support dual therapeutic approaches: immune-based interventions to eliminate residual infected cells and transcriptional silencing strategies to enforce durable proviral latency.

## Immune evasion and antibody escape

The HIV envelope glycoprotein (Env) is shielded by a dense array of host-derived N-linked glycans that occlude conserved epitopes from antibody recognition. Broadly neutralizing antibodies targeting the CD4-binding site, exemplified by [[vrc01|VRC01]], represent a promising cure strategy because they recognize conserved regions on Env present on both virions and infected cell surfaces.³ However, viral escape from VRC01-class antibodies occurs readily through mutations at glycosylation sites. The N276D mutation—which eliminates a glycan attachment site—confers resistance to VRC01 by day 42 of in vitro selection, yet paradoxically this same mutation can increase sensitivity to other CD4-binding site antibodies depending on the genetic context of the Env, the specific antibody, and the glycosylation machinery of producer cells.³ This dual role of individual glycans underscores the complexity of antibody-based cure strategies and the necessity of cocktail approaches using multiple broadly neutralizing antibodies.

## HIV-associated autoimmunity

Beyond direct viral cytopathicity, HIV infection drives autoimmunity that impairs immune reconstitution even during suppressive [[antiretroviral-therapy|ART]]. Anti-CD4 IgG autoantibodies remain elevated in ART-treated individuals and inversely correlate with CD4+ T-cell recovery.⁶ These autoantibodies are driven by systemic translocation of *Staphylococcus aureus* and its cell wall component [[peptidoglycan|peptidoglycan (PGN)]], which promote molecular mimicry and autoreactive B-cell activation.⁶ In EcoHIV mouse models, intraperitoneal injection of *S. aureus* PGN induces anti-CD4 IgG production, reduces the frequency of CD4+ T cells in gut-associated lymphoid tissue, and promotes surface IgG binding and apoptosis of CD4+ T cells—recapitulating the poor immune reconstitution phenotype observed in a subset of ART-treated people with HIV.⁶ Plasma markers of *S. aureus* translocation positively correlate with anti-CD4 IgG levels, establishing a mechanistic link between microbial translocation and autoimmunity-mediated immune failure.⁶

## Novel antiretroviral agents

Recent advances in medicinal chemistry have expanded the therapeutic arsenal against HIV-1. A unified photoredox coupling platform enables high-throughput synthesis of diverse [[nucleoside-analogs|nucleoside analog]] libraries, producing both carbon- and nitrogen-linked nucleosides and unifying synthesis of 4'-thio, 4'-imino, and ProTide classes from a single scalable intermediate.⁷ Screening of these libraries identified multiple hit compounds with anti-HIV-1 activity, demonstrating the value of flexible synthetic platforms for antiviral drug discovery.⁷ Among non-nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors, [[ainuovirine|ainuovirine]] exhibits favorable pharmacokinetic properties with a half-life of 43.5 hours and predominantly fecal excretion (>60% of administered dose).⁸ Metabolism proceeds primarily via CYP2C19-mediated mono-oxygenation to form metabolite M341, followed by glucuronidation, supporting its development as an anchor antiretroviral agent for HIV-1 treatment.⁸
