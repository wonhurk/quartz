**CD20** is a B-cell surface antigen and primary immunotherapy target in hematologic malignancies, particularly in relapsed or refractory B-cell non-Hodgkin's lymphoma and Richter transformation.¹² The antigen is engaged through two principal therapeutic modalities: bispecific [[cd3|CD3]]-engaging antibodies that simultaneously bind tumor CD20 and T-cell CD3 to redirect T cells toward malignant B cells, and dual-targeted [[cd19]]/CD20 chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cell therapies designed to overcome single-antigen escape.¹ The rationale for dual targeting stems from the clinical observation that single-antigen CD19-directed CAR-T therapy frequently fails due to antigen loss and limited CAR-T persistence, necessitating combinatorial targeting strategies.

---

## Bispecific CD20/CD3 engagement

Bispecific antibodies targeting CD20 and [[cd3|CD3]] represent a distinct mechanistic approach to B-cell lymphoma, avoiding the manufacturing complexity and off-target toxicity of CAR-T cell therapies. [[mosunetuzumab]], a bispecific CD20/CD3 T-cell-engaging antibody, was evaluated as monotherapy in 20 patients with relapsed or refractory Richter transformation—an aggressive lymphoma arising from underlying chronic lymphocytic leukemia.² The antibody directly bridges tumor cells and T lymphocytes, enabling rapid activation and redirected lysis. Mosunetuzumab achieved an overall response rate of 40%, with a complete response rate of 20%, demonstrating meaningful clinical activity in a chemorefractory patient population.² Critically, responding patients experienced durable remissions; two patients maintained complete responses for more than 20 months without additional therapy, and two additional patients successfully proceeded to allogeneic stem cell transplantation while in complete remission with no subsequent relapse, establishing the potential for long-term disease control.²

---

## Dual CD19/CD20 CAR-T therapy

Dual targeting of CD19 and CD20 addresses a fundamental resistance mechanism in CD19-directed CAR-T therapy: antigen loss during treatment, which permits escape and tumor recurrence.¹ An expanded phase I/II study evaluated bispecific CD19/CD20 CAR-T cells in relapsed or refractory B-cell non-Hodgkin's lymphoma, incorporating spatially resolved single-cell transcriptomics to identify tumor-intrinsic and microenvironmental correlates of response.¹ The transcriptomic approach enabled dissection of whether clinical failure originated from CAR-T dysfunction, tumor microenvironment suppression, or antigen downregulation. By engaging both antigens simultaneously, dual-targeted CAR-T cells theoretically raise the genetic barrier for escape, as tumors would require coordinate loss or downregulation of two independent antigens to evade cytotoxicity—a substantially higher mutational hurdle than single-antigen loss.

## Safety and tolerability

Both CD20-directed therapeutic modalities demonstrate manageable safety profiles in heavily pretreated patient populations. Cytokine release syndrome (CRS), the principal toxicity of bispecific CD20/CD3 engagement, occurred in 65% of mosunetuzumab-treated patients but was predominantly mild to moderate, with 20% experiencing grade 1 CRS and 40% experiencing grade 2 CRS, almost exclusively during the first treatment cycle.² Other observed adverse events included infections, neutropenia, thrombocytopenia, tumor flare reactions, and low-grade neurotoxicity; notably, no adverse events led to treatment discontinuation.² The favorable tolerability profile, coupled with rapid and durable responses, positions CD20-directed bispecific antibodies as viable monotherapy options in settings where chemotherapy has failed.

---

## Therapeutic challenges

Despite the promise of dual-targeting strategies, significant obstacles remain in CD20-directed therapies. Antigen loss during treatment—wherein tumor cells downregulate or lose CD20 surface expression under therapeutic pressure—remains a fundamental mechanism of escape from both bispecific antibodies and CAR-T therapies.¹ Additionally, diminished CAR-T cell persistence in vivo limits the duration of antitumor activity even when initial expansion and tumor killing are robust.¹ These challenges underscore the incomplete understanding of what determines durable CAR-T proliferation and long-term antigen-independent immune surveillance, and motivate ongoing investigation into combination approaches with chemotherapy, other novel agents, and checkpoint modulation to enhance both immediate cytotoxicity and sustained immunological control.
