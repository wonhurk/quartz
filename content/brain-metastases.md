**Brain metastases** represent a critical neoplastic complication in advanced [[ros1|ROS1-rearranged]] [[nsclc|non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC)]], occurring in approximately 36% of patients with ROS1 fusions and imposing significant therapeutic challenges due to the [[blood-brain-barrier|blood-brain barrier's]] restriction of systemic drug penetration to the central nervous system. The management of intracranial disease requires brain-penetrant targeted inhibitors such as [[jyp0322|JYP0322]], a highly selective [[ros1|ROS1]] inhibitor engineered to achieve effective cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) penetration while overcoming resistance mutations, particularly [[ros1|ROS1G2032R]], alongside minimally invasive neurosurgical techniques for lesion resection when indicated.¹ Contemporary multidisciplinary management integrates pharmacotherapy for systemic disease control with targeted neurosurgical intervention to optimize survival and neurological outcomes.

---

## Mechanism and pathophysiology

Brain metastases in ROS1-positive NSCLC arise through hematogenous dissemination of [[ros1|ROS1-rearranged]] tumor cells, which establish micrometastatic foci within the cerebral parenchyma, ventricular system, or meningeal spaces. The [[blood-brain-barrier|blood-brain barrier (BBB)]], a highly selective endothelial structure regulated by tight junction proteins and efflux transporters, presents a formidable impediment to conventional small-molecule therapies. To achieve intracranial disease control, drugs must demonstrate robust cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) penetration, typically quantified by CSF-to-plasma AUC (area under the curve) ratios exceeding 0.89.¹ Conventional [[ros1|ROS1]] inhibitors, while effective against systemic disease, often fail to achieve adequate CNS concentrations, allowing brain metastases to persist and progress as sites of sanctuary disease.

---

## ROS1-driven brain metastases in NSCLC

Brain metastases represent one of the defining oncologic complications in [[ros1|ROS1-rearranged]] NSCLC, with studies documenting involvement in approximately 36% of the patient population.¹ Unlike [[egfr|EGFR]]-mutant NSCLC, which exhibits distinct patterns of brain tropism, [[ros1|ROS1]]-fusion tumors demonstrate a propensity for intracranial dissemination. The molecular mechanisms underlying this predilection remain incompletely understood but likely reflect both the intrinsic invasiveness of [[ros1|ROS1]] fusion proteins and the capacity of these transformed cells to survive within the CNS microenvironment. Early [[ros1|ROS1]] inhibitors, such as crizotinib, achieve limited intracranial penetration, resulting in lower response rates within the brain despite efficacy against systemic disease. The emergence of brain-penetrant next-generation agents has transformed the therapeutic landscape, offering intracranial objective response rates of 55.6% in treatment-naive and heavily pretreated populations.¹

---

## Brain-penetrant ROS1 inhibitors

[[jyp0322|JYP0322]], a highly selective and brain-penetrant [[ros1|ROS1]] inhibitor, has emerged as a paradigm-shifting therapy for patients with [[ros1|ROS1]]-positive NSCLC and brain metastases. Preclinical studies established JYP0322's superior selectivity profile, demonstrating >130-fold selectivity over the off-target [[trka|TRKA]] kinase, a critical distinction that minimizes TRK-related neurotoxicity seen with earlier brain-penetrant inhibitors.¹ The drug achieves remarkable CNS penetration with a CSF-to-plasma AUC ratio of 0.893, meeting the threshold necessary for effective intracranial disease suppression.¹ In the phase 1 trial (NCT06128148) enrolling 89 [[ros1|ROS1]]-fusion NSCLC patients (36% with brain metastases), JYP0322 demonstrated an objective response rate of 95.7% in treatment-naive populations and 77.8% in patients harboring the [[ros1|ROS1G2032R]] resistance mutation.¹ Among the subset of 27 patients with brain metastases, intracranial objective response rate reached 55.6%, demonstrating durable disease control in the CNS.¹ The safety profile was notably favorable, with low-grade neurologic adverse events (dizziness 6.7%, headache 3.4%), substantially lower than historical experiences with pan-kinase inhibitors.¹

## Resistance mutations and escape mechanisms

Sequential [[ros1|ROS1]] inhibitor exposure selects for resistance mutations that impair drug binding while maintaining [[ros1|ROS1]] kinase function, with [[ros1|ROS1G2032R]] emerging as the most prevalent gatekeeper mutation conferring broad cross-resistance to first and second-generation [[ros1|ROS1]] inhibitors.¹ Critically, conventional [[ros1|ROS1]] inhibitors fail to suppress [[ros1|ROS1G2032R]]-mutant NSCLC while simultaneously achieving inadequate CNS penetration, creating a particularly challenging clinical scenario in which patients develop resistant brain metastases that remain beyond the reach of systemic therapy. Brain-penetrant inhibitors such as [[jyp0322|JYP0322]] overcome both obstacles simultaneously: the drug maintains potent activity against [[ros1|ROS1G2032R]] tumor models preclinically and achieved a 77.8% overall response rate in [[ros1|ROS1G2032R]]-mutant patients in the phase 1 trial, enabling treatment of heavily pretreated populations with primary [[ros1|ROS1]] inhibitor resistance.¹ This combination of CNS penetrance and resistance mutation coverage represents a substantial advance in managing intracranial disease burden in advanced ROS1-positive NSCLC.

## Surgical management

Minimally invasive neurosurgical approaches utilizing integrated camera-port retractor systems provide safe and effective alternatives for managing deep-seated intracranial lesions when surgical intervention is clinically indicated. The [[aurora-surgiscope|Aurora Surgiscope]] represents a single-use, camera-port retractor combining integrated lighting and visualization capabilities, eliminating the need for separate optical and illumination systems required by conventional port-based approaches.² A retrospective series of 25 patients treated with this technology documented brain metastases in 4 patients (16.0% of the cohort), with mean lesion depth of 3.5 cm and mean lesion diameter of 3.5 cm, treated at median surgical time of 2.4 hours.² Gross total resection was achieved in 70.6% of operations where complete resection was the surgical objective.² The integration of real-time imaging, reduced operative time, and improved visualization contributes to lower morbidity compared to historical open craniotomy approaches, particularly for lesions located deep within the cerebral hemispheres or ventricular system.

---

## Multidisciplinary management strategy

Optimal outcomes in brain-metastatic [[ros1|ROS1]]-positive NSCLC require a coordinated multidisciplinary approach integrating targeted pharmacotherapy with neurosurgical management tailored to individual patient anatomy and disease burden. For patients with limited intracranial disease, brain-penetrant [[ros1|ROS1]] inhibitors such as [[jyp0322|JYP0322]] offer systemically-administered disease control while achieving therapeutic CSF concentrations.¹ Conversely, patients with bulky or symptomatic lesions refractory to pharmacotherapy may benefit from neurosurgical resection using minimally invasive techniques, preserving neurological function while debulking disease.² Sequential or concurrent strategies—combining targeted therapy for systemic suppression with surgery for intracranial disease control—are increasingly employed to optimize survival and maintain quality of life. Emerging data suggest that early integration of neurosurgical expertise in treatment planning, coupled with modern brain-penetrant inhibitors, enables durable intracranial disease control while minimizing treatment-related neurological morbidity.
