**Neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs)** are web-like antimicrobial structures released by activated neutrophils that, while serving important host defense functions, become pathogenic drivers across multiple disease contexts through metabolic dysregulation. Recent multiomics studies have revealed that NETs are not merely markers of inflammation but active orchestrators of disease progression, driven primarily by metabolic abnormalities — particularly cholesterol biosynthesis, hyperglycemia, and [[ferroptosis]] — that converge on neutrophil activation and extracellular trap formation. Unlike their physiological antimicrobial role, pathogenic NETs cause microvascular obstruction, thrombotic complications, and exacerbation of ischemic or hypoxic tissue injury.¹²³

---

## Mechanism

NET formation (NETosis) is initiated when neutrophils encounter activating stimuli that trigger chromatin decondensation, nuclear and granular membrane dissolution, and release of cytoplasmic and nuclear contents into the extracellular space, forming a fibrous mesh of DNA, histones, and granular proteins.¹ Under physiological conditions, this process serves antimicrobial defense; however, metabolic dysregulation transforms it into a pathogenic pathway. In diabetes-associated pancreatic cancer, [[srebp2]]-dependent cholesterol biosynthesis drives aberrant NET formation through upregulation of the chemokine [[cxcl1]], which signals through CXCR1 and CXCR2 receptors to promote neutrophil recruitment and NETosis.¹ Similarly, acute hyperglycemia activates the receptor for advanced glycation end products ([[rage]]) and diaphanous-1 ([[diaph1]]), a formin family protein that stimulates actin polymerization and NET extrusion.² In hypoxic contexts such as high-altitude pulmonary edema, [[ferroptosis]] emerges as a critical upstream regulator: downregulation of [[gpx4]], the master ferroptosis brake, in pulmonary endothelial cells triggers ferroptotic cell death that subsequently drives NETosis through reactive oxygen species accumulation, Nrf2 nuclear translocation, and HMGB1-TLR4/MyD88 signaling.³

## Pancreatic cancer and diabetes

The intersection of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma ([[pancreatic-ductal-adenocarcinoma|PDAC]]) and diabetes mellitus represents a particularly aggressive malignancy-metabolism axis. Spatial metabolic and transcriptional profiling revealed that diabetes-induced metabolic remodeling of the tumor microenvironment markedly exacerbates PDAC progression through SREBP2-dependent cholesterol biosynthesis.¹ Elevated cholesterol biosynthesis amplifies [[cxcl1]] secretion from cancer-associated fibroblasts and tumor cells, establishing a potent chemotactic gradient that recruits neutrophils into the tumor microenvironment. Once recruited, neutrophils undergo enhanced NETosis via CXCR1/CXCR2 signaling, and the resulting NETs promote angiogenesis and suppress antitumor immunity while simultaneously accelerating tumor cell proliferation and invasion.¹ Clinical observations validate this pathogenic cascade: patients with PDAC and comorbid diabetes exhibit higher tumor stage at diagnosis, poorer histological differentiation, and significantly worse overall survival compared to non-diabetic PDAC cohorts.¹ Therapeutic interventions targeting this axis — including SREBP2 inhibition, CXCL1 blockade, and direct NET perturbation through DNase I administration — each markedly reduced PDAC growth in diabetic mouse models, demonstrating the non-redundant role of metabolic-immune cross-talk in this disease.¹

## Ischemic stroke and acute hyperglycemia

[[Acute-hyperglycemia|Acute hyperglycemia]] during the acute phase of ischemic stroke significantly worsens neurological outcomes through a distinct but equally pathogenic NET-dependent mechanism. In a [[ischemic-stroke|middle cerebral artery occlusion/reperfusion]] model, acute hyperglycemia — induced transiently via glucose injection — spontaneously normalized within hours; yet 24 hours after reperfusion, significantly elevated NET formation was detected in cerebral blood vessels and ischemic brain tissue.² Mechanistically, acute hyperglycemia-induced hyperglycemia activates RAGE and its downstream effector DIAPH1, which promotes actin polymerization and chromatin condensation reversal in neutrophils, culminating in robust NETosis.² These pathogenic NETs then lodge in cerebral microvessels, forming obstructive clots that worsen microvascular flow-no-reflow phenomena and exacerbate ischemic brain injury, explaining why standard glycemic control during recanalization therapy fails to prevent this complication.² Pharmacological antagonism of RAGE (via the antagonist FPS-ZM1) or direct enzymatic degradation of NETs (DNase I) both significantly attenuated hyperglycemia-exacerbated stroke pathology, suggesting that the RAGE/DIAPH1 axis, rather than chronic hyperglycemia per se, drives acute NETosis and its deleterious consequences.²

## Ferroptosis-driven NETs in high-altitude pulmonary edema

High-altitude pulmonary edema (HAPE) represents a life-threatening complication of hypoxic exposure in which NETs serve as a critical effector of lung injury. Single-cell transcriptomics of hypoxic lung tissue revealed that pulmonary endothelial cells experience significant downregulation of [[gpx4]], the central ferroptosis regulator, alongside upregulation of pro-ferroptotic factors and heightened reactive oxygen species accumulation.³ This GPX4 deficiency in endothelial cells unleashes ferroptotic cell death, which in turn orchestrates robust NET formation from recruited neutrophils through multiple signaling axes: ferroptosis-driven ROS accumulation activates Nrf2 nuclear translocation and NF-κB/ERK1/2 phosphorylation, and ferroptotic endothelial cells release HMGB1 damage-associated molecular patterns that activate TLR4/MyD88 signaling on nearby neutrophils to promote NETosis.³ Functionally, GPX4-deficient endothelial cells exhibited dramatically accelerated ferroptosis and subsequent NET formation in vitro, whereas GPX4 overexpression substantially mitigated these effects.³ In vivo, therapeutic GPX4 augmentation reduced pulmonary edema severity, improved lung function, suppressed both ferroptotic and NETotic markers, and ultimately protected against hypoxia-induced tissue injury, establishing ferroptosis as an upstream orchestrator of pathogenic NET formation in this context.³

---

## Therapeutic approaches

Three distinct therapeutic strategies have emerged to mitigate pathogenic NET formation across these disease contexts. Direct NET degradation via DNase I effectively dissolves extracellular traps and rapidly alleviates NET-mediated microvascular obstruction, demonstrating efficacy in both stroke and pulmonary edema models.²³ Pathway-specific antagonism, exemplified by RAGE antagonist FPS-ZM1 in the hyperglycemia-stroke axis, selectively blocks the RAGE/DIAPH1 signaling cascade to prevent hyperglycemia-triggered NETosis without broadly suppressing neutrophil function.² Metabolic intervention targeting upstream drivers — such as [[srebp2]] inhibition to suppress cholesterol-driven [[cxcl1]] production in the tumor microenvironment, or GPX4 enhancement to prevent ferroptosis-orchestrated NETosis in hypoxic contexts — addresses the root metabolic dysregulation rather than NET formation itself.¹³ These layered therapeutic approaches suggest that optimal management of NET-driven pathology may require combination strategies addressing both immediate NET effects and underlying metabolic drivers.
