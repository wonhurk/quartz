**Biofilms** are polymeric matrices secreted by microorganisms that serve as a critical virulence factor and the primary obstacle to conventional antimicrobial therapy in chronic infections. These structured communities enable bacterial and fungal pathogens including [[pseudomonas-aeruginosa|Pseudomonas aeruginosa]], [[staphylococcus-aureus|Staphylococcus aureus]], [[cryptococcus-neoformans|Cryptococcus neoformans]], and [[candida-glabrata|Candida glabrata]] to evade antibiotics and immune responses by adopting protected, sessile lifestyles.¹ Biofilm-associated infections persist in [[cystic-fibrosis|cystic fibrosis]] lung colonization, [[osteomyelitis]], [[periodontitis]], diabetic wounds, and implant-associated submillimeter infection pockets, where they sustain persistent inflammation despite paradoxical downregulation of traditional virulence factors.²³

---

## Mechanism and pathogenesis

Biofilm formation transforms planktonic microorganisms into surface-attached communities encased in extracellular polymeric substances that physically exclude antibiotics and immune effectors. During chronic [[cystic-fibrosis|CF]] lung colonization, [[pseudomonas-aeruginosa|P. aeruginosa]] adopts a biofilm-associated mucoid lifestyle characterized by reduced production of classical virulence factors yet enhanced pro-inflammatory properties through novel mediators such as [[dksa|DksA]], a transcription factor that sustains excessive inflammation in three-dimensional lung epithelial models despite low expression of known immune activators.³ In [[osteomyelitis]], biofilm-forming bacteria invade bone tissue and resist eradication due to poor antibiotic penetration, uneven drug distribution across the biofilm architecture, and protective effects of the matrix itself.² Submillimeter infection pockets at soft tissue-implant and hard tissue interfaces present multicurvature spaces where biofilms persist on dual surfaces, inaccessible to conventional surgical debridement or systemic antimicrobials.⁴

---

## Fungal biofilm infections

Invasive fungal biofilms formed by [[cryptococcus-neoformans|Cryptococcus neoformans]] and [[candida-glabrata|Candida glabrata]] pose severe threats due to high mortality and limited treatment options. Iron homeostasis disruption has emerged as a potent anti-biofilm mechanism: the pyrazolone derivative [[compound-d2|compound D2]] exhibits subnanomolar antifungal activity (MIC range 0.00012–0.0078 μg/mL), disrupts fungal iron metabolism, impairs mitochondrial structure and function, triggers oxidative stress damage, and effectively inhibits biofilm formation alongside other virulence factors including capsule, urease, and melanin production.¹ In mouse models of cryptococcal meningitis and candidiasis, [[compound-d2|compound D2]] significantly reduced fungal burden and alleviated tissue damage through dual fungicidal and antivirulence effects, validating iron-targeting strategies for treating recalcitrant fungal biofilm infections.¹

---

## Bacterial biofilm therapy challenges

Traditional approaches including systemic antibiotics and surgically implanted beads made of calcium sulfate or polymethyl methacrylate fail to control biofilm infections due to cytotoxicity, uneven drug distribution, and thermal denaturation of antibiotics during polymerization.² [[mrsa|Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus]] biofilms in chronic wound infections remain refractory to conventional therapies, necessitating novel localized treatment modalities.⁶ In [[periodontitis]] exacerbated by diabetes mellitus, hyperglycemic states drive reactive oxygen species overproduction that inhibits periodontal osteogenic differentiation while accelerating dental plaque biofilm reproduction and local inflammation, creating compounding therapeutic challenges.⁵

---

## Novel therapeutic strategies

Emerging multifaceted approaches target biofilm infections through synergistic mechanisms. [[microswarm|Microswarms]] self-assembled from magnetic hydrogel particles exploit a bridging effect to establish continuous contact across concave, plane, and convex surfaces within infection pockets, delivering enzymatic degradation combined with mechanical disruption that enhances biofilm eradication efficiency eightfold compared to mechanical disruption alone, achieving greater than 99% elimination in ex vivo and in vivo models when paired with [[multifunctional-hydrogel-filler|multifunctional hydrogel fillers]] that remove debris and inhibit microbial recolonization.⁴

[[h-se-nps|Selenium-albumin corona nanoparticles]] assembled through albumin/nano-selenium strategies demonstrate anti-inflammatory effects via M1-to-M2 macrophage polarization, anti-bacterial properties superior to commercial selenium nanoparticles, and ROS-scavenging capacity that activates the TrxR1/ROS/β-catenin antioxidation cascade to promote periodontal osteogenesis in diabetic environments while suppressing subgingival biofilm colonies.⁵ [[qds-phage-nanoarchitectonics|Quantum dot-phage nanoarchitectonics]] integrate [[mrsa|MRSA]]-specific bacteriophages with photothermally efficient PbS quantum dots, creating intelligent therapeutic platforms operating on a "target-first, activate-later" principle wherein near-infrared laser irradiation produces significant synergistic bactericidal activity against both planktonic [[mrsa|MRSA]] and mature biofilms, accelerating wound closure and tissue repair in murine models without systemic toxicity.⁶

[[pmma-ba|Antibiotic-loaded latex beads]] produced through solvent-free droplet aggregation of poly(methyl methacrylate-co-butyl acrylate) latex nanoparticles offer ready-to-use formulations that circumvent thermal denaturation concerns, delivering prolonged antibacterial activity and strong inhibition of [[staphylococcus-aureus|S. aureus]] biofilms in [[osteomyelitis]] while maintaining osteoblast viability and supporting tissue integration in vivo.²
