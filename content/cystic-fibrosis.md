**Cystic fibrosis (CF)** is a genetic disorder characterized by progressive airway inflammation, chronic polymicrobial infection, and severe lung disease driven by defective mucociliary clearance and dysregulated immune responses. The hallmark pathogen is [[pseudomonas-aeruginosa]], which establishes chronic, biofilm-associated lung infection and sustains excessive airway inflammation that progressively destroys lung architecture.¹ The CF airway environment is shaped not only by the underlying genetic defect but also by therapeutic interventions—notably inhaled [[hypertonic-saline]], used to improve mucociliary clearance—which paradoxically drives microbiota shifts toward antibiotic-tolerant, salt-resistant opportunistic pathogens including [[haemophilus-influenzae]] and fungi.² A central paradox of CF pathogenesis is that chronically colonizing [[pseudomonas-aeruginosa]] populations downregulate classical virulence factors and adopt a mucoid, biofilm-associated lifestyle; despite this apparent adaptation, excessive airway inflammation persists through the action of novel bacterial immunomodulators such as [[dksa]], a transcription factor that sustains pro-inflammatory [[il-8]] secretion independent of canonical virulence mechanisms.¹

---

## Mechanism and pathogenesis

CF airway disease results from loss of functional [[cftr|CFTR]] protein, leading to impaired ion transport, thickened secretions, and severely compromised mucociliary clearance. This environment provides ideal conditions for establishment of chronic, polymicrobial biofilm infections. [[pseudomonas-aeruginosa]] emerges as the dominant pathogen through adaptive phenotypic changes: downregulation of classical virulence factors, acquisition of mucoid forms, and transition to biofilm-associated growth modes.¹ Paradoxically, this bacterial adaptation does not suppress immune activation; CF lungs harbor persistently elevated levels of [[il-8]] and neutrophilic inflammation despite the apparent attenuation of virulence factor production.

This paradox reflects the discovery of novel bacterial mediators that sustain inflammation independently of canonical virulence regulation. [[dksa]], a [[pseudomonas-aeruginosa]] transcription factor, has been identified through comparative proteomics as a sustained pro-inflammatory driver in the CF lung microenvironment.¹ DksA-mediated signaling directly induces robust [[il-8]] secretion from lung epithelial cells, even in clinical CF isolates that produce minimal amounts of established virulence factors such as elastase or exotoxins. CF airway isolates display remarkable heterogeneity in pro-inflammatory capacity; individual isolates show variable induction of [[il-8]] despite similar biofilm phenotypes, indicating that bacterial genotype and fine-tuned inflammatory signaling, rather than virulence factor burden alone, determine the inflammatory landscape of the CF lung.¹

## Therapeutic interventions and unintended microbiota consequences

Inhaled [[hypertonic-saline]] is a cornerstone therapy for CF, used to enhance mucociliary clearance by osmotic fluid recruitment into the airway lumen.² However, longitudinal [[metagenomic-sequencing]] analysis of 704 nasal swabs from infants with and without CF reveals that hypertonic saline therapy inadvertently reshapes the respiratory microbiota in ways that promote pathogenic expansion.² Compared with [[isotonic-saline]], hypertonic saline drives compositional shifts in the nasal microbiome toward salt-resistant bacterial species, particularly [[haemophilus-influenzae]] and opportunistic fungi carrying salt-associated transporter genes.² The altered osmotic environment selectively enriches organisms equipped with high-affinity ion uptake systems, enabling their dominance in the salt-perturbed CF airway.

In vitro studies confirm that hypertonic saline accelerates [[haemophilus-influenzae]] growth and induces upregulation of bacterial efflux pumps associated with antibiotic tolerance.² This represents a critical trade-off in CF management: while hypertonic saline improves mucus clearance, it simultaneously selects for and amplifies populations of pathogens with reduced susceptibility to antimicrobial therapy, complicating disease control and potentially accelerating antibiotic resistance evolution within the lung.

## Microbiota complexity and adaptive resistance

The CF respiratory microbiota is not a static collection of pathogenic species. Construction of a functional microbiome gene atlas through comprehensive [[metagenomic-sequencing]] of 704 longitudinal infant nasal samples establishes a detailed reference framework for understanding how early-life therapeutic interventions functionally perturb airway microbiota.² This atlas reveals that specific microbial genes encoding salt-responsive transporters are enriched in CF-associated opportunists, providing mechanistic insight into how osmotic therapy inadvertently selects for salt-tolerant strains. The expansion of [[haemophilus-influenzae]] and fungi in response to hypertonic saline reflects not passive osmotic stress but active expression of specific salt-associated genetic programs that confer growth advantages in the altered airway environment.²

Beyond osmotic selection, development of antibiotic tolerance represents a parallel adaptive mechanism. [[haemophilus-influenzae]] isolates recovered from CF airways following hypertonic saline exposure exhibit elevated expression of efflux pump genes, enabling survival of antibiotic stress without acquiring canonical resistance mutations.² This tolerance phenotype can precede and facilitate eventual emergence of stably resistant strains, creating a critical window during which opportunistic pathogens establish themselves in the CF lung and become increasingly difficult to eradicate through antimicrobial therapy.
