**Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH)** is a progressive liver disease representing the inflammatory and fibrotic stage of metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), affecting over 30% of the global population with advanced [[liver-fibrosis|fibrosis]] as the principal driver of [[liver-cirrhosis|cirrhosis]], [[liver-cancer|hepatocellular carcinoma]], and liver-related mortality.¹⁻² The disease is mechanistically driven by dysregulation of de novo [[lipogenesis]] through the [[ifrd1|IFRD1]]-GLUD1-[[alpha-ketoglutarate|α-ketoglutarate]] metabolic-epigenetic axis, [[gut-microbiota|gut microbiota]] dysbiosis mediated by pathobionts including *Turicibacter sanguinis* and *Clostridium perfringens*, reprogramming of hepatic macrophage populations with accumulation of pro-inflammatory GPNMB+ subsets, and activation of [[cd8-t-cells|CD8+ T cells]] through ammonia-mediated signaling.³⁻⁶

---

## Mechanism

MASH pathogenesis is orchestrated by interconnected metabolic, microbial, and immune pathways. At the metabolic core, the [[ifrd1|IFRD1]]-GLUD1-[[alpha-ketoglutarate|α-ketoglutarate (α-KG)]] axis functions as a critical regulatory hub suppressing hepatic de novo lipogenesis.¹ IFRD1 promotes GLUD1 mitochondrial localization through direct interaction, enhancing α-KG production, which in turn reduces H3K36me3 histone methylation at lipogenic gene loci and thereby inhibits lipid synthesis. Loss of hepatic IFRD1 exacerbates MASH progression in multiple mouse models, while hepatocyte-specific IFRD1 overexpression or α-KG supplementation ameliorates disease.¹ The progression from [[steatosis]] to fibrosis is potentiated by hepatocyte-derived exosomes carrying pathogenic cargo including palmitoylated NAT10, which activates hepatic stellate cells to drive fibrogenesis.³ Selective inhibition of the non-canonical MTORC1 signaling pathway — specifically MTORC1-mediated phosphorylation of TFEB without affecting canonical MTORC1 substrates — represents a mechanistic node for suppressing hepatic steatosis, inflammation, and fibrosis while avoiding unwanted systemic metabolic effects.⁷

---

## Gut microbiota and dietary synergy

Dietary components shape the gut microbiota in ways that directly promote MASH fibrogenesis. Saturated fat and cholesterol act synergistically to induce fibrosing MASH through dysbiosis-dependent mechanisms; neither component alone is sufficient in specific pathogen-free mice, and germ-free mice are completely protected.⁴ This dietary combination promotes a distinct microbial composition enriched in *Parasutterella* spp., which correlates with hepatic fibrosis severity, and drives altered [[bile-acids|bile acid]] metabolism including increased deoxycholic acid that activates hepatic stellate cells in vitro.⁴ Two candidate pathobionts emerge as mechanistic drivers: *Turicibacter sanguinis* and *Clostridium perfringens*. *Turicibacter sanguinis* is consistently enriched across MASH models and clinical datasets, exacerbates steatosis, inflammation, and fibrosis when administered to mice, and operates by altering bile acid composition to inhibit hepatic [[fxr|FXR]] signaling, thereby increasing bile acid synthesis and lipogenesis via upregulation of CYP7A1 and SREBP1c.⁶ *Clostridium perfringens* drives disease through ammonia production, which disrupts the [[intestinal-barrier-dysfunction|intestinal barrier]] and triggers FosB-mediated upregulation of chemokine CCL5 in CD8+ T cells, driving hepatic T cell cytotoxicity.⁵

---

## Macrophage reprogramming

MASH progression is marked by profound alterations in the hepatic macrophage compartment. Single-nucleus transcriptomics, spatial multi-omics, and proteomics reveal progressive depletion of resident Kupffer cells accompanied by emergence of diverse, phenotypically distinct macrophage subsets.⁹ Disease advancement toward MASH is characterized by accumulation of antigen-presenting, phagocytic GPNMB+ macrophages exhibiting pro-inflammatory and metabolically adaptive phenotypes, supported spatially by IL32-producing hepatocytes.⁹ These macrophage markers enable patient stratification by disease activity and stage, offering biomarker potential for non-invasive disease monitoring.⁹

## Therapeutic strategies

Emerging therapeutic interventions successfully target multiple pathogenic pathways in MASH. [[fxr|Farnesoid X receptor (FXR)]] represents a validated target, with the non-carboxylic steroidal FXR agonist [[compound-27|compound 27]] demonstrating dual-path efficacy through enhanced FXR/TGR5 activity coupled with suppression of the off-target receptor MRGPRX4, attenuating collagen deposition in liver fibrosis models and improving steatosis and inflammation in MASH models.² The natural compound [[byakangelicin|byakangelicin]] selectively inhibits MTORC1-mediated phosphorylation of TFEB by binding directly to folliculin (FLCN), alleviating hepatic steatosis, inflammation, insulin resistance, and fibrosis without affecting canonical MTORC1 substrates.⁷ Microbiota-modulating interventions show promise, with the [[glycine]]-based tripeptide [[dt-109|DT-109]] effectively lowering *C. perfringens* abundance, reducing intestinal ammonia, restoring intestinal barrier integrity, and alleviating CD8+ T cell dysregulation in both non-human primate and mouse MASH models.⁵ [[alpha-ketoglutarate|α-Ketoglutarate]] supplementation reverses MASH exacerbation in IFRD1-deficient mice by restoring the metabolic-epigenetic regulatory axis.¹

## Risk stratification and diagnostics

Early identification of individuals at high risk for advanced fibrosis is essential for preventing liver-related events. The [[fibrosis-4|Fibrosis-4 (FIB-4) index]] is recommended as a widely accessible, reliable first-line noninvasive tool for fibrosis risk stratification.⁸ Patients with elevated FIB-4 values or clinical features suggesting progressive injury should undergo secondary assessment with elastography and specialist referral when appropriate.⁸ While [[liver-biopsy|liver biopsy]] is not routinely required for MASLD diagnosis, it remains essential for establishing a definitive diagnosis of at-risk MASH and assessing inflammatory activity.⁸ CT-based composite biomarkers offer promise for non-invasive identification of MASH and steatosis grade in people with obesity.¹⁰ GPNMB+ macrophage markers identified through spatial multi-omics enable patient stratification by disease activity and stage across independent clinical cohorts.⁹

---

## Protective factors

Breastfeeding in infancy confers sex-specific, long-term protection against MASH and adverse liver outcomes in adulthood, suggesting early-life nutritional exposures exert durable influence on metabolic liver disease susceptibility.¹¹ This finding highlights the potential for primary prevention strategies targeting critical developmental windows.

---

## Preclinical models

The PWK/PhJ mouse strain has emerged as a robust model for severe MASH, exhibiting high susceptibility to Western diet and closely mimicking the clinical and molecular profile of human disease.¹² Male and female PWK/PhJ mice develop hepatic fibrosis and [[cardiac-contractile-dysfunction|cardiac dysfunction]] after 17 weeks of Western diet challenge, with chronic exposure exacerbating lipid accumulation, inflammation, fibrosis, and disrupting amino acid and mitochondrial metabolism.¹² These alterations increase hepatic synthesis of ceramides and deoxy-ceramides, contributing to elevated sphingolipid levels in plasma and [[heart-disease|heart tissue]], establishing the strain as a model for studying cardio-metabolic cross-talk.¹²
