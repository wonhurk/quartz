**Hemagglutinin (HA)** is the major surface glycoprotein of influenza viruses and serves as the primary target for vaccine-induced immunity and therapeutic monoclonal antibody development. HA mediates viral entry by binding sialic acid receptors on host cells and catalyzing membrane fusion, making it the immunodominant antigen in [[standard-influenza-vaccine|seasonal influenza vaccines]].¹ The protein undergoes continuous antigenic drift through amino acid substitutions—most notably K136E in post-2019 Victoria-lineage [[influenza-b|influenza B viruses]]—that enable escape from previously effective broadly neutralizing antibodies (bnAbs).¹ Despite this variability, the receptor-binding site (RBS) within the HA head domain can be targeted by bnAbs such as [[cav-cf22|CAV-CF22]] and [[cav-ch76|CAV-CH76]] that sterically mimic sialic acid binding to achieve cross-lineage neutralization.¹

---

## Antigenic drift and immune escape

Influenza viruses continuously evolve through antigenic drift, accumulating point mutations in HA that disrupt recognition by antibodies elicited during prior infections or vaccinations. Phylogenetic analysis of [[influenza-b|IBV]] isolates revealed fixation of the K136E substitution in Victoria-lineage HA after 2019, which abolishes binding by most previously characterized head-directed monoclonal antibodies.¹ This single amino acid change in the antigenic site demonstrates how minimal genetic variation can confer population-level immune escape, necessitating periodic updates to seasonal vaccine strain composition. Similar drift dynamics occur in [[influenza-a-virus|influenza A]] subtypes, where the HA head domain accumulates substitutions that erode vaccine effectiveness over successive seasons.²

## Broadly neutralizing antibodies

Despite antigenic variability, certain epitopes in the HA head domain can be targeted for broad neutralization. [[cav-cf22|CAV-CF22]] and [[cav-ch76|CAV-CH76]], isolated from individuals who received quadrivalent seasonal vaccine, exhibit potent neutralizing activity against both Victoria- and Yamagata-lineage influenza B viruses and confer protection in murine challenge models.¹ High-resolution structural studies reveal that these antibodies insert their heavy-chain complementarity-determining region 3 (HCDR3) directly into the receptor-binding site, sterically mimicking the interaction between HA and sialic acid.¹ This binding mode targets highly conserved RBS residues that cannot tolerate mutation without compromising viral fitness, explaining the antibodies' resilience to recent antigenic drift and informing structure-guided vaccine design strategies.¹

## Immunological imprinting

Childhood influenza exposures profoundly shape subsequent antibody responses to antigenically distinct strains through a phenomenon termed immunological imprinting. Individuals born in the 1990s who were first exposed to H3N2 viruses during early life later produced cross-subtype antibodies upon 2021-22 H1N1 vaccination that recognized epitopes on both the contemporary H1 HA and 1990s-era H3 HA—a remarkable feat given the substantial sequence divergence between these subtypes.² The unmutated common ancestors of many cross-reactive monoclonal antibodies bound both the 1990s H3N2 strains and the 2021-22 H1N1 vaccine strain, demonstrating that germline-encoded antibody sequences can recognize conserved structural features across viral subtypes.² Sequential ferret infections recapitulated this phenomenon, confirming that early H3N2 priming established a memory B cell repertoire capable of cross-reacting with later H1N1 exposures.² Recent H1N1 evolution has introduced substitutions that abrogate binding by these historically imprinted antibodies, illustrating the ongoing evolutionary contest between viral escape and host immune memory.²

## Vaccine platforms

Self-amplifying RNA (saRNA) vaccines encoding HA demonstrate superior dose-sparing efficacy compared to conventional mRNA and inactivated vaccine platforms. Trivalent saRNA vaccines delivered at ultra-low doses (0.1 μg) induced robust humoral immunity and conferred complete protection against [[influenza-a-virus|influenza A]] virus challenge in murine models, outperforming quadrivalent inactivated vaccines administered at 20-fold higher antigen doses.³ Long-term antibody monitoring over 20 weeks revealed that saRNA maintained markedly more durable antibody titers than conventional mRNA, with sustained protection against homologous viral challenge.³ However, [[influenza-b|influenza B virus]] HA exhibits suboptimal immunogenicity with standard mRNA platforms at equivalent doses, failing to elicit detectable antibodies or protection at 0.1 μg—a limitation consistent with clinical trial observations of reduced efficacy against IBV.³ The saRNA platform overcomes this constraint, achieving 100% survival against IBV challenge at low doses where conventional mRNA conferred only 14% protection.³ These findings highlight platform-specific immunogenicity differences and support saRNA as a next-generation approach for universal influenza vaccine development. Additionally, HA-encoding mRNA has been employed as a model antigen to evaluate immune-remodeling adjuvants, where co-administration with interferon regulatory factor 8 or NF-κB-inducing kinase mRNA enhanced humoral responses approximately 5-fold and cellular responses approximately 15-fold.⁵

## Mucosal immunity

Intranasal live-attenuated influenza vaccine ([[flumist|FluMist]]) induces local HA-specific memory B cell responses in the upper respiratory tract that are phenotypically and anatomically distinct from those elicited by intramuscular vaccination. Nasopharyngeal sampling of adenoid tissue revealed substantial increases in both IgG+ and IgA+ HA-specific memory B cells after intranasal immunization, with responses directed against H1, H3, and influenza B HA antigens.⁶ These upper airway memory B cells displayed an activated CD27+CD21- phenotype and remained elevated at 6 months post-vaccination, persisting far longer than typically observed for mucosal immune responses.⁶ The local B cell expansion was associated with circulating T follicular helper (cTFH) cells, indicating robust germinal center activity in upper airway lymphoid tissues.⁶ In contrast, intramuscular vaccinees showed minimal upper respiratory tract memory B cell responses despite generating systemic antibodies and circulating HA-specific memory B cells, underscoring the compartmentalization of mucosal and systemic immunity.⁶ These findings demonstrate that intranasal vaccination establishes site-specific immunological memory at the portal of viral entry, a property with implications for next-generation influenza vaccine design aimed at preventing transmission as well as disease.⁶
