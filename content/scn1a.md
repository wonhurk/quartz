**SCN1A** is a protein-coding gene that encodes the α subunit of the voltage-gated sodium channel [[nav1.1|Nav1.1]], a critical regulator of neuronal excitability and inhibitory signaling. Loss-of-function mutations in SCN1A cause two distinct inherited epileptic disorders: [[gefs+|generalized epilepsy with febrile seizures plus (GEFS+)]], characterized by febrile seizures in early childhood progressing to generalized tonic-clonic seizures, and [[dravet-syndrome|Dravet syndrome]], a severe neurodevelopmental disorder marked by drug-resistant epilepsy, temperature-sensitive seizures, progressive cognitive impairment, and high incidence of sudden unexpected nocturnal death in epilepsy (SUDEP).¹ The pathogenic mechanism operates through impaired [[nav1.1|Nav1.1]] function specifically in [[parvalbumin|parvalbumin]]-expressing GABAergic interneurons, reducing inhibitory synaptic transmission and unleashing unopposed excitatory drive. Recent therapeutic breakthroughs using [[prime-editing|prime editing]] and [[adenine-base-editing|adenine base editing (ABE)]] have demonstrated the capacity to correct pathogenic SCN1A variants in preclinical models, achieving substantial DNA and mRNA correction rates and robust restoration of neuronal inhibitory function and seizure control.

---

## Mechanism

SCN1A encodes the primary voltage-gated sodium channel essential for rapid action potential generation in inhibitory neurons. Loss-of-function mutations impair the structure or gating kinetics of [[nav1.1|Nav1.1]], reducing sodium current density in [[gaba|GABAergic]] interneurons. This deficit specifically dampens the frequency and amplitude of inhibitory postsynaptic currents, impairing the neuron's ability to transmit inhibitory signals to downstream excitatory networks.¹ The net result is network-level hyperexcitability: with reduced inhibition, excitatory glutamatergic activity propagates unopposed, triggering recurrent seizures. Notably, the excitatory pyramidal neurons themselves retain largely intact sodium channel function, making the interneuron dysfunction the primary driver of epileptogenesis in SCN1A loss-of-function disorders.

## Generalized epilepsy with febrile seizures plus

Generalized epilepsy with febrile seizures plus is an autosomal-dominant inherited disorder in which heterozygous, loss-of-function mutations in SCN1A predominate among identified genetic causes.² Affected individuals typically present with febrile seizures—seizures provoked by fever—in early infancy or toddlerhood between ages six months and three years. These seizures are often prolonged, and many patients progress to afebrile generalized tonic-clonic seizures by late childhood or adolescence. The clinical spectrum within families varies considerably, reflecting both the particular impact of each SCN1A variant on channel function and broader genetic and environmental modifiers of seizure threshold. Some individuals experience only occasional breakthrough seizures managed with standard antiepileptic drugs, while others present with more severe, difficult-to-control seizure patterns approaching the severity of Dravet syndrome.

---

## Dravet syndrome

Dravet syndrome represents the most severe clinical manifestation of SCN1A loss-of-function, typically arising from de novo heterozygous mutations.³ The disorder emerges within the first year of life with prolonged febrile seizures that rapidly escalate into multiple seizure types—including myoclonic, atonic, and absence seizures alongside persistent generalized tonic-clonic events. The seizures are characteristically temperature-sensitive, worsening dramatically with fever and even high ambient temperature. Progressive cognitive and motor decline accompany the seizure burden, and many patients develop intractable status epilepticus requiring intensive hospitalization. Unlike GEFS+, Dravet syndrome is notably resistant to multiple standard antiepileptic medications; conventional treatments suppress symptoms temporarily but fail to address the underlying [[nav1.1|Nav1.1]] channel dysfunction. Sudden unexpected nocturnal death in epilepsy (SUDEP) claims a significant proportion of Dravet patients, representing a major cause of premature mortality in this disorder and a critical therapeutic target.

## Gene editing therapeutics

[[Prime-editing|Prime editing]] and [[adenine-base-editing|adenine base editing]] have emerged as transformative therapeutic strategies capable of directly correcting pathogenic SCN1A variants and restoring normal [[nav1.1|Nav1.1]] function at the molecular level.

In a [[gefs+|GEFS+]] mouse model, [[prime-editing|prime editing]] of the pathogenic SCN1A-K1270T mutation achieved 34.7% correction of the mutant allele in cortical DNA and robust 81.2% correction at the mRNA level.² Intracranial delivery of the optimized prime editor via [[aav|adeno-associated virus]] (AAV) under neuron-specific promoter control enabled efficient in vivo editing. Treated animals showed dramatic clinical improvements: survival increased from 80% to 100%, inhibitory postsynaptic current frequency more than tripled (from 0.32 to 1.32 hertz), and the frequency of induced febrile seizures dropped from 78.6% to 13.3%. These findings establish prime editing as a viable path toward durable correction of GEFS+ in preclinical models.

[[Adenine-base-editing|Adenine base editing]] achieved even higher editing efficiency when correcting SCN1A-R613X, a recurrent loss-of-function variant found in [[dravet-syndrome|Dravet syndrome]] patients.³ Dual-[[aav|AAV]] serotype 9 delivery of an optimized ABE system in neonatal mice resulted in 59% DNA correction and exceptional 97% mRNA correction in bulk neocortices. The editing efficiently restored wild-type [[nav1.1|Nav1.1]] sodium current and [[parvalbumin|parvalbumin]]-expressing interneuron excitability to normal levels. Strikingly, ABE treatment improved survival 3.3-fold in a 45-day observation window (90% treated versus 27% vehicle-treated), demonstrating a remarkable biological impact. Even when administered at postnatal day 12—later than the optimal neonatal window—ABE treatment yielded a 3.0-fold survival benefit over 60 days, indicating a meaningful therapeutic window for intervention in Dravet syndrome.
