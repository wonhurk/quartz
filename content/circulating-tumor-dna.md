**Circulating tumor-DNA (ctDNA)** is a non-invasive blood-based biomarker technology that detects cancer-derived DNA fragments in patient circulation to assess [[minimal-residual-disease|minimal residual disease (MRD)]] and enable molecular risk stratification across multiple cancer types. The assay leverages tissue-free epigenomic approaches, predominantly measuring methylation of specific cancer-associated markers including [[bcat1|BCAT1]] and [[ikzf1|IKZF1]], to provide superior or complementary prognostic information beyond traditional pathologic assessments.¹ A distinguishing feature is the assay's robustness to radiation treatment—[[bcat1|BCAT1]]/[[ikzf1|IKZF1]] methylation patterns remain unaffected by radiotherapy, maintaining diagnostic reliability in patients undergoing combined chemoradiotherapy.² This stability, combined with the non-invasive nature of serial blood sampling, positions ctDNA as a transformative tool for perioperative and post-treatment monitoring across adjuvant chemotherapy, chemoradiotherapy, and maintenance immunotherapy regimens.

---

## Assay technology and mechanism

ctDNA assays measure cancer-specific epigenomic signatures—primarily methylation at [[bcat1|BCAT1]] and [[ikzf1|IKZF1]] loci—using multiplex quantitative PCR-based platforms. These methylation patterns originate from the tumor genome and circulate in cell-free DNA within the bloodstream, where they remain detectable at ultrasensitive levels capable of identifying minimal disease burden. The [[bcat1|BCAT1]]/[[ikzf1|IKZF1]] markers exhibit high tumor specificity while remaining conserved across heterogeneous tumor cell populations, enabling consistent detection of residual disease even in the setting of clonal evolution during treatment.

A key mechanistic advantage emerges from the stability of DNA methylation under therapeutic pressure. Proof-of-principle experiments demonstrated that ionizing radiation and 5-fluorouracil treatment—standard components of neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy for [[rectal-cancer|rectal cancer]]—do not alter [[bcat1|BCAT1]]/[[ikzf1|IKZF1]] methylation status in cancer cell lines or resected tumor tissues.² In contrast, tumor burden itself decreases methylated ctDNA in the bloodstream, allowing the assay to discriminate treatment response from treatment-induced epigenetic drift.

## Breast cancer applications

In [[triple-negative-breast-cancer|triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC)]] and [[her2-positive-breast-cancer|HER2-positive breast cancer]] patients receiving neoadjuvant therapy, ctDNA minimal residual disease assessment provides additional or superior risk stratification compared to pathologic complete response (pCR) alone. Although achievement of pCR strongly predicts favorable long-term outcomes, a substantial fraction of patients without pCR experience durable survival, while some pCR-negative patients develop recurrence.¹ Ultrasensitive ctDNA measurements identify a ctDNA-positive subgroup among pCR-negative patients—those with persistent circulating tumor-DNA detectable at the time of surgery—who face elevated recurrence risk and may benefit from intensified adjuvant strategies or extended monitoring.

## Colorectal cancer: adjuvant and metastatic settings

### Postoperative risk stratification in stage III colon cancer

The Alliance N0147 phase III trial prospectively validated a tissue-free, epigenomic ctDNA assay in stage III colon cancer patients receiving adjuvant FOLFOX chemotherapy.³ ctDNA detection in postoperative blood samples predicted recurrence-free survival beyond conventional clinicopathologic staging, enabling superior postoperative risk stratification. This finding established ctDNA as a molecular complement to TNM classification and histologic grade, identifying a molecular high-risk subgroup among standardly staged patients and supporting therapeutic escalation or intensification in selected populations.

### ctDNA dynamics in metastatic disease with maintenance cetuximab

The TIME-PRODIGE-28 randomized trial investigated whether circulating tumor-DNA changes during induction chemotherapy predict clinical benefit from continued maintenance cetuximab versus observation in metastatic colorectal cancer (mCRC).⁴ Patients received four months of FOLFIRI plus cetuximab induction, followed by randomization to maintenance cetuximab or observation. ctDNA kinetics—measured at baseline, after induction, and during the maintenance phase—correlated with progression-free and overall survival, and guided patient stratification for continued versus discontinued maintenance therapy. Importantly, the trajectory of ctDNA decline during induction proved more informative than pCR-equivalent endpoints in conventional imaging, establishing ctDNA dynamics as a response biomarker suited to real-time treatment adaptation.

## Biomarker stability and chemoradiotherapy integration

A critical clinical advantage of [[bcat1|BCAT1]]/[[ikzf1|IKZF1]]-based ctDNA assays is their resistance to ionizing radiation. In [[rectal-cancer|rectal cancer]] patients undergoing neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy—a standard-of-care approach for locally advanced disease—reduced methylated ctDNA levels in surgical blood samples correlated with histologic response grade, while radiation treatment itself did not artificially lower or eliminate [[bcat1|BCAT1]]/[[ikzf1|IKZF1]] methylation.² This property ensures that ctDNA assays remain reliable prognostic tools even in heavily pretreated patients and avoids false-negative results stemming from radiation-induced epigenetic changes. Consequently, ctDNA assessment integrates seamlessly into multimodal treatment protocols combining chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and surgery.

---

## Therapeutic decision support and longitudinal monitoring

ctDNA dynamics enable serial monitoring during and after treatment, supporting real-time therapeutic decisions on treatment intensity, continuation, or de-escalation. In the adjuvant setting, persistence of ctDNA after surgery despite pCR identifies patients at heightened recurrence risk warranting adjuvant intensification. In the metastatic setting, the rate and magnitude of ctDNA decline during induction guides decisions on whether to continue maintenance therapy or transition to observation, potentially sparing patients toxicity from unnecessary prolonged treatment while maintaining efficacy in responders. The non-invasive nature of blood-based ctDNA testing enables frequent sampling throughout the treatment course, providing dynamic prognostic information complementary to conventional imaging and clinical assessment.
